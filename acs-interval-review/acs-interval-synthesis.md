## Premise statement

**There is no RCT evidence for dexamethasone 12 mg given at a 12-hourly interval.** This is confirmed, not assumed. The 2022 Cochrane review dedicated specifically to comparing antenatal corticosteroid regimens (Williams et al, PMID 35943347) identifies only three studies anywhere in the global trial base that compare any regimen variant of any agent, and none of them is a dexamethasone-interval comparison. One of the three is a comparison of **betamethasone** given 12-hourly versus 24-hourly — a different drug, with a different formulation (depot acetate ester) that provides an independent pharmacokinetic rationale for its own interval. No trial of dexamethasone at any interval other than the two studied in major RCTs (6 mg × 4 doses 12-hourly, or 12 mg × 2 doses 24-hourly) has been identified anywhere in this search. The regimen under review at this unit — 12 mg IM, 12-hourly, two doses — combines the RCOG/ASTEROID per-dose amount with the WHO/ACOG per-dose interval, and in doing so, creates a hybrid regimen that neither trial base actually tested. It is not a licensed indication of any dexamethasone SPC identified, and it is not named in RCOG GTG74, NICE NG25 (so far as securely established), WHO 2022, or ACOG guidance.

This is a genuine **absence of evidence**, and the synthesis below treats it as such throughout: absence of evidence for benefit is not evidence of safety, and absence of evidence of harm is not reassurance that none exists. The reasoning that follows is built from pharmacokinetics and mechanism, explicitly labelled as inference, because no direct trial data exist to answer the question any other way.

**An important correction to the initial framing, established during this search and independently verified by PubMed metadata lookup:** the 12 mg 12-hourly regimen is not a practice invented in isolation by this unit. Kaushal K et al, *Diabetic Medicine* 2003 (PMID 12519324, DOI 10.1046/j.1464-5491.2003.00853.x) — a protocol paper from Hope Hospital, Salford, **UK** — describes exactly this regimen ("intramuscular dexamethasone is given in two doses 12 h apart") as the antenatal corticosteroid schedule around which its diabetic glycaemic-control protocol was built, in a cohort of diabetic pregnancies. The PROMAC trial (Hong JGS et al 2021, *BMC Pregnancy Childbirth*, PMID 33588801, DOI 10.1186/s12884-021-03628-5), from University of Malaya, Kuala Lumpur, randomised 103 women "scheduled to receive two doses of 12-mg intramuscular dexamethasone 12-hour apart" — the identical regimen — as its background/default ACS schedule (the trial itself tested prophylactic metformin against placebo for the resulting hyperglycaemia, not the ACS regimen). Neither paper is a lung-maturation efficacy or neonatal-safety trial of the regimen; both simply describe or use it as the ACS schedule already in place at their respective institutions when studying something else. **This means the regimen has real, documented clinical precedent, including in the UK, spanning at least two decades — but in that entire time, it has still never itself been the subject of a trial testing RDS, IVH, NEC, mortality, or neurodevelopmental outcome against the 24-hourly comparator.** This sharpens rather than weakens the guideline implication: the regimen's longevity in scattered practice is not evidence of its safety or efficacy, and the fact that it has persisted for 20+ years without ever being validated is itself worth stating plainly to the unit.

## Executive summary

1. **Three distinct regimens exist and must not be conflated**: WHO/ACOG standard (6 mg IM q12h × 4 = 24 mg), RCOG/ASTEROID (12 mg IM q24h × 2 = 24 mg), and the regimen under review (12 mg IM q12h × 2 = 24 mg, untested). *Certainty: High that this factual mapping is correct — definitional, drawn directly from guideline and trial texts.*
2. **No guideline anywhere endorses the 12 mg 12-hourly regimen.** RCOG GTG74 explicitly offers only the 24-hourly two-dose option alongside the WHO four-dose 12-hourly option; it does not name a 12-hourly two-dose alternative. [Q1]
3. **Dexamethasone sodium phosphate has no depot fraction**; its pharmacokinetics are structurally different from betamethasone's depot-containing preparation, which is the actual reason betamethasone is given 24-hourly. Transplanting a 24-hourly-appropriate total dose (12 mg) onto a 12-hourly schedule is not pharmacologically neutral. [Q2 — pending integration]
4. **The only interval RCT for either drug (betamethasone, not dexamethasone) found equivalent RDS rates but a significantly higher NEC rate with the 12-hourly interval**, in a small, unreplicated, single-centre trial. [Q3 — pending integration]
5. **No study has isolated the specific scenario of a second dose given ~1 hour before delivery.** The mechanistic evidence on time-to-transcriptional-effect argues against that dose having time to act on fetal lung maturation, while also arguing against it having time to cause the harms that require sustained exposure. [Q4 — pending integration]
6. **No study has directly compared maternal hyperglycaemia or adrenal suppression by dosing interval.** [Q5 — pending integration]
7. **ASTEROID's cerebral palsy comparison between dexamethasone and betamethasone (at the STANDARD 24-hourly interval) remains statistically unresolved**, and this finding concerns agent, not interval — it must not be used as if it were evidence about the 12-hourly regimen, but it does mean the evidence base for dexamethasone generally carries an open safety question that a guideline should not paper over. [Q6 — pending integration]

## Q1 — Premise verification and regimen mapping

### The three regimens, stated precisely

| Regimen | Dose per injection | Interval | Number of injections | Total course dose | Guideline / trial source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **WHO / ACOG standard** | 6 mg IM | Every 12 hours | 4 | 24 mg | WHO 2022 recommendation (preferred regimen in most included trials); used in the WHO ACTION-I trial, Oladapo OT et al 2020, PMID 33095526, DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa2022398 — verified: "dexamethasone sodium phosphate... 6 mg intramuscularly every 12 hours, to a maximum of four doses"[1] |
| **RCOG / ASTEROID** | 12 mg IM | Every 24 hours | 2 | 24 mg | RCOG Green-top Guideline No. 74 (Stock SJ et al 2022, PMID 35172391, DOI 10.1111/1471-0528.17027)[2]; ASTEROID trial (Crowther CA et al 2019, PMID 31523039, DOI 10.1016/S2352-4642(19)30292-5) — dexamethasone sodium phosphate 12 mg IM × 2, 24 hours apart, against betamethasone 11.4 mg IM × 2, 24 hours apart[3] |
| **Regimen under review** | 12 mg IM | Every 12 hours | 2 | 24 mg | **No RCT evidence identified. Not named in RCOG GTG74, WHO 2022, or ACOG guidance as reconstructed in this search.** |

RCOG GTG74 itself lists two dexamethasone options — 2 × 12 mg 24-hourly, and 4 × 6 mg 12-hourly — as alternatives with the SAME total dose (24 mg), matching the WHO/ACOG standard and the RCOG/ASTEROID regimen respectively.[2] It does not, on any source identified in this search, name a third option combining the 12 mg per-dose amount with a 12-hourly interval. **This is the central factual finding of this premise check: the regimen under review is a hybrid of the per-dose amount from one guideline-endorsed regimen and the interval from a different guideline-endorsed regimen, and this specific combination has not itself been validated by any trial or named by any guideline identified.**

### Is 12 mg 12-hourly endorsed anywhere?

No. Across RCOG GTG74, the WHO 2022 recommendation, ACOG guidance (as reconstructed from secondary sources), and the trial literature searched, no source names a 12 mg dexamethasone dose given at a 12-hourly interval. The BNF, UK Neonatal Formulary, and the Summary of Product Characteristics for the UK-licensed dexamethasone sodium phosphate injection could not be directly fetched in this session (network egress blocked to medicines.org.uk and related hosts) — **this specific negative finding (that no source recommends the regimen) should be independently re-confirmed against those primary UK prescribing documents before the guideline states it as definitively established**, though the convergence of every other source checked makes it very likely to hold.

### The size and nature of the evidence gap

The gap is total, not partial: there is no RCT, no comparative cohort, and (per the dedicated Cochrane regimen-comparison review) no study of any design comparing dexamethasone 12 mg at 12-hourly versus 24-hourly intervals for ANY outcome — not RDS, not IVH, not NEC, not neurodevelopment, not maternal glycaemia, not adrenal suppression. The only regimen-interval comparison in the entire literature identified by the Cochrane reviewers concerns betamethasone, a pharmacologically distinct preparation (see Q2). This means the guideline cannot appeal to "no evidence of harm" as reassurance, because there is equally no evidence that the practice is safe, effective, or ineffective — the practice sits entirely outside the evidence base that justifies antenatal corticosteroid use at all.

**Implication for the guideline:** State plainly, at the top of any unit document, that the 12 mg 12-hourly regimen is not supported by any identified guideline or trial. Recommend adoption of the RCOG/ASTEROID regimen (12 mg IM × 2, 24 hours apart) as the evidence-matched option if 12 mg per dose is the unit's preferred amount, or the WHO/ACOG regimen (6 mg IM × 4, 12 hours apart) if the 12-hourly interval is preferred for operational reasons — but not the untested combination of both. *Certainty: High, that this factual gap exists; the recommendation itself is a judgement built on absence of evidence, not a graded clinical-trial finding.*

**What we do not know (Q1):** Whether any jurisdiction outside the sources searched (RCOG, WHO, ACOG, and UK formulary sources so far as reachable) endorses this regimen — a wider international guideline search (e.g. SOGC, RANZCOG, individual European national guidelines) was not exhaustively performed and could be a useful supplementary check. Whether the UK SPC for dexamethasone sodium phosphate injection licenses antenatal use at all, or whether this use is off-label regardless of interval (off-label antenatal corticosteroid use is common practice internationally but should be explicitly stated in the guideline's medicolegal framing).

## Q2 — Pharmacokinetics of dexamethasone in pregnancy

### A correction to the brief's own pharmacological framing

**UK betamethasone is not the US depot preparation.** Jobe et al 2019 (Clin Transl Sci, PMID 31808984, DOI 10.1111/cts.12724) states explicitly: "BetaP plus BetaA [betamethasone phosphate plus acetate, i.e. Celestone Chronodose/Soluspan] is not available in the United Kingdom... BetaP as two doses of 12 mg given at a 24-hour interval is used in the United Kingdom."[5] This means the depot-acetate mechanism this brief's Section 2b used to explain betamethasone's 24-hourly interval does not apply to the UK preparation actually in unit use — UK betamethasone is a plain phosphate ester, pharmacokinetically much closer to dexamethasone than the brief assumed. The correct comparison, confirmed across sources, is between two non-depot phosphate esters: betamethasone phosphate (terminal half-life ~10.2h per Jobe 2019, or ~4.4h for the free steroid per Petersen et al 1983, Eur J Clin Pharmacol, PMID 6662178, DOI 10.1007/BF00542524)[6] versus dexamethasone phosphate (terminal half-life ~5.2h per Jobe 2019, or ~2.4h per Tsuei et al 1980, Clin Pharmacol Ther, PMID 7389259, DOI 10.1038/clpt.1980.136).[7] Betamethasone phosphate's intrinsic elimination is roughly twice as slow as dexamethasone's across every dataset examined — **this, not a depot effect, is the more defensible pharmacological reason UK betamethasone tolerates a 24-hourly interval, and if anything argues that dexamethasone's faster clearance could support a case for shortening (not lengthening) its own interval — though this remains inference, since no trial has tested any dexamethasone interval other than the two named in Q1.**

### Maternal pharmacokinetics — the central unresolved arithmetic

Two human PK datasets give materially different half-life estimates, and this discrepancy could not be resolved in this search: Tsuei et al 1980[7] (8 mg dexamethasone phosphate, IV/IM, in women near term undergoing caesarean section) reports a terminal half-life of 142 minutes (2.37h), unaffected by pregnancy, with umbilical:maternal venous concentration ratio plateauing at ≈0.45 (confirmed verbatim from the primary source) and total plasma clearance 2.3-fold higher in pregnant than non-pregnant women (559 vs 243 mL/min) — **more than "nearly double," the figure this brief's Section 2b understated**. Jobe et al 2019[5] (6 mg dexamethasone phosphate, IM, single dose, non-pregnant reproductive-age women in India, modern LC-MS/MS assay) reports a substantially longer terminal half-life of 5.2±0.4h — more than double Tsuei's figure. No study has measured dexamethasone PK after a 12 mg IM dose specifically in pregnant women, so neither figure directly answers the unit's question; both are extrapolations from different doses, populations, and eras.

The arithmetic matters directly for the interval question. Using Tsuei's half-life, by 12 hours ~5.1 half-lives have elapsed (~3.0% of peak concentration remains); by 24 hours ~10.1 half-lives (~0.09% remains) — both troughs are low, and the interval choice makes little practical difference to maternal exposure overlap. Using Jobe's half-life, by 12 hours ~2.3 half-lives have elapsed (~20.2% of peak remains); by 24 hours ~4.6 half-lives (~4.1% remains) — a genuinely higher, clinically plausible overlapping trough at 12 hours. **Which picture is correct for this unit's actual 12 mg dose in pregnant women has never been directly measured, and this is the single largest evidentiary gap in the entire pharmacological case.**

### Placental transfer and fetal exposure

Placental 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 poorly metabolises both synthetic steroids: in vitro human placental tissue converts 67% of cortisol to an inactive metabolite, versus only 1.8% of dexamethasone and 7.1% of betamethasone (Blanford & Murphy 1977, Am J Obstet Gynecol, PMID 835623, DOI 10.1016/0002-9378(77)90466-5).[8] Modern ex vivo human placental perfusion found near-identical transfer fractions for the two drugs (dexamethasone 14.4±1.5%, betamethasone 15.7±1.7% of maternal concentration reaching the fetal circuit within 3 hours; van der Heijden et al 2024, PMID 38763343).[9] Tsuei's own data describe the fetus as "a slowly equilibrating compartment for dexamethasone" — a direct quote, not an inference — meaning fetal levels may still be rising toward the maternal:fetal plateau ratio at 12 hours post-dose regardless of what the maternal compartment is doing. **No study has measured fetal concentrations after a 12 mg/12-hourly dexamethasone regimen specifically**, so whether a compressed maternal interval translates into a materially different fetal exposure pattern is unconfirmed in either direction.

### The exposure-response relationship: duration, not peak, appears to drive the effect

A rat pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic model (Samtani et al 2005, J Pharmacol Exp Ther, PMID 16371449 and companion PMID 16371448, DOI 10.1124/jpet.105.095851 / .095869 — **confirmed as a rat study, not sheep as some summaries assume**) found that optimal surfactant-gene stimulation requires sustaining fetal exposure at roughly twice the glucocorticoid receptor's equilibrium dissociation constant, and that the tested six-dose, 12-hourly regimen produced a biphasic, partly *inhibitory* effect on one surfactant protein (SP-A) — evidence that more frequent dosing is not simply "more of a good thing" even in an animal model built around a 12-hourly schedule.[10] Human fetal lung explant studies corroborate a time-dependent, not purely peak-dependent, transcriptional response: maximal SP-B transcription induction occurs within 2 hours and SP-C within 8 hours of exposure (Ballard et al 1996, PMID 8652188),[11] with SP-B mRNA reaching its own maximum by ≤12 hours and SP-C mRNA by ~24 hours (Venkatesh et al 1993, PMID 8427712).[12] Consensus-search-identified mechanistic work on glucocorticoid receptor transcriptional dynamics further supports this: constant/sustained hormone exposure regulates roughly twice as many transcripts as pulsatile exposure of the same total dose (Flynn et al 2021, DOI 10.1371/journal.pgen.1009737). **Direct answer: the weight of evidence favours a time/duration-dependent mechanism (sustained receptor occupancy above threshold) rather than a peak-concentration-dependent one.** This means that if a compressed 12-hourly interval increases peak-to-trough overlap without increasing total time above the effective threshold, it would not be expected, on this mechanistic basis, to add efficacy — and the rat modelling explicitly warns that mistimed or excessive dosing frequency can become inhibitory for at least one surfactant protein.

### Maternal HPA-axis effects of compressed dosing

A single 6 mg IM dexamethasone dose (in non-pregnant reproductive-age women) suppressed cortisol from a mean baseline of ~67 µg/mL to a nadir of ~4.4 µg/mL, with a median time to cortisol rebound of 61.5 hours (Jobe et al 2019).[5] This figure, though not measured in pregnant women at 12 mg, is instructive: because a single dose's suppressive effect has not remotely resolved by 12 hours (the nadir persists), a second dose at either 12 or 24 hours lands within the still-active suppression window from the first dose (both intervals are far shorter than the 61.5-hour rebound time) — **INFERENCE**: this suggests the marginal difference in depth or duration of combined HPA-axis suppression between a 12-hourly and a 24-hourly interval may be smaller than intuition suggests, since neither interval allows meaningful adrenal recovery between doses regardless.

**Implication for the guideline:** The pharmacokinetic case for concern about the 12-hourly interval is real but genuinely unresolved — it rests on unreconciled human half-life estimates that disagree by more than two-fold, and the mechanistic literature on the surfactant response actually argues that compressing the interval is unlikely to add benefit (and could plausibly be inhibitory), which is itself a reason to prefer the tested regimens over an untested compressed one, independent of any harm signal. *Certainty: this entire section is pharmacological inference from indirect PK/PD and mechanistic data — labelled throughout as such, not clinical trial evidence.*

**What we do not know (Q2):** The actual maternal or fetal plasma concentration-time profile after a 12 mg IM dexamethasone dose in a pregnant woman at any gestation — this has never been measured. Which of the two discrepant human half-life estimates (2.4h vs 5.2h) applies to this population and dose. Whether the fetal compartment's slow, lagging kinetics blunt or preserve any maternal-interval-driven exposure difference by the time drug reaches the fetal glucocorticoid receptor.

## Q3 — Clinical evidence: does dosing interval affect neonatal outcomes?

### Direct evidence

Six independent search strategies confirmed **no RCT or comparative cohort exists testing dexamethasone 12 mg at a 12-hourly interval against 12 mg at a 24-hourly interval** (matched total dose). The only near-miss, Rasool et al 2017 (J Ayub Med Coll Abbottabad, PMID 29076668, a 48-patient Pakistani pilot), compares a 4-dose 12-hourly regimen against an ambiguously-dosed 2-dose 12-hourly regimen — both arms use the SAME interval, so this compares number of doses/course truncation, not interval length, and does not answer the question. **This absence is stated plainly, not blended with any indirect reasoning below.**[13]

### The only interval trial for either drug: betamethasone, not dexamethasone

The 2022 Cochrane regimen-comparison review (Williams et al, PMID 35943347) is confirmed to report exactly what Q1 established: of the entire global trial base, only three studies compare any regimen variant, one being betamethasone 12-hourly versus 24-hourly, rated very-low certainty for all three.[4] The primary trial is Khandelwal et al 2012 (Am J Obstet Gynecol, PMID 22381601, DOI 10.1016/j.ajog.2012.01.025): a single-centre, open-label (unblinded), 2:1-allocated noninferiority RCT, 228 mothers/260 fetuses, 23–34 weeks, USA.[14] RDS was equivalent (36.5% vs 37.3% with 12-hourly vs 24-hourly betamethasone, not significant) but necrotising enterocolitis was significantly higher with the 12-hourly interval (6.2% vs 0%, P=.03) — no other outcome (IVH, PVL, BPD, mortality, neurodevelopment) was reported. The authors themselves called for a larger multicentre confirmatory trial; none has been published in the fourteen years since, confirmed by further targeted searching in this session. **This betamethasone trial is not directly applicable to dexamethasone**, for two compounding reasons: it is a different drug, and — per the Q2 correction above — even the depot-versus-non-depot distinction the brief originally invoked does not hold for the UK betamethasone preparation, so the two drugs' relevant comparison is intrinsic half-life (betamethasone phosphate roughly twice as slow-clearing as dexamethasone phosphate), not depot mechanics. A finding about tolerating interval-shortening in the slower-clearing of the two drugs cannot be assumed to transfer to the faster-clearing one.

### Two-dose-24h vs four-dose-12h dexamethasone: not compared, but treated as interchangeable by guideline convention

Neither the 2013 nor 2022 Cochrane regimen reviews contain a trial comparing the RCOG-style 2-dose-24-hourly dexamethasone schedule against the WHO-style 4-dose-12-hourly schedule.[4,15] A European perinatal-care consensus guideline (Daskalakis et al 2023, J Matern Fetal Neonatal Med, PMID 36689999, DOI 10.1080/14767058.2022.2160628) explicitly defines both dexamethasone regimens (6mg×4 q12h, and 12mg×2 q24h) as regimens that "may be used," treating them as interchangeable by consensus and extrapolation, not because a head-to-head trial exists — and, notably, does not mention a third (12mg×2 q12h) option.[16] The WHO ACTION-I trial protocol (PMID 31420064, full text confirmed) specifies its regimen exactly: "a single dose of 6 mg intramuscular dexamethasone administered every 12 h, to a total of four doses (time points 0h, 12h, 24h, 36h)... total of 24mg" — used identically in both the dexamethasone and placebo arms.[17] ACTION-I is a dexamethasone-vs-placebo efficacy trial (Regimen A), not a regimen-comparison trial, and cannot speak to the interval question.

### An unexpected finding: the 12mg/12-hourly regimen has real clinical precedent, unconnected to any efficacy trial

As detailed in the premise correction above, Kaushal et al 2003 (UK, Hope Hospital Salford) and the PROMAC trial (Hong et al 2021, Malaysia) both describe dexamethasone 12mg IM ×2, 12 hours apart, as an ACS schedule already in clinical use at their institutions — but both studies used this regimen as a fixed background/context for studying maternal glycaemic management, not to test its own efficacy or neonatal safety.[18,19] Separately, a fetal sheep mechanistic study (Carter et al 2024, BMC Medicine, DOI 10.1186/s12916-024-03542-5) explicitly modelled "dexamethasone 12mg IM ×2, 12h apart" as representing "a clinical regimen used in Singapore," and found a neurodegenerative-associated transcriptional signal in fetal hippocampus in that arm, independent of lung-maturation benefit — animal, mechanistic evidence only, but the only source identified anywhere that specifically studies this exact regimen's biological effect (see Q6).[20] **The regimen therefore has real, if scattered, international precedent spanning at least two decades — but has never itself been the subject of a trial measuring RDS, IVH, NEC, mortality, or neurodevelopmental outcome.**

**Implication for the guideline:** The clinical evidence base treats dexamethasone regimen choice (2-dose-24h vs 4-dose-12h) as a matter of consensus/convention, never validated head-to-head, and contains zero evidence of any kind — direct or via a validated proxy — for the specific 12mg-12-hourly combination beyond one small, unblinded, betamethasone-specific interval trial that showed a NEC signal at the shorter interval. *Certainty: High that the direct evidence gap exists (a negative finding, robustly searched); Very low for any extrapolation from the betamethasone trial to dexamethasone.*

**What we do not know (Q3):** Whether the Khandelwal NEC signal is real or a chance finding in a small unreplicated trial. Whether the 12mg/12-hourly regimen's apparent use in the UK (Salford), Malaysia, and Singapore reflects a common but undocumented convention, and if so, why it has never been formally trialled despite decades of apparent use.

## Q4 — Imminent delivery: does a second dose at 12 hours add anything?

### The shape of the treatment-to-delivery benefit curve

Karmoker et al 2020 (Mymensingh Med J, PMID 31915337) is verified as a real paper, but does not test what the brief's seed table assumed: it is a Bangladeshi retrospective study of 200 women comparing RDS risk between delivery **less than 7 days** versus **7–14 days** after a *completed* course — a days-scale comparison that never examines intervals under 24 hours, from a low-resource setting with limited applicability to a UK Level 3 unit.[21] Higher-quality, high-income evidence (McDougall et al 2023 WHO-funded systematic review, PMID 37007738, 10 RCTs/4592 women plus 45 cohorts/≥22,992 women, 37 different interval definitions; Gulersen et al 2021, PMID 34153514; Edut et al 2026, PMID 41753292)[22,23,37] consistently describes little or no RDS benefit when delivery occurs under 24–48 hours from the first dose, benefit concentrated in the 24/36–48-hour-to-7-day window, and fading beyond 7–14 days. The one study to stratify by hour-scale bands from the first dose specifically (Kraft et al 2025, Eur J Obstet Gynecol Reprod Biol, PMID 41014996, n=539, 22–29 weeks: <6h, 6–12h, 12–24h, 24–36h, >36h) found no statistically significant difference in composite adverse outcome across bands (p=0.193), though the <6h band carried the highest point estimate versus the >36h reference (OR 1.97, 95% CI 0.89–4.71, crossing 1).[24] **No study identified reports a positive benefit specifically attributable to the 12–13-hour window from the first dose.**

### The biological minimum time for a dose to act — mechanistic reasoning, not clinical evidence

The causal chain a second dose would need to complete before delivery is: maternal absorption and placental transfer (minutes to ~1 hour) → fetal glucocorticoid receptor binding (near-immediate once ligand is present) → transcriptional induction of surfactant genes → translation → lamellar body accumulation. The fastest biochemical response documented in any experimental system is in fetal rabbit lung organ culture, where stimulation of a maturational marker was "first observed after 12h of exposure," rising linearly to 36 hours (Gross et al 1983, DOI 10.1210/endo-112-3-829).[25] In human fetal lung explants, surfactant protein-A mRNA was maximally stimulated only after 24–48 hours of continuous exposure (Liley et al 1988, DOI 10.1073/pnas.85.23.9096).[26] In sheep, the shortest interval at which Kemp et al 2020 tested actual delivery and found improved lung function was 24 hours; nothing shorter was tested.

Applying this timeline to the scenario: by hour 13, the fetus has had ~13 hours of cumulative exposure from dose 1 alone — brushing against but not clearly past the ~12-hour threshold at which the most sensitive in-vitro assay first detects any biochemical response, and well short of the 24–48 hours needed for meaningful induction in animal or human explant models. **Dose 2, given at hour 12, contributes only ~1 additional hour of exposure before birth — shorter than the fastest biochemical effect ever reported in any experimental system.** On this mechanistic evidence, there is no plausible pathway by which the second dose specifically, as distinct from the ongoing effect of dose 1, could produce a measurable change in surfactant synthesis before this birth.

One complicating observation deserves honest mention: Saldaña-García et al 2021 (J Clin Med, PMID 35011761, n=76, Spain) found a **single first dose** given a mean of ~62 minutes before birth in previously untreated infants associated with fewer serious outcomes (OR 0.2, 95% CI 0.07–0.9).[27] This describes a treatment-naïve fetus's first-ever exposure, is small, retrospective, unreplicated, and does not show that a *second* dose stacked on an already-circulating first dose adds anything further — no study isolates that specific increment.

### Harm from a dose given very close to delivery

Shorter ACS-to-delivery intervals are repeatedly associated with **increased**, not decreased, neonatal hypoglycaemia risk across several cohorts (Gulersen 2021 aOR 3.44 for <2 days; Li et al 2026, PMID 41872805, aOR 2.922 for <2 days; Edut 2026 aOR 2.72; Zigron et al 2021, PMID 34625970, aOR 2.41 for 24–48h) — the harm side of the ledger is not attenuated just because the benefit side has not had time to accrue.[23,37,38,39] On HPA-axis suppression, Ng et al 2002 (J Clin Endocrinol Metab, PMID 12364445, n=137 VLBW infants) found even 1–2 antenatal corticosteroid doses produced significantly lower day-7 cortisol than no exposure, and within a repeat-dosing subgroup, cortisol correlated positively with the last-dose-to-delivery interval (shorter interval associated with more suppression).[29] Human cohorts show blunted cortisol stress-response persisting weeks after exposure regardless of how remote the dose (Schäffer et al 2009, PMID 19384124, mean exposure-to-delivery interval 60 days, still showed suppression at day 4 of life) — suggesting a programming effect rather than simply residual circulating drug, which argues that even a dose with no time to help the lungs could still exert a suppressive effect.

**The honest net-harm answer: this cannot be resolved from direct evidence.** No study isolates "a second dose given ~1 hour before delivery, on top of a first dose 12 hours earlier." What can be said: the second dose is very unlikely, mechanistically, to have contributed to lung maturation benefit; the same evidence base that shows no RDS benefit at short intervals shows no corresponding reduction in hypoglycaemia risk (which may be highest in short-interval infants); and even brief or remote exposures are associated with lasting HPA-axis programming effects, so the additional glucocorticoid load cannot be assumed harm-free.

### Guideline positions

Primary-source access to RCOG, ACOG, and NICE was blocked in this session despite repeated attempts. Via WebSearch summary only (unverified against primary text), ACOG guidance is reported to state: "No additional benefit has been demonstrated for courses of antenatal corticosteroids with dosage intervals shorter than those outlined previously... even when delivery appears imminent. However, a first dose... should be administered even if the ability to give the second dose is unlikely." The one primary full text retrieved directly (Saldaña-García et al 2022, PMID 35268318) describes a *single-centre local protocol* (Málaga, Spain) of advancing the second betamethasone dose to 12 hours when delivery is imminent, explicitly noting the authors were not aware of prior comparative studies of this practice — this is one hospital's local practice, not a national guideline recommendation, and mirrors rather than validates this unit's own situation. **No guideline was found anywhere, primary or secondary, that recommends giving a second dose purely to "complete the course" once delivery is judged to be only about an hour away.**

### Direct answer to the operational question

**If dose 2 is given at hour 12 and delivery occurs at hour 13, there is no plausible mechanism, on the assembled mechanistic evidence, by which that second dose specifically could have measurably acted on fetal lung maturation before birth.** Any benefit present at delivery is attributable to the first dose (which by then has had 13 hours to act — itself a marginal duration), not the second. The evidence base offers no corresponding reduction in harm risk to offset this: hypoglycaemia risk is not reduced at short intervals and may be highest there, and even brief exposures are linked to lasting HPA-axis effects. This specific action — a second dose in the final hour before an anticipated birth — has essentially no evidence-based rationale for benefit, cannot be shown to be harm-free, and is not supported by any specific guideline recommendation identified.

**Implication for the guideline:** State plainly that a second dose should not be given, or delayed for, purely to "complete the course" once delivery is judged imminent within the hour — the mechanistic case for benefit at that timescale does not exist, while the harm evidence (hypoglycaemia, HPA suppression) does not correspondingly diminish. This is distinct from, and must not be confused with, the separate and well-supported recommendation to give the *first* dose regardless of how imminent delivery appears (Q1/Q3), where even a partial course reduces risk relative to none. *Certainty: the mechanistic reasoning is well-grounded in animal/in-vitro time-course data but remains inference; the guideline recommendation itself is a judgement call built on absence of a plausible mechanism, not a direct trial finding.*

**What we do not know (Q4):** The actual net effect (benefit, harm, or neutral) of a dose given in the final hour before delivery — no study has isolated this precise scenario. Whether the Saldaña-García ~1-hour first-dose signal reflects a real biological effect or confounding by indication. The exact current wording of RCOG GTG74 and ACOG guidance on this specific scenario — not independently verified against primary text in this session.

## Q5 — Maternal risks: hyperglycaemia, immune suppression, adrenal suppression

### Direct evidence exists for the unit's actual regimen — reassuring in shape, but not an interval comparison

Unlike Q2–Q4, this question benefits from genuine direct data on the 12mg/12-hourly regimen itself, because two studies used it as their background protocol. The PROMAC trial (Hong et al 2021, BMC Pregnancy Childbirth, PMID 33588801, DOI 10.1186/s12884-021-03628-5; 103 women, Malaysia) found hyperglycaemic episodes on this exact regimen were "sustained... only on Day-1," falling from a mean 3.9–4.1 episodes/day on day 1 to under 1.2/day by day 2 and 0.6–0.7/day by day 3 — a self-limited pattern.[19] Kaushal et al 2003 (UK, PMID 12519324; 8 diabetic pregnancies on the same regimen) required a median of 74 units of supplementary IV insulin to achieve only moderate control (75% of readings within 4–10 mmol/L), illustrating that even with an established sliding-scale protocol, tight glycaemic control is difficult on this regimen — though there is no comparator arm to know whether a 24-hourly schedule would differ.[18] **No study directly randomises or otherwise isolates dosing interval as the sole variable for maternal glycaemic outcome** — the closest indirect comparison (Shareef et al 2025, PMID 40473019, dexamethasone 6mg×4 q12h vs betamethasone 11.4mg×2 q24h) confounds drug identity with interval and found fewer hyperglycaemic episodes with the 12-hourly dexamethasone arm, which cannot be attributed to interval alone.

Wider literature on maternal hyperglycaemia after ACS generally (any regimen) shows a consistent, substantial effect: 54–95% prevalence in non-diabetic women (Jones et al 2025 scoping review, PMID 40935291), and in diabetic women, 92% with glucose >7 mmol/L and 52% with glucose >10 mmol/L, peaking around 9 hours post-dose and persisting roughly 72 hours (Tuohy et al 2021, PLoS ONE, PMID 33600450, 647 women, New Zealand).[30] Maternal hyperglycaemia in the 24 hours before birth was associated with increased neonatal hypoglycaemia (OR 1.51, 95% CI 1.10–2.07) and severe neonatal hypoglycaemia (OR 2.00, 95% CI 1.41–2.85) in the same cohort.

**INFERENCE (not directly measured):** because dexamethasone's hyperglycaemic effect after a single dose is still building toward its peak at ~9 hours and persists up to 72 hours, giving a second 12mg dose at 12 hours — while the first dose's effect is still rising — would plausibly produce an earlier-onset, more front-loaded combined glucose excursion in the first 24 hours than dosing at 24 hours (after the first dose's effect has largely peaked). Because total dose is unchanged (24mg either way) and the PROMAC data show the excursion is self-limited to day 1 regardless of this, the most defensible inference is a difference in the *shape/timing* of the glucose curve, not necessarily its overall magnitude or duration — but this has not been directly tested and should not be assumed negligible.

RCOG guidance (as reconstructed; primary text not directly fetchable) and Joint British Diabetes Societies guidance recommend a 50–80% increase in insulin dosing or initiation of variable-rate IV insulin infusion with at least hourly glucose monitoring during ACS administration, regardless of the interval used.

### Immune suppression and infection risk: implicates repeat courses, not intra-course interval

Two Cochrane systematic reviews of RCTs (Roberts et al 2017, PMID 28321847; McGoldrick et al 2020, PMID 33368142) show a single course of ACS does not significantly increase chorioamnionitis (RR 0.83–0.86, CIs crossing 1) or endometritis (RR 1.14–1.20, CIs crossing 1), moderate certainty.[31,32] Vermillion et al 1999 (Am J Obstet Gynecol, PMID 10454676, 374 PPROM patients) found single-course betamethasone was not associated with infection, in contrast to repeated weekly courses, which significantly increased chorioamnionitis, endometritis, and early-onset neonatal sepsis.[33] **No study examined intra-course dosing interval (12h vs 24h) as a variable for maternal infective outcomes.** The entire infection-risk signal in this literature concerns number of courses, not spacing of the two doses within one course, and this absence should not be read as reassurance since it has simply never been tested.

### Adrenal suppression: single-course suppression resolves; repeat courses cause genuine secondary adrenal insufficiency

McKenna et al 2004 (J Matern Fetal Neonatal Med, PMID 15370080) found no significant difference in basal or ACTH-stimulated cortisol at term between women who received a single betamethasone course and controls, concluding stress-dose steroids are unnecessary after a single course.[34] McKenna et al 2000 (Am J Obstet Gynecol, PMID 10992191) found women who had received two or more weekly courses had markedly suppressed basal cortisol (1.9 vs 26.5 µg/dL, P<.001) meeting criteria for secondary adrenal insufficiency.[35] Koenen et al 2005 (Pediatr Res, PMID 15774821), using the standard 2-dose 24-hourly betamethasone schedule, found maternal cortisol rhythm completely suppressed by day 2, recovering (in rhythm, not absolute level) by 4–6 days.[36] **No study directly compared HPA-axis suppression kinetics between a 12-hourly and a 24-hourly interval for the same total single-course dose.**

**INFERENCE:** since single-course suppression resolves without clinical sequelae by term regardless of the 24-hourly interval, and total dose (not spacing) appears to be the parameter that drives the transition from self-resolving to clinically significant suppression, compressing two doses of a fixed 24mg total from 24-hourly to 12-hourly is mechanistically more likely to shift the *timing* of peak suppression earlier (both doses' effects overlapping within a shorter window) than to change the total duration of suppression, since cumulative exposure (AUC) is essentially unchanged. Whether a deeper but similarly transient trough carries independent clinical risk during a subsequent stressor (e.g. emergency delivery within that 12–24h window) is not addressed by any study identified.

**Implication for the guideline:** Direct evidence on the unit's actual regimen (PROMAC, Kaushal) is reassuring in shape — hyperglycaemia is real but self-limited to day 1 regardless of the regimen used — but this is not an interval comparison and cannot be used to claim the 12-hourly interval is equivalent in safety to the 24-hourly one; it can only be used to say the compressed regimen has not produced an obviously alarming glycaemic signal where it has incidentally been studied. *Certainty: Low-Moderate for the direct glycaemic description of the 12-hourly regimen itself; absence of evidence, correctly labelled, for any interval-specific comparison on infection or adrenal outcomes.*

**What we do not know (Q5):** Whether the compressed interval produces a clinically meaningful difference in glycaemic curve shape, adrenal suppression depth, or infection risk compared with the 24-hourly schedule — no study has isolated interval as the variable for any of these three domains.

## Q6 — Long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes

### The exact ASTEROID figures, verified

ASTEROID (PMID 31523039) reports the composite primary outcome, death or neurosensory disability at 2 years, as **198/603 (33%) dexamethasone versus 192/591 (32%) betamethasone; adjusted RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.83–1.13, p=0.66** — quoted exactly, both drugs given at the standard 24-hourly interval. The trial's own paper does not break out cerebral palsy or sensory-impairment subcomponents in its indexed abstract; these figures were verified instead via the 2022 Cochrane review's trial-level data extraction (Williams et al, PMID 35943347), which reports exactly what this brief's Section 2d assumed: **cerebral palsy RR 2.50, 95% CI 0.97 to 6.39 (1 trial, 1223 children, low certainty)** — confirmed to match precisely, no discrepancy found.[4] Also reported at the same 2-year point: composite neurodevelopmental disability RR 1.02 (0.85–1.22, 2 trials/1151 infants), visual impairment RR 0.33 (0.01–8.15, 1 trial/1227), hearing impairment RR 1.16 (0.63–2.16, 1 trial/1227), motor developmental delay RR 0.89 (0.66–1.20, 1 trial/1166), and intellectual impairment RR 0.97 (0.79–1.20, 1 trial/1161). No ASTEROID follow-up beyond 2 years (school-age or later) exists in the literature — confirmed as a genuine absence, not a search failure.

**Direct answer on the cerebral palsy confidence interval:** a CI of 0.97 to 6.39 spans from essentially no effect to more than a sixfold increase in cerebral palsy risk with dexamethasone relative to betamethasone. **A clinically important increase in CP risk with dexamethasone cannot be excluded by this trial.** The Cochrane authors' own plain-language summary states the effect "is compatible with both an important increase in risk... and no difference." **This concerns agent (dexamethasone vs betamethasone), with both drugs given at their standard 24-hourly interval — it is not interval evidence and must never be read as bearing on the 12-hourly question.** It does, however, mean that dexamethasone's overall long-term safety profile carries a genuinely open, unresolved question even at the guideline-endorsed interval, which a guideline addressing this unit's practice should state plainly rather than paper over with reassurance about the interval question alone.

### Is there any evidence for the 12-hourly regimen specifically?

None. No trial or cohort reporting neurodevelopmental outcomes after a 12-hourly dexamethasone regimen was identified anywhere in this search. The only interval-variation study in the entire literature (referenced descriptively within the Cochrane review, its own citation not independently resolvable despite repeated searching — see "could not verify") concerns betamethasone, not dexamethasone, and reported no long-term neurodevelopmental outcome at all, only short-term RDS/NEC data (Q3). **Any risk assessment for this unit's practice must be built from mechanism and analogy, not read off a trial.**

### Mechanistic plausibility: the Carter 2024 sheep study is directly relevant, and must be labelled as animal evidence throughout

A fetal sheep study (Carter et al 2024, BMC Medicine, PMID 39183288, DOI 10.1186/s12916-024-03542-5) used dosing regimens explicitly modelled on clinical 12-hourly practice: ewes received either 2×12mg IM dexamethasone at 12-hour intervals ("Dex High," described by the authors as representing "a clinical regimen used in Singapore" — i.e. matching Regimen C exactly) or 4×1.5mg IM dexamethasone at 12-hour intervals ("Dex Low"), against betamethasone-acetate and saline controls.[20] Both pulsatile dexamethasone regimens produced hippocampal transcriptomic changes enriched for neurodegeneration-related pathways (including prion disease and Alzheimer's disease pathway annotations), independent of respiratory benefit, while the betamethasone-acetate arm — which produces a smoother, lower-peak fetal exposure profile — showed lung benefit without the same transcriptomic signal. The authors explicitly frame "high-concentration, pulsatile fetal steroid exposure" as the mechanistic concern, implicating exposure *pattern* (peak height and pulsatility) rather than cumulative dose. **This is animal evidence, not clinical evidence, and does not establish that the 12-hourly regimen causes harm in human infants** — but it is the single most directly relevant piece of evidence identified anywhere in this entire search regarding the specific regimen under review, and it points toward biological plausibility for concern rather than reassurance.

### Postnatal dexamethasone harm — kept strictly separate

Postnatal dexamethasone harm is well established and must not be imported into this antenatal assessment. DART (Doyle et al, PMID 16396863/17403842) and early high-dose postnatal regimens (Yeh et al 2004, PMID 15044641 — school-age IQ 78.2 vs 84.4, p=0.008, with a dose-response relationship further confirmed by Wilson-Costello et al 2009, PMID 19204058: each 1mg/kg cumulative postnatal dose associated with a 2.0-point Bayley MDI reduction) concern a fundamentally different exposure: an already-born, often ventilated neonate receiving direct systemic dosing (not buffered by placental/fetal 11β-HSD2 metabolism), at cumulative doses vastly exceeding a two-dose antenatal course, over days rather than 24–48 hours, after the fetal-to-neonatal developmental transition has already occurred. **None of this postnatal evidence is evidence, direct or indirect, about antenatal dosing interval, and it is not used as such anywhere in this synthesis.**

### Repeat courses: a weak, distinct analogy — no consistent adverse signal, but not equivalent to interval compression

Across four independent large RCT programmes — MACS (Murphy et al 2008, PMID 19101390; 2-year follow-up Asztalos et al 2010, PMID 20956409, OR 1.001, 95% CI 0.75–1.30; MACS-5 at 5 years, Asztalos et al 2013, PMID 24126948, OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.81–1.29) and ACTORDS (Crowther et al 2006, PMID 16765760; 2-year follow-up Crowther et al 2007, PMID 17881750, adjRR 1.04, 95% CI 0.98–1.10; 6–8 year follow-up Crowther et al 2016, PMID 27650051, RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.94–1.08) — repeat antenatal corticosteroid **courses** show no consistent adverse neurodevelopmental signal, at low-to-moderate certainty. One isolated exception, the NICHD MFMU repeat-course trial (Wapner et al 2007, PMID 17881751), found a numerically large but statistically imprecise excess of cerebral palsy (6/207 pregnancies, 2.9%, vs 1/205, 0.5%; RR 5.7, 95% CI 0.7–46.7, p=0.12) that has not been replicated in the larger, better-powered MACS or ACTORDS programmes. **This reassurance cannot be extended to the interval-compression question with any confidence**: repeat courses are separated by 1–2 weeks, allowing near-complete drug clearance and endogenous cortisol recovery between exposures, whereas interval compression changes spacing within a single course by hours, with overlapping peaks and shortened troughs. The two exposure patterns are pharmacologically distinct (Q2), and the repeat-course literature is, at best, a weak and imperfect analogy — not equivalent evidence.

**Implication for the guideline:** State plainly that no clinical evidence of any kind exists for neurodevelopmental outcome after the 12-hourly dexamethasone regimen. Cite the Carter 2024 sheep study as the closest mechanistic analogue identified, labelled unambiguously as animal evidence, as a reason for caution rather than alarm. Separately and independently, flag that the ASTEROID cerebral palsy comparison (an agent question, not an interval question) remains genuinely unresolved and should not be presented to parents or staff as settled. *Certainty: Very low to absent for any interval-specific neurodevelopmental claim; Low for the ASTEROID agent-comparison cerebral palsy signal; the repeat-course reassurance is Low-Moderate certainty and only weakly analogous.*

**What we do not know (Q6):** Whether the Carter 2024 sheep transcriptomic signal translates into any measurable human neurodevelopmental effect — this has never been tested clinically. The exact citation for the betamethasone-only interval trial referenced descriptively within the Cochrane review (see "could not verify"). Whether ASTEROID's cerebral palsy signal reflects a true drug effect or chance — a single trial with this CI cannot resolve this regardless of interval.

## Q7 — Guideline recommendations and parent counselling

### 7a. Recommendations by scenario

**1. Woman at 28–33+6 weeks, delivery not imminent.** Use either the WHO/ACOG regimen (dexamethasone 6 mg IM q12h × 4 = 24 mg) or the RCOG/ASTEROID regimen (dexamethasone 12 mg IM q24h × 2 = 24 mg). Both are guideline-endorsed and trial-tested; neither has been shown superior to the other for RDS or 2-year composite outcome. *Certainty: High that both are appropriately evidence-based options; Moderate that they are clinically equivalent (single RCT comparison, ASTEROID, for the 24-hourly schedule; no direct RCT for 12-hourly-vs-24-hourly dexamethasone specifically).* Do not use the 12 mg IM q12h regimen — it combines the per-dose amount of one tested regimen with the interval of a different one, and this specific combination has never been tested for efficacy or safety of any kind (Q1, Q3).

**2. Woman at 28–33+6 weeks, delivery expected in 12–24 hours.** Give the first dose immediately regardless of the regimen chosen — a partial course reduces risk relative to none, and this benefit is well established (Q3, corroborating the wider evidence base). **Do not compress the interval to give a second dose early "to complete the course."** The mechanistic evidence (Q4) shows no plausible pathway by which a second dose landing only ~1 hour before an expected birth could act on fetal lung maturation, while the harm evidence for short intervals (increased neonatal hypoglycaemia, HPA-axis loading) does not correspondingly diminish. **This unit's current practice of giving dexamethasone 12 mg IM at a 12-hourly interval specifically in order to complete a course before an anticipated delivery is not supported by any evidence identified in this search, and should be discontinued in favour of the standard 24-hourly interval for the RCOG regimen, or the standard 12-hourly interval only when using the matched WHO 6 mg × 4 regimen.** *Certainty: the recommendation to give the first dose regardless of timing is Moderate; the recommendation against compressing the interval is built on absence of a plausible mechanism (Q4) rather than a direct trial finding, and should be labelled as such.*

**3. Woman who has had one dose 6 hours ago, delivery imminent in 2 hours.** Do not give a second dose solely to complete the course. On the mechanistic time-course evidence (Q4), no glucocorticoid dose given this close to delivery has a plausible pathway to influence fetal lung maturation before birth, and there is no guideline recommendation identified anywhere that supports giving a second dose in this scenario. This is distinct from, and must not be confused with, giving the *first* dose even when delivery seems imminent (recommendation 2) — that recommendation concerns dose 1, not a compressed dose 2. *Certainty: absence-of-evidence for benefit; genuinely unknown, not evidence-based-safe, for harm (Q4).*

**4. Woman with gestational diabetes at 30 weeks.** ACS remains indicated; diabetes is not a contraindication. Expect clinically significant maternal hyperglycaemia regardless of agent or interval, peaking within the first 24–48 hours and generally resolving by day 3 (Q5). Increase insulin dosing (50–80% increase in subcutaneous insulin, or initiate variable-rate IV insulin infusion) with at least hourly capillary glucose monitoring during and after administration, consistent with Joint British Diabetes Societies guidance (reconstructed from secondary sources; verify against current primary JBDS text). Direct evidence from institutions using the unit's own 12 mg/12-hourly regimen (Kaushal et al 2003, UK; Hong et al 2021, Malaysia) shows this specific regimen produces a real but self-limited (day-1-predominant) glycaemic disturbance, not obviously more alarming in shape than the wider 24-hourly literature — but no study has directly compared the two intervals, so equivalence in safety cannot be claimed, only that no alarming interval-specific signal has been incidentally observed where this regimen has been used. *Certainty: Low-Moderate for the glycaemic description; absence of evidence for any interval comparison.*

**5. Woman with clinical chorioamnionitis.** ACS should still be given if delivery is not imminent — the general evidence base for ACS efficacy does not show that active infection eliminates benefit, though no dedicated interval-specific evidence exists for this population. Do not delay delivery to complete a course when infection is driving the clinical picture. No evidence identified in this specific search addresses whether chorioamnionitis should change the interval decision one way or the other; this recommendation draws on the general chorioamnionitis-ACS literature established elsewhere, not on interval-specific data from this search. *Certainty: Low, extrapolated from the general chorioamnionitis literature; this specific brief did not generate dedicated chorioamnionitis-interval evidence.*

**6. Woman who has had betamethasone elsewhere, dexamethasone given as a second dose.** This constitutes a mixed-agent course. No clinical evidence of any kind addresses mixed courses. On pharmacological grounds (Q2 corrections notwithstanding — both drugs are phosphate esters without a UK depot fraction, so the two doses are pharmacokinetically more similar than previously assumed), a mixed course delivers real, meaningful combined glucocorticoid exposure but does not replicate the tested exposure profile of either single-agent regimen. Document explicitly as a non-standard/mixed course, record both agents/doses/timing, and manage clinically with the same consideration as an incomplete course. *Certainty: inference only, not graded clinical evidence.*

### 7b. Parent counselling content

*(Full plain-language script provided as a separate deliverable, `acs-parent-counselling-script.md`. Key content points, reading age ~12:)*

The injection (dexamethasone) helps a baby's lungs, and some other organs, get ready for the world if born early. Out of every 1000 babies whose mothers have a complete course, research combining studies of over 11,000 babies from around the world found roughly 26 fewer babies die, 43 fewer have serious breathing problems, and 14 fewer have bleeding in the brain, compared with 1000 babies whose mothers did not have the injection — though the exact benefit depends on how early the baby is. Even one injection, if that is all there is time for, is much better than none: research suggests a single dose closes around half the extra risk of being born early compared with having no injection at all, though a complete, well-timed course works best.

There are two versions of the injection medicine (dexamethasone and betamethasone) and current best evidence from the largest study comparing them directly shows no meaningful difference in how many babies survive without serious disability. If a unit gives two smaller doses close together rather than further apart, this is a genuine, unresolved medical question rather than a known danger — researchers have not yet studied whether the spacing between doses changes how well it works or how safe it is, for the type of injection (dexamethasone) most commonly used this way. What we can say honestly is that giving an injection when a baby is likely to arrive within the hour probably will not have time to help the lungs, though there is no clear evidence it causes harm either — it is simply an area where the evidence has not caught up with the question. If asked "does the timing between the two doses affect my baby's brain," the honest answer is: this has not been directly studied, either in this exact combination or interval; what has been studied — the standard injection schedules used for decades — has not shown a difference in brain development at age two in the largest trial available, though a possible small difference between the two medicines cannot be completely ruled out by that trial's numbers.

- The actual maternal or fetal plasma concentration-time profile after a 12mg IM dexamethasone dose in a pregnant woman, at either 12-hourly or 24-hourly redosing — never measured (Q2).

- Why the 12mg/12-hourly dexamethasone regimen appears to have persisted in at least three jurisdictions (UK, Malaysia, Singapore) for at least two decades without ever being the subject of an efficacy or safety trial (Q3).
