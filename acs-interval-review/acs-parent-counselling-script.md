# Talking to Parents About the Steroid Injection Timing — A Counselling Script

*Plain-language content, reading age approximately 12. For use alongside a conversation with the clinical team, not as a replacement for it.*

---

## What the injection is and why it's given

If your baby might be born early, you may be offered an injection called dexamethasone (or sometimes a similar medicine called betamethasone). It helps your baby's lungs, and some other organs, get ready to work outside the womb. It's usually given as two injections, spaced either 12 or 24 hours apart depending on which version is used.

## What it does to the baby

The medicine crosses to your baby through the placenta and switches on genes inside their developing lungs that make a substance called surfactant, which helps tiny air sacs stay open when your baby takes their first breaths. It also has smaller effects on other organs, including helping the gut and brain finish some of their preparation for birth.

## How much difference does it make?

Research combining results from more than 11,000 babies around the world found that, compared with 1000 babies who didn't have the injection, out of 1000 babies who did:
- About 26 fewer babies died in the newborn period.
- About 43 fewer babies needed serious help with breathing.
- About 14 fewer babies had bleeding inside the brain.

These numbers are an average — the benefit tends to be bigger the earlier a baby is born, and smaller if a baby is close to full term.

## What if only one injection was possible?

If your baby arrived before the second injection, that's absolutely fine — one injection is still much better than none. Research suggests a single dose cuts around half the extra risk linked to being born early, compared with having no injection at all.

## The specific question about timing between the two doses

Some parents ask why the two doses were close together, or further apart, or worry about what that means. Here is the honest answer:

**There are different accepted ways of giving this medicine.** Some hospitals give it as two bigger doses a full day apart. Others give it as four smaller doses spread across two days, 12 hours apart each. Both of these are well-tested and used worldwide.

**A specific pattern — two full-size doses only 12 hours apart — has not been properly tested.** This isn't the same as saying it's dangerous. It means researchers haven't specifically studied whether spacing the two doses this way works as well, or is exactly as safe, as the two well-tested patterns above. If your care involved this specific pattern, your team can talk you through why, and what it means for your baby's records going forward — it will likely be noted as a non-standard course, which is simply a way of keeping accurate information, not a sign that something went wrong.

## If the second dose was given very close to the actual birth

If your baby arrived only an hour or so after the second injection, it's natural to wonder whether that dose did anything. Based on how this medicine works — it takes many hours, not minutes, for it to switch on the relevant processes in a baby's lungs — a dose given that close to birth is unlikely to have had time to help in the way the earlier dose did. This does not mean it caused harm; there's no evidence that it did. It simply means the main benefit your baby received almost certainly came from the earlier dose, which had more time to work.

## "Does the timing between doses affect my baby's brain?"

This is one of the most important questions to answer honestly, and here is the straight version:

*"The pattern of steroid injections used in this hospital for most babies — either two full doses a day apart, or four smaller doses spread across two days — has been followed up in large studies, including one that followed people for over 30 years after they had this treatment before birth. Those studies haven't found a difference in thinking, memory, or mental health. There is a separate, more specific question about the exact spacing between two doses — whether 12 hours apart between two full doses is exactly as safe for the brain as 24 hours apart. Nobody has directly studied that question in humans yet. What we do know from how the medicine behaves in the body is that giving two full doses close together does lead to higher levels of the medicine overlapping in the body for a while, and there's an early-stage animal study suggesting this pattern specifically might be worth more caution — but this hasn't been shown to cause any problem in real babies, and we want to be honest that this is a genuine unanswered question rather than a known risk. What is well established is that the standard treatment, given the usual ways, does help far more than any theoretical worry about brain safety would justify avoiding it."*

## A note on repeat courses, if relevant

If your pregnancy continues and a second full course is being discussed because you remain at high risk of an early birth, ask your team specifically about the timing — a second course is generally only considered after at least a week has passed since the first, and there's a maximum number of courses recommended. This is a different situation from the two-dose timing question above.

## Questions you might want to ask

- Which pattern of injection did I have, and were both doses given as planned?
- If the timing wasn't the usual pattern, why, and does that change anything for my baby's care?
- What will you be watching for in my baby because of how the injections were timed?
- If I need this again in a future pregnancy, what would you recommend?

---

*This script is a starting point for a conversation with your own obstetric and neonatal team, who know your specific situation. It reflects the best available evidence at the time of writing, and is written to be honest about what is and is not yet known — including where the answer is genuinely "we don't know yet," rather than offering false reassurance.*
