# Domain B — ABO haemolytic disease efficacy

Search: PubMed + Consensus, 20 Sep 2026 (full strings in `01-search-log/searches-B.md`, searches B1–B3 plus the Consensus cross-check and the Domain A cross-reference). Corpus of 67 records on ABO+IVIG+neonate; restricted trial/SR/MA search yielded 9 records, all included/cross-referenced. The Consensus cross-check added 7 further verified leads not captured by the publication-type filter — mostly retrospective cohorts, which is exactly the study design the RCT/SR/MA filter is expected to miss. Extraction rows B1–B10 in `02-extraction/extraction-B.csv` (6 RCTs, 4 non-randomised cohort/case-control studies); the systematic reviews and the one clinical guideline identified (Cortey 2014, Li 2010, Gottstein & Cooke 2003, plus the Domain A reviews Alcock & Liley 2002, Louis 2014, Zwiers 2018, and the Lieberman 2022 BJH international guideline / Slaughter 2022 AAP technical report) are synthesised narratively below rather than given individual extraction rows, matching the convention set in `domain-A.md`.

## (a) What the evidence shows, and in whom

Ten primary studies constitute the ABO-specific evidence base: six RCTs (Miqdad/Shaheed 2004 PMID 15590442; Huang 2006 PMID 16982453; Nasseri 2006 PMID 17143357, ABO subgroup; Alpay 1999 PMID 10102158; Tanyer 2001 PMID 11245352; Hu 2026 PMID 41968063) and four retrospective cohort/case-control studies (Okulu 2022 PMID 35573949, n=531; Daunov 2022 PMID 38286423, n=579; Pan 2021 PMID 33589732, n=114; Al-Lawama 2019 PMID 31803318, n=202). Three of the six RCTs enrol ABO disease exclusively (Miqdad, Huang, Hu); the other three enrol mixed ABO+Rh populations and either report an ABO-specific subgroup result (Nasseri — the only trial in either domain that directly contrasts Rh vs ABO response within one protocol) or do not disaggregate by antibody type at all (Alpay, Tanyer). All four cohort studies are ABO-specific (Al-Lawama is "predominantly ABO" but not disaggregated numerically). Populations are uniformly term or near-term; DAT positivity is required for trial entry where stated (Miqdad, Nasseri, Alpay), and one cohort (Daunov) reports a DAT-positivity prevalence of 74% without stratifying outcomes by DAT status.

The pooled evidence, drawn from the three dedicated systematic reviews that include ABO trials, tells a **more discouraging story for ABO disease than for Rh disease** (cf. `domain-A.md`). Cortey et al. 2014 (Arch Pédiatr, PMID 25125032), an ABO-specific meta-analysis of 6 trials (out of 28 screened), found RR 0.27 (95% CI 0.17–0.42) for exchange transfusion, NNT 5. Louis et al. 2014 (PMID 24514437, the pivotal review already extracted for Domain A) pooled 5 ABO trials, n=350: RR 0.31 (95% CI 0.18–0.55) — but explicitly states that **only studies with high risk of bias were available for the ABO analysis**; unlike the Rh analysis in the same paper, there is no low-risk-of-bias ABO tier to sensitivity-check this estimate against. Zwiers et al. 2018 (Cochrane, PMID 29551014) pools Rh and ABO together (9 trials, n=658; overall RR 0.35, 95% CI 0.25–0.49) but its critical placebo-controlled, low-risk-of-bias sensitivity analysis (RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.48–1.98) is drawn from the two Rh-only trials identified in Domain A (Leiden and Brazil) — **no equivalent placebo-controlled low-risk-of-bias ABO trial exists in the literature identified by any of the three reviews**. This is the central finding of Domain B: the apparent benefit of IVIG in ABO disease rests entirely on trials with acknowledged high risk of bias, and the rigorous, blinded, placebo-controlled trial design that overturned the apparent Rh benefit has never been run for ABO disease.

Consistent with this, the newest and largest ABO-specific datasets — all non-randomised, but numerically the biggest in this domain by a wide margin — point in a null-or-adverse direction. Okulu et al. 2022 (Turkish national registry, n=531) found that a single dose of IVIG given near the exchange-transfusion threshold did **not** prevent exchange transfusion and was instead associated with a *higher* exchange-transfusion rate, longer phototherapy, more re-hospitalisation, and more acute bilirubin encephalopathy (all p≤0.01) than phototherapy alone. Daunov et al. 2022 (US single-centre cohort, n=579, the largest cohort with a documented DAT-positivity rate) found IVIG was not associated with reduced transfusion use or number (p=0.51, p=0.33). Pan et al. 2021 (n=114) and Al-Lawama et al. 2019 (n=202) found no benefit or a paradoxical harm signal respectively. In every one of these four cohorts the direction of the observed association is attributable at least in part to confounding by indication — IVIG was preferentially given to infants with more severe disease (lower baseline haemoglobin, higher bilirubin, earlier presentation) — so none can be read as evidence of *causal* harm, but equally none supports a benefit, and the shared direction across four independent, differently-designed cohorts in three countries is notable.

## (b) Why results might differ between studies

As in Domain A, era and study design explain most of the variance, but with an important structural difference: **there is no ABO-specific RCT of adequate rigour (placebo-controlled, blinded, modern phototherapy) to test whether the same era-effect resolution seen in Rh disease would also apply here.** All six ABO/mixed RCTs identified are open-label or have blinding status unstated, and none uses a documented "intensive" phototherapy comparator of the kind used in the Leiden and Brazilian Rh trials. The positive ABO-specific RCTs (Miqdad 2004, Huang 2006) and the positive ABO-subgroup finding embedded in mixed trials (Alpay 1999, and by contrast Nasseri's *negative* ABO subgroup) are all small, single-centre, and unblinded — exactly the profile Louis 2014 flags as "high risk of bias" for the ABO tier specifically. A second explanatory factor unique to this domain is **confounding by indication in the newer, larger, non-randomised cohorts**: because IVIG in routine ABO practice is often given selectively to infants approaching the exchange threshold (Okulu, Al-Lawama) or with more severe baseline haemolysis (Daunov, Al-Lawama), any observational comparison is structurally biased toward making IVIG look ineffective or harmful, independent of its true effect — this cuts in the opposite direction from the era-effect bias in Domain A's early Rh trials, but has the same net effect of making the pooled high-risk-of-bias RCT estimate untrustworthy as a stand-alone number.

**Rh vs ABO comparison (cross-referencing `domain-A.md`):** Nasseri et al. 2006 (PMID 17143357), the one trial that randomised both antibody types under an identical protocol, explicitly reports that "the efficacy of IVIg was significantly better in Rh versus ABO isoimmunization" — i.e. even within a single trial, at a time when the overall pooled literature judged both antibody types to show benefit, IVIG performed *worse* against ABO haemolysis than against Rh haemolysis. This is consistent with the underlying immunology and mechanism note (naturally-occurring anti-A/anti-B is largely IgM as well as IgG, ABO haemolysis is typically milder and slower-onset than Rh haemolysis, and the population at risk of severe ABO disease is less reliably identified antenatally than Rh disease), and it means the Domain A conclusion — that the best-conducted trials in Rh disease are null — cannot be assumed to generalise favourably to ABO disease; if anything the ABO evidence base is *weaker* in its methodological foundations (no low-risk-of-bias trial at all) even though its headline pooled RR looks superficially similar to the pre-2011 Rh pooled estimate.

## (c) Dose/timing used

Doses in the six RCTs ranged from 400 mg/kg/day ×2–3 days (Huang 2006) to 500 mg/kg single dose (Miqdad 2004) to 1 g/kg single dose (Alpay 1999, mixed population) to 0.5 g/kg every 12h ×3 doses (Nasseri 2006, ABO subgroup). Tanyer 2001 is the only trial directly comparing multiple-dose vs single-dose vs no-IVIG regimens (mixed ABO/Rh, n=61): exchange transfusion occurred in 0% (multiple dose), 12% (single dose) and 33% (no IVIG), with no RR/CI given in the abstract — suggestive of a dose-response relationship, but from a small, unblinded, three-arm trial. A 2026 dose-comparison meta-analysis identified via the Consensus cross-check (Huang et al., not ABO-specific, 10 RCTs n=690, PMID 42458120) found high-dose IVIG associated with a lower exchange-transfusion rate than low-dose IVIG (Peto OR 0.24, 95% CI 0.12–0.50) and shorter phototherapy/hospital stay — broadly consistent with Domain A's Elalfy 2010 dose-comparison finding (0.5 vs 1 g/kg, higher dose better) but again drawn predominantly from high-risk-of-bias trials. Timing in the ABO-specific trials is uniformly early/rescue (as soon as significant hyperbilirubinaemia is identified or bilirubin approaches the exchange threshold), rather than prophylactic; the 2026 Hu et al. RCT (post-plasma-exchange IVIG) is the only trial using a materially different treatment paradigm, and it does not report an exchange-transfusion outcome at all — its comparator arms are biomarker- and quality-of-life-focused, which limits its relevance to the guideline's primary question. **No trial in this domain randomised prophylactic-vs-rescue timing** — the same gap noted in Domain A.

## (d) Where it failed or was null

Unlike Domain A, there is no single placebo-controlled RCT in ABO disease that can be pointed to as the definitive null result. Instead, the null/adverse signal in this domain comes from convergent non-randomised evidence: Okulu 2022 (n=531, the largest ABO-specific dataset identified in either domain) found IVIG associated with *more* exchange transfusion, not less, when given near the exchange threshold; Daunov 2022 (n=579) and Pan 2021 (n=114) found no significant difference on any outcome; Al-Lawama 2019 (n=202) found IVIG-treated infants had significantly worse hyperbilirubinaemia and more transfusion, though this is confounded by indication rather than necessarily causal. Nasseri 2006's within-trial ABO subgroup was also non-significant even in an era (mid-2000s) when the Rh-disease literature was still predominantly positive. Taken together, four independent cohorts spanning Turkey, the USA, China and Jordan, plus one RCT subgroup, all point the same direction (no benefit, or possible harm), even though none individually is a rigorous causal test. The one clear positive dose-comparison signal (Tanyer 2001, multiple-dose vs single-dose vs none) is a 61-patient unblinded trial and does not resolve this.

**DAT-negative ABO disease — the named secondary research question:** a dedicated search (`searches-B.md`, search B3) combining ABO terms with Coombs/DAT/direct-antiglobulin terms and IVIG returned 21 records; none reports an efficacy outcome (exchange transfusion, phototherapy duration, or any other) stratified by DAT-negative vs DAT-positive status. Every trial that specifies DAT status at all requires DAT positivity for enrolment (Miqdad, Nasseri, Alpay). The single cohort that quantifies DAT positivity in an unselected ABO-HDN population (Daunov 2022: 431/579, 74%, implying roughly a quarter of the cohort was DAT-negative) does not report outcomes separately for the DAT-negative subgroup. **No direct evidence identified** for whether IVIG efficacy differs in DAT-negative versus DAT-positive ABO disease; any extrapolation from the DAT-positive trial evidence to DAT-negative infants (who make up a substantial minority of clinically-diagnosed ABO-HDN in practice) is an assumption, not a finding, and should be flagged as such in the guideline.

## (e) Implication for the unit guideline

**GRADE: Very low certainty that IVIG reduces exchange transfusion in ABO haemolytic disease of the newborn.** Reason for downgrade: very serious risk of bias (every RCT showing benefit is unblinded/open-label and explicitly characterised as high-risk-of-bias by the one ABO-specific meta-analysis that assessed study quality — Louis 2014 — with **no low-risk-of-bias ABO trial existing at all**, unlike Rh disease where two such trials exist and are null); serious inconsistency (the four largest, most recent, non-randomised datasets converge on a null-or-adverse direction, discordant with the positive small unblinded RCTs); and indirectness for the DAT-negative subgroup, where no data exist at all. This is a materially lower certainty rating than Domain A's Low-to-Moderate certainty for Rh disease, and the direction of the best available (if still weak) evidence in ABO disease is, if anything, less favourable to IVIG than in Rh disease — both because Nasseri's head-to-head comparison found Rh responded better than ABO, and because ABO lacks any rigorous placebo-controlled trial to counter the high-risk-of-bias positive trials, whereas Rh disease has two.

This synthesis is concordant with, though independently derived from primary trial data rather than borrowed from, the two dedicated evidence-based statements identified in this domain: the Lieberman et al. 2022 international multi-society guideline (BJH, PMID 35415922 — logged as guidance, not evidence, per CLAUDE.md rule 8) concludes "IVIG should not be routinely used to treat Rh or ABO antibody-mediated HDN," and the 2022 AAP technical report (Slaughter et al., PMID 35927519, also guidance) states IVIG "has unclear benefit for preventing exchange transfusion in infants with isoimmune hemolytic disease, with a possible risk of harm due to necrotizing enterocolitis" (the NEC signal itself is addressed in Domain E, not here). Both guidance documents post-date and are consistent with the primary-evidence pattern found independently in this domain's search.

For the Imperial NICU guideline, the practical implication is that **ABO disease has an even weaker evidentiary case for routine IVIG than Rh disease does**, and the largest real-world datasets available (Okulu, Daunov, Pan, Al-Lawama) — while individually confounded — collectively give no reassurance of benefit and some signal of harm associated with IVIG use in practice, most plausibly reflecting reverse causation (sicker infants selected for treatment) rather than a true adverse drug effect. Any guideline recommendation to use IVIG in ABO disease should rest explicitly on individual clinical judgement in infants approaching the exchange-transfusion threshold where exchange transfusion carries its own risks, not on a presumption of trial-proven efficacy, and should explicitly note that the DAT-negative subgroup — a meaningful fraction of clinically-diagnosed ABO-HDN — has **no direct evidence** to guide practice at all.
