## (a) What the evidence shows, and in whom

**Necrotising enterocolitis (NEC) — the primary focus of this domain.** The core calibrated search returned three quantitative comparative studies, one meta-analysis, and a run of case reports/series spanning 1990–2025, plus one AAP technical-report evidence synthesis. The signal is real in the sense that it recurs across three decades and multiple countries, but its magnitude and even its direction depend heavily on study design. The largest quantitative signal comes from Figueras-Aloy et al. 2009 (Spain, retrospective cohort, n=492 term/late-preterm infants with severe Rh or ABO haemolytic jaundice, PMID 19948572): NEC occurred in 10/167 (6%) IVIG-treated infants versus 1/325 (0.3%) untreated, with IVIG an independent predictor on multivariate analysis (adjusted OR 31.66, 95% CI 3.25–308.57); five infants required urgent surgery and two died (one of massive intestinal necrosis, one later of short bowel syndrome). Corvaglia et al. 2012 (Italy, retrospective cohort, n=88 Rh-HDN, PMID 22882154) found 2/54 (3.7%) NEC in the IVIg group versus 0/34 in the no-IVIg group, alongside more top-up transfusions and longer hospitalisation in the IVIg arm — the authors themselves flag this as a side effect requiring monitoring rather than a settled causal finding. Yang et al. 2016's meta-analysis of five studies (four Chinese-database, one presumably international; total n not stated in the available abstract; PMID 27735024) pooled these and similar studies to an OR of 4.53 (95% CI 2.34–8.79, p<0.00001), while noting the IVIG and control groups differed significantly in birth weight at baseline — an explicit signal of baseline non-comparability between arms.

Set against this, the largest and best-controlled comparative study identified — Li et al. 2022 (China, n=1259 late-preterm/term HDN infants, 192 IVIG-treated, using propensity-score matching, interaction analysis, and multivariable logistic regression, PMID 35613867) — found **no significant association** between IVIG and NEC (5/192, 2.6% vs 24/1067, 2.2%; p>0.05), and no interactive effect of IVIG with prematurity, low birth weight, caesarean delivery, low admission haemoglobin, or patent ductus arteriosus — the factors that *were* independently associated with NEC. A smaller cohort (Freyne et al. 2011, Ireland/UK, n=74, 33 IVIG-treated Rh-disease infants, PMID 22171016) reported the single NEC case in the whole cohort occurring in the *non*-IVIG-treated infant. Between these two poles sit a cluster of single case reports and small series describing NEC (including one localised to appendiceal perforation, Atikan et al. 2015, PMID 25899199, with onset within 10 hours of infusion) and, in Navarro et al. 2009's three-case series (Spain, PMID 19397554), histopathological confirmation of disseminated microvascular thrombi in the resected bowel — a specific, testable mechanistic hypothesis (IVIG-associated hyperviscosity/thrombosis causing mesenteric ischaemia) rather than a vague association. The most recent case report identified, Çıplak et al. 2025 (Turkey, PMID 40405449), describes a *fatal* NEC temporally associated with IVIG infusion in a term infant with HDN, confirming the signal remains an active clinical concern; only the title could be verified this session (no abstract available anywhere), so no further numeric detail is transcribed. A single case report in a related but distinct alloimmune neonatal disease — neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia rather than HDFN — proposed the same thrombotic mechanism as early as 1990 (Merlob et al., PMID 2332014); this is flagged **INDIRECT** throughout, as it is a different disease (platelet, not red-cell, alloimmunisation), included only because it independently tests the same product/dose/mechanism hypothesis.

The one identified prospective mechanistic study directly testing the vascular hypothesis in vivo — Louis/Patil et al. 2015 (India, n=30, Doppler ultrasound of coeliac and superior mesenteric artery flow before/immediately after/12–18h after IVIg, PMID 25598445) — found no clinically meaningful change in intestinal blood flow and no NEC or feed intolerance in its cohort, though it mixed Rh-HDN with alloimmune thrombocytopenia infants and is underpowered to detect a signal of the magnitude reported in the larger retrospective cohorts. The 2022 AAP technical report underpinning the current US hyperbilirubinaemia guideline (Slaughter et al., *Pediatrics*, PMID 35927519 — a peer-reviewed evidence synthesis, treated narratively here rather than as an extraction row, following the precedent set in Domain A for systematic reviews) reached essentially the same overall conclusion as this domain's own synthesis: "IVIG has unclear benefit for preventing exchange transfusion in infants with isoimmune hemolytic disease, with a possible risk of harm due to necrotizing enterocolitis," and it revised the 2022 AAP guideline specifically "to limit IVIG use" on this basis. A 2022 international expert-panel evidence-based guideline (Lieberman et al., *British Journal of Haematology*, PMID 35415922) reached the same practical conclusion independently: "IVIG should not be routinely used to treat Rh or ABO antibody-mediated HDN."

**IVIG-induced haemolysis from product isoagglutinins.** This is a distinct, mechanistically well-characterised harm, separate from the underlying alloimmune haemolysis the infant already has. IVIG products are pooled donor plasma and inevitably contain some residual anti-A/anti-B isoagglutinins; passively transferred, these can coat the recipient's own red cells (particularly in blood group A or AB recipients) and cause a positive direct antiglobulin test and, in a minority, clinically significant haemolysis with anaemia and (rarely) renal impairment. The most substantial evidence here is Desborough et al. 2013's review of MHRA, EU, FDA and Canada Vigilance data plus a systematic review of published case reports (PMID 24164446): 925 vigilance-reported cases and 62 published case reports were identified (nearly all in **adults**, so this is **INDIRECT** for the neonatal HDFN population specifically), 97% at cumulative IVIG doses ≥2 g/kg, most commonly in blood group A (65%) or AB (26%) recipients. Directly in a neonate, Christensen et al. 2015 (USA, single case, PMID 26074177) used end-tidal carbon monoxide monitoring — a direct physiological measure of haem catabolism/haemolysis rate — before, during and after two 500 mg/kg doses of IVIG in a group-O, D-positive neonate with Rh erythroblastosis fetalis, and found haemolysis *increased*, not decreased, after both doses: the opposite of IVIG's intended mechanism of action. A 2026 neonate-specific laboratory study (Ling et al., n=30, though for sepsis/NEC/undetermined indications rather than HDFN specifically — **INDIRECT** by population — PMID 41543094) confirmed the mechanism is laboratory-detectable in neonates at doses (0.8–2.9 g/kg) overlapping typical HDFN regimens: all 30 neonates developed a positive direct antiglobulin test, free-antibody test and elution test after IVIG, with a statistically significant fall in hameoglobin/red-cell count and rise in LDH/unconjugated bilirubin, and cross-matching incompatibility by the standard gel-card method in all 30 (resolved by switching to a tube method) — a practical blood-bank finding relevant to timing top-up transfusions after IVIG.

**Thromboembolism and hyperviscosity.** No study identified in the neonatal HDFN population directly quantifies a thromboembolism or hyperviscosity incidence attributable to IVIG. **No direct evidence identified** (CLAUDE.md rule 10). The evidence base is extrapolated: the FDA's 2013 boxed-warning update for thromboembolism across all human immune globulin products (all ages, all routes, all indications; general population risk factors of advanced age, immobilisation, hypercoagulable states, indwelling catheters and hyperviscosity are named, none of them neonate-specific) and the mechanistic/histopathological thrombosis findings in the NEC case reports above (Navarro 2009, PMID 19397554; Merlob 1990, PMID 2332014, INDIRECT) are the closest available evidence, and both are indirect.

**Hypotension and infusion reactions.** The direct RCT evidence in this domain (cross-referenced to Domain A) is reassuring: Santos et al. 2012 (Brazil, the highest-directness null RCT, Domain A row A7) reported "no adverse events related to the drug or the form of administration," and Miqdad et al. 2004 (Saudi Arabia, RCT, ABO-HDN, n=112, PMID 15590442) reported no adverse effects related to IVIG administration. Freyne et al. 2011 (PMID 22171016) reported no adverse effects at time of transfusion across 58 IVIG doses. Two small retrospective series report a single infusion-associated event each: Vardar et al. 2022 (Turkey, n=63, PMID 35975373) recorded apnoea as the only complication, in 1/63 (1.6%) infants, and Zheng et al. 2023's repeat-dosing case series (USA, n=11, 3–4 doses each, PMID 38143377) reported no adverse events at all. Taken together, acute infusion-related hypotension/reactions appear to be an uncommon harm in this population at the doses typically used (0.5–1 g/kg), though none of these studies was designed or powered to detect a rare event.

**Infection transmission.** No case of pathogen transmission via IVIG in the neonatal HDFN population was identified in this search. **No direct evidence identified.** Modern IVIG manufacturing includes viral inactivation/removal steps, and this domain's search strategy (E1–E4) was not designed to specifically capture historical plasma-product infection-transmission literature (which predates current manufacturing standards and is largely outside a 2026 UK NICU's relevant risk window); this is recorded as a scope limitation rather than a finding of safety.

**Late hyporegenerative anaemia and top-up transfusion requirement.** Per the task brief, this is fundamentally a monitoring issue intrinsic to HDN itself, not primarily an IVIG-attributable harm, and the evidence bears this out. The narrowed search (E2b, 29 records, all screened) found late/hyporegenerative anaemia described repeatedly as a feature of the underlying disease process — principally intrauterine-transfusion-related bone-marrow suppression and ongoing extravascular haemolysis — independent of postnatal IVIG use. Al-Alaiyan & al Omran 1999 (Saudi Arabia, n=36 Rh-HDN infants, PMID 10379500 — a different, verified paper from the one Consensus initially surfaced; see `could-not-verify-E.md`) found 30/36 (83%) developed late anaemia regardless of intrauterine transfusion history, and — counter-intuitively — that infants who received an *exchange* transfusion had *less* late anaemia, not more. Mitchell & James 1999 (Canada, PMID 20212966) and multiple erythropoietin-treatment case series (Ovali et al. 1996, a placebo-controlled RCT of EPO reducing transfusions from 4.2 to 1.8 per infant, PMID 8726237; Donato et al. 2009, n=50, 14% still required transfusion during EPO therapy, PMID 19452083) corroborate that this is a well-recognised, EPO-responsive complication of the disease itself.

Only two studies in this domain directly compare an IVIG-treated group against a non-IVIG comparator for late anaemia specifically. Nasseri et al. 2006 (Domain A row A5, PMID 17143357) found late anaemia in 11.8% of the IVIG group versus 0% of controls — numerically higher but not statistically significant (p=0.48) in this small (n=34) trial. Miqdad et al. 2004 (E21, PMID 15590442, ABO-HDN RCT, n=112) reported late anaemia was "not of concern in either group," with no numeric rate given by arm. Both of Domain A's highest-directness, double-blind, placebo-controlled null RCTs also directly addressed *top-up transfusion* specifically (a closely related but distinct outcome from "late anaemia" as a diagnosis): Smits-Wintjens et al. 2011 (Domain A row A6) found top-up transfusion required in 34/41 (83%) of the IVIg group versus 34/39 (87%) of the placebo group — if anything numerically *fewer* in the IVIG group, and not significantly different. A large Japanese national survey (Ono et al. 2023, n=916 BTHDN infants, 219 IVIG-treated, PMID 38037498) found approximately 20% of IVIG-treated infants developed late-onset anaemia requiring treatment — but this is a single-arm descriptive figure with **no non-IVIG comparator**, so per the task brief's explicit instruction it cannot be used to conclude IVIG causes or increases late anaemia; it is presented here purely as background incidence context, consistent with (not distinguishable from) the substantial baseline rate already described above.

## (b) Why results might differ between studies

**Confounding by indication is the central, unresolved problem in the NEC literature, and it is discussed here explicitly as instructed.** Every quantitative NEC-signal study identified (Figueras-Aloy 2009, Corvaglia 2012, Yang 2016's pooled studies) shares the same structural vulnerability: IVIG in this literature is not randomly allocated but is given, by clinical protocol, specifically to the *sicker* infants — those approaching the exchange-transfusion threshold, with the fastest-rising or highest bilirubin, and (mechanistically) the most active ongoing haemolysis. These same features — severe haemolysis, higher bilirubin, greater physiological instability — are themselves plausible independent risk factors for NEC via mechanisms unrelated to IVIG (e.g. relative gut hypoperfusion during acute severe anaemia/haemolysis, more frequent exchange transfusion and top-up transfusion exposure — and transfusion itself is an independently well-established NEC risk factor, per Mohamed et al. 2012's meta-analysis of "transfusion-associated NEC," PMID 22351894, cited here for mechanistic context only, not as IVIG evidence). Figueras-Aloy 2009's multivariate model adjusts for caesarean delivery and 5-minute Apgar score, but *not* for bilirubin severity or rate of rise — i.e., not for the specific clinical indication that drove IVIG use in the first place. Yang 2016's own pooled data show a statistically significant birth-weight imbalance between IVIG and control groups, which the authors report but do not resolve — itself evidence of baseline non-comparability. Two further studies surfaced via Consensus and independently PubMed-verified illustrate the same problem outside the strict NEC question: Okulu et al. 2022 (Turkish national registry, n=531 ABO-HDN, PMID 35573949) found the IVIG group had significantly lower baseline haemoglobin (i.e., were more anaemic/severe) and went on to have *higher* rates of exchange transfusion, longer phototherapy, more rehospitalisation, and more acute bilirubin encephalopathy — outcomes that, read naively, would suggest IVIG is harmful across the board, but that the study's own baseline-severity imbalance makes uninterpretable as a causal claim. Al-lawama et al. 2019 (Jordan, n=202, PMID 31803318) shows the identical pattern: the IVIG group had significantly higher baseline bilirubin (14.6 vs 12.6 mg/dL, p=0.0001) and more downstream transfusion/exchange-transfusion, while the authors themselves report "no side effects documented" — an internally revealing inconsistency that only makes sense if the excess downstream morbidity is attributed to baseline severity rather than to IVIG.

Against this backdrop, Li et al. 2022 (PMID 35613867) stands out as the only study in this domain specifically designed, via propensity-score matching and multivariable/interaction analysis, to separate IVIG's effect from the severity/indication that drives its use — and it found no NEC signal. This does not prove IVIG is safe; propensity-score methods can only adjust for *measured* confounders (this study did not have, or at least does not report having, a direct measure of bilirubin rate-of-rise or haemolysis intensity at the point IVIG was given), and residual confounding by unmeasured severity cannot be excluded. But it is the single strongest piece of evidence that the pooled OR of 4.53 (Yang 2016) and the striking single-study OR of 31.66 (Figueras-Aloy 2009) likely overstate IVIG's true causal contribution to NEC, because neither of those studies controls for the confound that this domain's own task brief specifically flagged as central.

**A second explanatory factor is dose and administration context.** The isoagglutinin-haemolysis literature (Desborough 2013, PMID 24164446) found 97% of cases occurred at cumulative doses ≥2 g/kg — well above a single 0.5–1 g/kg HDFN dose but squarely within the range used in Kara et al. 2013's two-course (2×1 g/kg = 2 g/kg cumulative) ABO-HDN case (PMID 23930883) and in the repeat-dosing regimens seen elsewhere in Domain A (Nasseri 2006, 3×0.5 g/kg = 1.5 g/kg) and Domain E (Zheng 2023, 3–4 doses, PMID 38143377). A 2026 dose-comparison meta-analysis of 10 RCTs (Huang et al., n=690, PMID 42458120) found no significant difference in *overall* adverse-reaction rate between high- and low-dose IVIG arms, but did not analyse NEC as a distinct outcome — so this cannot settle whether cumulative dose specifically drives the NEC or haemolysis signal, only that it does not obviously drive a generic "adverse reaction" composite.

**A third explanatory factor, and arguably the most informative single contrast identified in this domain, is indication/population mismatch between the HDFN literature and the wider neonatal IVIG literature.** The Cochrane 2020 update on prophylactic IVIG for infection prevention in preterm/low-birth-weight infants (Ohlsson & Lacy, 19 RCTs, ~5000 infants, PMID 31995650) found **no significant difference in NEC** incidence, and explicitly states "no major adverse effects of IVIG were reported in any of these studies." This is a fundamentally different population and indication — prophylactic, repeated, lower-acuity dosing in infants who are not actively haemolysing — and is flagged **INDIRECT** throughout for that reason. But the contrast is instructive: if IVIG per se, as a drug class, reliably caused NEC, one might expect some signal across ~5000 preterm infants receiving it prophylactically. Its absence there, set against the signal (however confounded) in the acute HDFN-treatment literature, is consistent with the harm — if real — being specifically linked to the acute severe-haemolysis physiological state and/or to the different (often single, larger-bolus) dosing pattern used in HDFN treatment, rather than to IVIG as a drug class in general. This is offered as an interpretive hypothesis, not a settled finding, and is exactly the kind of "why results might differ between studies" question the task brief asks to be surfaced rather than resolved by fiat.

## (c) Dose/timing used (as relevant to harms)

Doses associated with a reported harm signal in this domain's case reports and cohorts range from single 500 mg/kg administrations (Atikan 2015, Christensen 2015 [2×500 mg/kg = 1 g/kg cumulative]) up to repeat 2–4-dose regimens reaching 2 g/kg or more cumulative (Kara 2013, 2×1 g/kg; Zheng 2023, 3–4 doses). The isoagglutinin-haemolysis vigilance literature (Desborough 2013) specifically identifies ≥2 g/kg cumulative dose as the threshold above which 97% of reported haemolysis cases occurred — a figure directly relevant to any unit protocol permitting repeat dosing (cross-reference Domain A's Nasseri 2006, A5, and Domain D for repeat-dose-interval questions generally). Timing relative to onset of NEC/harm in the case reports is short: Atikan 2015 reported onset within 10 hours of infusion; Navarro 2009's three cases developed signs "shortly after the perfusion finished." This short latency is consistent with either a direct pharmacological/immunological mechanism (isoagglutinin-mediated haemolysis, complement activation, hyperviscosity) or a mechanism secondary to the acute haemodynamic/rheological change of a rapid large-volume protein infusion, but the evidence identified cannot distinguish between these mechanistic hypotheses. No trial in this domain randomised dose or infusion rate specifically against a harms outcome; the one dose-comparison meta-analysis (Huang 2026) addressed efficacy and a composite adverse-reaction rate, not NEC or haemolysis specifically. **No direct evidence identified** for an optimal infusion rate that minimises harm in HDFN specifically; UK product SPCs (Privigen, KIOVIG, Flebogamma DIF — see `guidance-table.md`) give general graduated infusion-rate schedules (e.g. Privigen: initial 0.5 mg/kg/min, up to a maximum of 8 mg/kg/min if tolerated) that are not HDFN-specific and were not independently verified against the current in-force SPC text this session (WebFetch was blocked for medicines.org.uk).

## (d) Where it failed or was null

The clearest null findings in this domain are, deliberately, given equal billing with the positive-signal studies (CLAUDE.md rule 7). For NEC: Li et al. 2022's propensity-score-matched analysis of 1259 infants (PMID 35613867) — the largest and most rigorously confounding-controlled comparative study identified — found no significant IVIG-NEC association. Freyne et al. 2011 (PMID 22171016) found the sole NEC case in its cohort in the non-IVIG arm. The prospective mechanistic Doppler study (Louis/Patil 2015, PMID 25598445) found no clinically significant intestinal blood-flow compromise and no NEC in 30 infants. For haemolysis/infusion safety: three RCTs (Santos 2012/Domain A-A7, Miqdad 2004/E21, and the repeat-dosing case series Zheng 2023/E23) reported no IVIG-attributable adverse events at all. For late anaemia/top-up transfusion specifically: both of Domain A's highest-directness placebo-controlled RCTs (Smits-Wintjens 2011/A6, Santos 2012/A7) found no significant difference in top-up transfusion requirement between IVIG and placebo arms, and Miqdad 2004 (E21) explicitly reported late anaemia was "not of concern in either group." Taken together, the RCT-level evidence — as opposed to the retrospective cohort/case-report evidence — is consistently null or reassuring across nearly every harm this domain was asked to cover, with the important exception that none of these RCTs was powered to detect a rare event like NEC (Smits-Wintjens' and Santos' own publications note this explicitly, and this is flagged again in Domain A's synthesis, part (d)). The tension in this domain is therefore not really "positive trials vs null trials" (as in Domain A's efficacy question) but "underpowered/unconfounded RCTs showing no signal vs larger, better-powered but confounded-by-indication retrospective cohorts showing a signal" — a different and, if anything, harder methodological problem to resolve with the literature that currently exists.

## (e) Implication for the unit guideline

**GRADE: Very Low certainty that IVIG causes necrotising enterocolitis in neonatal HDFN, as a distinct effect separable from the disease severity that leads to IVIG being given.** Reason for downgrade: serious to very serious risk of bias across the positive-signal studies (retrospective, unadjusted or inadequately adjusted for the specific indication — bilirubin severity/rate of rise — that drives both IVIG use and NEC risk), serious inconsistency (the largest and best-confounding-controlled study, Li 2022, found no effect at all, directly contradicting the pooled meta-analytic OR), and serious indirectness for parts of the evidence base (much of the pooled meta-analytic signal derives from non-UK, Chinese-database studies of unclear phototherapy-era comparability). This does **not** mean the signal should be dismissed: it recurs across 35 years, multiple countries, and — critically — has a specific, testable, histopathologically supported mechanism (thrombotic mesenteric microvascular occlusion, Navarro 2009) that is biologically plausible for a hyperviscous, immunologically active blood product given to an already-compromised infant. The practical implication for a UK NICU guideline is that **IVIG should not be used routinely or prophylactically in HDFN**, consistent with the international expert consensus already identified (Lieberman et al. 2022, PMID 35415922; the 2022 AAP technical report, PMID 35927519) and with Domain A's own conclusion that IVIG's efficacy signal does not survive contemporary trial conditions. Where IVIG is used at all — reserved, on the current evidence, for situations where exchange transfusion is imminent or unavailable, per Lieberman 2022 — infants should be under enhanced monitoring for feed intolerance and abdominal signs for at least the first 24–48 hours post-infusion, given the short latency to onset reported across the case-report literature.

**GRADE: Very Low certainty for a quantified neonatal-HDFN-specific incidence of IVIG-induced isoagglutinin haemolysis**, downgraded for serious indirectness (the largest dataset, Desborough 2013's 925 vigilance cases, is overwhelmingly adult) and for the inherent limitations of case-report/spontaneous-reporting data (no denominator). Nonetheless the mechanism is well-established and directly demonstrated in a neonate by objective physiological monitoring (Christensen 2015) and by laboratory testing in a neonatal cohort, albeit not an HDFN-specific one (Ling 2026). The practical implication is that **any unit protocol permitting IVIG in HDFN should include a baseline and post-dose full blood count/reticulocyte count and direct antiglobulin test, with explicit awareness that a positive cross-match by gel-card technique after IVIG may reflect IVIG-induced isoagglutinin coating rather than a true incompatibility** (tube-method cross-matching as a workaround, per Ling 2026) — a detail with direct blood-bank operational relevance for timing any subsequent top-up transfusion. Cumulative dose ≥2 g/kg is the threshold most associated with haemolysis in the wider (largely adult) literature and is a reasonable trigger for closer monitoring if repeat dosing is used.

**GRADE: No direct evidence identified for thromboembolism, hyperviscosity, or infection transmission as IVIG-attributable harms specifically in neonatal HDFN** — the evidence here is entirely extrapolated (general-population FDA boxed-warning risk factors; mechanistic thrombosis findings from the NEC case reports) or absent. This absence should be stated as such in the guideline rather than papered over with an assumed-safe or assumed-unsafe default; it is also a genuine gap that UK haemovigilance (SHOT) does not appear to separately capture (see `guidance-table.md` and `searches-E.md`), which is itself a recommendation-worthy finding — **the guideline should recommend explicit local incident reporting for any suspected IVIG-associated thromboembolic or hyperviscosity event in a neonate, in addition to routine MHRA Yellow Card reporting**, precisely because the existing national surveillance infrastructure does not appear designed to detect this signal if it exists.

**GRADE: Low to Moderate certainty that IVIG does not meaningfully increase late anaemia or top-up transfusion requirement above the substantial baseline rate intrinsic to HDFN itself.** This is the most reassuring finding in this domain and rests on the two highest-directness, placebo-controlled RCTs in the entire evidence base (Domain A's Smits-Wintjens 2011 and Santos 2012), both of which found no significant difference in top-up transfusion rates between IVIG and placebo arms, corroborated by a second direct RCT comparison (Miqdad 2004) finding no meaningful late-anaemia difference. The one numerically higher (though non-significant) late-anaemia rate reported after IVIG (Nasseri 2006, 11.8% vs 0%) comes from the smallest of these trials (n=34) and should not be over-weighted against the two larger, better-conducted null RCTs. **Per the task brief's explicit instruction, this domain's synthesis does not conflate "late anaemia occurs in HDN" (which is well-established, EPO-responsive, and driven principally by intrauterine-transfusion-related marrow suppression and ongoing extravascular haemolysis — a monitoring issue for every HDN infant regardless of IVIG exposure) with "IVIG causes late anaemia" (for which the direct comparative evidence is null).** The practical implication for the guideline is that **routine post-discharge haemoglobin/reticulocyte surveillance for late anaemia should be recommended for all HDN infants with a history of significant haemolysis or intrauterine transfusion, irrespective of whether they received IVIG** — this is a disease-based, not an IVIG-based, monitoring recommendation, and the guideline should be explicit that IVIG receipt is not, on current evidence, a reason to intensify (or to relax) that surveillance beyond what the underlying disease severity already warrants.
