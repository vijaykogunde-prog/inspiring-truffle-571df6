# Domain A — Rh (anti-D and other Rh antibodies) efficacy

Search: PubMed, 20 Sep 2026 (full strings in `01-search-log/searches.md`, searches A1–A2). Corpus of 151 records on Rh+IVIG+neonate; restricted trial/SR/MA search yielded 12 records, 10 included after screening (2 excluded: one maternal-antenatal IVIG meta-analysis, one fetal-administration-during-IUT RCT — both out of scope on the postnatal-neonatal eligibility criterion). Extraction rows A1–A7 in `02-extraction/extraction.csv`; the three systematic reviews (Alcock & Liley 2002, Louis 2014, Zwiers 2018) are synthesised narratively below rather than given individual extraction rows, as they are reviews of the same primary trials already extracted.

## (a) What the evidence shows, and in whom

Ten primary RCTs and three systematic reviews/meta-analyses constitute the Rh-disease evidence base identified. All are RCTs or quasi-RCTs in term or near-term neonates with DAT-positive (where stated) Rh-D haemolytic disease; none is restricted to a preterm-only population. Study sizes are small throughout (n=32–92 analysed per trial). Design quality is bimodal: early trials (1992–2010) are open-label or unclear-blinding, single- or few-centre, and use "conventional" (non-intensive) phototherapy as the co-intervention; the two most recent trials (Smits-Wintjens/Leiden 2011, PMID 21422084; Santos/Brazil 2012, PMID 22882285) are double-blind, placebo-controlled, and explicitly describe modern (in the Brazilian trial, "high-intensity") phototherapy in both arms.

The three systematic reviews chart this evolution directly. The original 2002 Cochrane review (Alcock & Liley, PMID 12137687), based on 3 trials/189 infants (mixed Rh+ABO), found a large effect (typical RR for exchange transfusion 0.28, 95% CI 0.17–0.47; NNT 2.7) but explicitly noted the protocols of two of the three trials mandated early exchange transfusion, limiting generalisability. The 2014 update by Louis et al. (Archives of Disease in Childhood – Fetal & Neonatal Edition, PMID 24514437, DOI 10.1136/archdischild-2013-304878) is the pivotal analysis: across 10 Rh trials (n=463), studies at high risk of bias showed a large benefit (RR 0.23, 95% CI 0.13–0.40), but the low-risk-of-bias studies that also used prophylactic phototherapy showed no significant difference (RR 0.82, 95% CI 0.53–1.26) — the risk-of-bias split accounts for essentially the entire discrepancy in the literature. The 2018 Cochrane update (Zwiers et al., PMID 29551014, DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD003313.pub2), now with 9 trials/658 infants (Rh and ABO combined), reproduces this pattern: overall RR 0.35 (95% CI 0.25–0.49) across all trials, but the two trials at lowest risk of bias (placebo-controlled, blinded outcome assessment — these are the Leiden and Brazilian Rh trials in this domain) show no difference at all (RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.48–1.98).

## (b) Why results might differ between studies

The between-study heterogeneity in this domain is explained almost entirely by two co-varying factors, both captured in the extraction table: (i) trial-era phototherapy intensity, and (ii) blinding/placebo control. Every positive trial (Rübo 1992, Voto 1995, Dağoğlu 1995, Elalfy 2010, Nasseri 2006) either predates intensive phototherapy as standard neonatal care, used an unblinded or no-placebo design, or both. Both null trials (Leiden, Brazil) are double-blind, placebo-controlled, and explicitly co-administered with modern-standard phototherapy. This is not a subtle confound — it is, per the brief's framing, very likely the central finding of Domain A rather than a footnote. A secondary contributor is control-arm exchange-transfusion rate: control rates run from 69% (Rübo, oldest trial) down to 15% (Leiden/Brazil, most recent trials), consistent with intensive phototherapy already having removed much of the exchange-transfusion burden that early IVIG trials were powered to detect.

## (c) Dose/timing used

Doses ranged from 0.5 g/kg to 1 g/kg as single-dose regimens (most trials), with one 3-day high-dose regimen (Voto, 800 mg/kg/day ×3) and one repeat-dose regimen (Nasseri, 0.5 g/kg every 12h ×3). Elalfy (2010, PMID 20924607) is the only identified head-to-head dose-comparison trial (0.5 vs 1 g/kg), finding the higher dose more effective at avoiding exchange transfusion, though both doses shortened phototherapy/admission similarly. Timing varied from within-6-hours prophylactic administration (Brazil trial, Leiden trial — both by protocol, irrespective of bilirubin trajectory) to rescue dosing triggered by a defined rate of bilirubin rise (Nasseri: ≥0.5 mg/dL/h). No trial in this domain directly randomised prophylactic-vs-rescue timing as its comparison; this question is addressed cross-sectionally in Domain D.

## (d) Where it failed or was null

The two most methodologically rigorous trials in this domain — Smits-Wintjens et al. 2011 (Leiden, PMID 21422084) and Santos et al. 2012 (Brazil, PMID 22882285) — are both null for the primary outcome of exchange transfusion, and null on every secondary outcome reported (phototherapy duration, peak bilirubin, length of stay). The Leiden trial's long-term follow-up (van Klink et al. 2015, PMID 26159803) found no difference in neurodevelopmental outcome at a median age of 4 years. Neither null trial found any adverse effect specifically attributable to IVIG, but neither was powered to detect a NEC signal (see Domain E).

## (e) Implication for the unit guideline

**GRADE: Low to Moderate certainty that prophylactic or early IVIG reduces exchange transfusion in term Rh-D disease when modern intensive phototherapy is already in use; the direction of the point estimate in the best-conducted trials is null, not merely imprecise.** Reason for downgrade: serious risk of bias across the positive trials (open-label, no placebo, mandated-early-exchange protocols) driving inconsistency; the two low-risk-of-bias trials are individually imprecise (small samples) but concordant with each other and with the risk-of-bias-stratified meta-analysis (Louis 2014) and the Cochrane sensitivity analysis (Zwiers 2018). Under intensive phototherapy — which is standard at both Imperial NICU sites — the trial evidence does not support a routine benefit of IVIG in term Rh-D disease. This does not mean IVIG has no role; it means the historical rationale (large effect sizes from the 1990s trials) does not survive contemporary trial conditions, and any guideline recommendation to use IVIG in Rh disease rests on guidance/consensus and biological plausibility rather than on the highest-quality trial evidence. No trial in this domain enrolled a preterm-only population or reported outcomes separately for infants who had received prior intrauterine transfusion versus those who had not (Smits-Wintjens stratified for IUT at randomisation but did not report an IUT-stratified subgroup result in the abstract) — **no direct evidence identified** for a preterm-specific or IUT-history-specific efficacy estimate; this is addressed further in Domain D/cross-cutting.
