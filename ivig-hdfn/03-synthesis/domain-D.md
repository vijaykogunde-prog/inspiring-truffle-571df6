# Domain D — Dose, timing, repeat dosing and thresholds

This domain is cross-sectional over the Domain A, B and C trial/case base rather than an independently searched evidence set. Domain B (`03-synthesis/domain-B.md`) had not yet been written when this file's PubMed search (D1, below) was first run; it became available before this file was finalised and is fully incorporated below. One targeted supplementary PubMed search was run specifically for direct trial evidence on prophylactic-vs-rescue timing or repeat-dose strategy; it found none. All dose/timing/repeat-dose/comparator data below are read directly from `02-extraction/extraction.csv` (rows A1–A7), `02-extraction/extraction-B.csv` (rows B1–B10) and `02-extraction/extraction-C.csv` (row C6), by their id, not re-described from the original trial reports.

Search: PubMed, 20 Sep 2026 (full string and screening detail in `01-search-log/searches-CD.md`, search D1).

## (a) What the evidence shows, and in whom

**No trial in the retrieved evidence base randomised or directly compared prophylactic-versus-rescue IVIG timing, or compared different repeat-dose strategies/intervals, as its primary research question.** The supplementary search for such a trial returned 9 records, of which 3 (PMID 26159803, 24514437, 21422084) are already extracted/synthesised in Domain A and 6 were off-topic on screening (adult autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, fetal/neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia, alloimmune neutropenia, and monoclonal-anti-D/Rh-antibody mechanism reviews — see the search log for the full exclusion list). What Domain D can answer, therefore, is only what can be read across the seven Rh trials Domain A already extracted (A1–A7), each of which used a fixed, single-arm-per-study dose/timing/repeat-dose regimen rather than randomising that variable, plus the one Domain C anecdote (C6) with an explicit repeat-dosing course.

**Dose (0.5 vs 1 g/kg):** the Domain A extraction table's `ivig_dose` column shows a spread from 500 mg/kg (A1 Rübo, A3 Dağoğlu, A7 Santos/Brazil) up to 0.75 g/kg (A6 Smits-Wintjens/Leiden) and 1 g/kg equivalents (A2 Voto's 800 mg/kg/day ×3 days is a cumulative 2.4 g/kg over the course), with A5 (Nasseri) using 0.5 g/kg every 12h ×3 doses (cumulative 1.5 g/kg). Domain B's ABO trials add a parallel spread: 500 mg/kg single dose (B1 Miqdad), 400 mg/kg/day ×2–3 days (B2 Huang), 0.5 g/kg every 12h ×3 doses (B3 Nasseri ABO subgroup — same protocol as A5, different antibody), and 1 g/kg single dose (B4 Alpay). The only trial that directly randomised *dose amount* as its comparison in either Rh or ABO disease is A4 (Elalfy 2010, PMID 20924607, 3-arm: 0.5 g/kg vs 1 g/kg vs conventional phototherapy alone), and per `domain-A.md` (c) it found the 1 g/kg arm numerically more effective at avoiding exchange transfusion than 0.5 g/kg (0% vs 5%, against 22% in the phototherapy-only comparator; p=0.03 across groups), with both doses shortening phototherapy/admission similarly. This single-trial signal is now corroborated, though not replaced, by a broader dose-comparison meta-analysis identified via Domain B's Consensus cross-check (Huang et al., not ABO-specific, 10 RCTs pooling both antibody types, n=690, PMID 42458120, per `domain-B.md` (c)): high-dose versus low-dose IVIG was associated with a lower exchange-transfusion rate (Peto OR 0.24, 95% CI 0.12–0.50) and shorter phototherapy/hospital stay — directionally consistent with Elalfy but drawn predominantly from the same high-risk-of-bias trial pool Domain B and Domain A both flag elsewhere. No dose-comparison trial exists for the Domain C antibody types (case reports report a single dose per patient, most commonly 1 g/kg where stated at all — see C12, PMID 1709770 — with no within-study comparison).

**Infusion duration:** reported in four rows across Domains A and B, all in the same narrow range: A1 (Rübo) 2 hours; A5/B3 (Nasseri, both the Rh and ABO subgroups of the same trial) 4 hours per dose; B4 (Alpay) 4 hours. The remaining rows (A2–A4, A6, A7, B1, B2, B5–B10) mark infusion duration "not stated in abstract." No Domain C case reports an infusion duration. Four consistent data points at 2–4 hours, from three separate trials, is the closest thing to a converging (if entirely non-comparative) signal in this domain — but it describes only what durations were *used* in these protocols, not that any duration was tested against another, so it cannot be read as evidence that a 2–4 hour infusion is superior to a faster or slower one. Any guideline duration would still have to rest primarily on product SPC guidance (grey literature, not evidence — see (e) below) and general neonatal infusion-tolerability practice, with this 2–4 hour range offered only as a description of what the included trials actually did.

**Prophylactic vs rescue timing:** answerable only descriptively, by reading each trial's own protocol timing off the extraction tables' `timing` columns, not from any trial that randomised timing itself. Domain A's `domain-A.md` (c) and Domain B's `domain-B.md` (c) both make this point explicitly for their respective antibody types: no trial in either domain directly randomised prophylactic-vs-rescue timing as its comparison. Reading the tables: A6 (Smits-Wintjens/Leiden) and A7 (Santos/Brazil) — the two modern, double-blind, placebo-controlled, null Rh trials — both used **prophylactic** dosing (A6: "given regardless of bilirubin trajectory, stratified by IUT status"; A7: "within the first 6 hours of life," irrespective of bilirubin trajectory). Every ABO trial and cohort in Domain B (B1, B3, B7, B10 explicitly; B9 as "early neonatal period") instead uses **rescue** timing, triggered by a bilirubin level or rate of rise approaching the exchange threshold — there is no prophylactic-timing ABO trial at all. A5 (Nasseri, Rh) also used explicit rescue dosing (≥0.5 mg/dL/h bilirubin-rise trigger). A1, A3, A4 (Rh) describe "early" or "early/rescue" timing not cleanly classifiable as pure prophylaxis or pure rescue. Because the two prophylactic-timing trials are also the two null, lowest-risk-of-bias Rh trials, and because timing co-varies with era, blinding and antibody type across both domains' datasets, it is not possible to attribute the Rh null result specifically to prophylactic timing as opposed to modern phototherapy intensity, blinding, or the fact that no rescue-timed trial has ever been run under equally rigorous conditions. **No direct evidence identified** that isolates timing (prophylactic vs rescue) as an independent variable, in either Rh or ABO disease.

## (b) Why results might differ between studies

The apparent "dose-response" signal in A4 (Elalfy) sits inside a single 3-arm trial with unclear blinding/allocation concealment (per its own extraction row) and a comparator arm not described as reflecting intensive multi-light phototherapy — the indirectness flag already recorded in A4's `key_limitation` field. The corroborating 10-RCT dose meta-analysis (PMID 42458120, cited in `domain-B.md`) does not resolve this, because — per Domain B's own framing — it too draws predominantly on the same high-risk-of-bias trial pool as the ABO efficacy literature generally; a meta-analysis of low-quality trials is not equivalent to one high-quality trial, and neither Rh nor ABO disease has a placebo-controlled, blinded dose-comparison trial of the kind that overturned the general-efficacy question in Domain A. Similarly, the repeat-dose regimens (A2's 3-daily-dose, A5/B3's 3-dose-every-12h, B2's 2–3 daily doses) and the single-dose regimens (A1, A3, A4, A6, A7, B1, B4, B7) were not compared head-to-head anywhere except B5 (Tanyer 2001, see (a) below); any apparent difference in effect between single-dose and repeat-dose trials elsewhere is confounded by era, blinding, antibody type and phototherapy intensity in exactly the pattern Domains A and B's (b) sections describe for the efficacy question generally, and the same caution applies here.

One further, genuinely comparative data point exists and was not covered above: **B5 (Tanyer 2001, PMID 11245352, mixed ABO/Rh, n=61)** is the only trial identified in Domains A–D that directly compares a *multiple-dose* IVIG regimen against a *single-dose* regimen against *no IVIG* within one protocol. Per `domain-B.md` (c), exchange transfusion occurred in 0% (multiple-dose arm), 12% (single-dose arm) and 33% (no-IVIG arm), with no RR/CI given in the abstract. This is the closest the entire evidence base comes to a direct repeat-dosing comparison, and it is suggestive of a dose-frequency response — but it is a 61-patient, unblinded, three-arm trial in a mixed antibody population, with no replication, and `domain-B.md` (d) notes it "does not resolve" the domain's null-vs-positive discordance. It is reported here, by its extraction-table id, as the single most relevant (and still weak) piece of evidence on repeat-dosing frequency.

## (d) Where it failed or was null

The two trials using prophylactic timing (A6, A7) are the same two trials that were null for exchange transfusion in Domain A — but as noted in (a), this cannot be disentangled from those trials' era and blinding advantages. No Rh trial using rescue timing (A5) or the less clearly classified "early" timing (A1, A3, A4) was null; all four were positive. Read uncritically, this could look like "prophylactic timing is associated with null results and rescue/early timing with positive results" — but this is exactly the same confound as Domain A's era-effect (the null trials are also the modern, blinded, intensive-phototherapy trials), and asserting a timing-specific causal claim from this pattern would be exactly the kind of indirect inference CLAUDE.md rule 5 requires flagging rather than asserting. The pattern does not hold for rescue-timed dosing generally, however: every ABO trial and cohort in Domain B uses rescue timing, and that domain's largest, most recent, non-randomised datasets (B7 Okulu, B8 Daunov, B9 Pan, B10 Al-Lawama) are themselves null-or-adverse despite being rescue-timed — so rescue timing is not itself protective against a null/adverse finding once trial rigour and dataset size increase. The honest statement, across both domains, is that timing, era, trial/study quality and antibody type are all confounded together in this dataset, and no trial exists anywhere in the evidence base that varies timing while holding the others constant.

## (e) Implication for the unit guideline

**GRADE for the dose-comparison question (0.5 vs 1 g/kg, or higher vs lower dose generally): Low certainty that higher-dose IVIG is more effective than lower-dose IVIG at avoiding exchange transfusion**, based on one 3-arm RCT in Rh disease (A4, Elalfy 2010: unclear blinding/allocation concealment, indirectness concern on the comparator arm, no replication) corroborated directionally, but not strengthened in certainty, by a 10-RCT dose meta-analysis spanning both antibody types (PMID 42458120, Peto OR 0.24, 95% CI 0.12–0.50) that Domain B itself notes is drawn predominantly from high-risk-of-bias trials. Downgraded for risk of bias and imprecision — one level rather than two because two independent analyses (one trial, one meta-analysis) point the same direction and the effect is biologically coherent with a dose-dependent Fc-receptor-blockade mechanism, to the extent that mechanism is accepted (see `00-mechanism.md`). This certainty rating applies to "higher dose is directionally better," not to a specific numeric threshold (e.g. exactly 1 g/kg vs exactly 0.5 g/kg) — no trial has tested the full range of doses used in practice (400 mg/kg/day to 1 g/kg single-dose to cumulative 2.4 g/kg over 3 days) against each other.

**GRADE for infusion duration: No direct evidence identified.** Only 4 rows across Domains A and B report a duration at all (2–4 hours: A1, A5/B3, B4), all descriptive of what was *used*, not compared against alternatives, and no data at all from Domain C. Any guideline infusion duration would have to rest on product SPC guidance for the specific IVIG product the unit stocks (grey literature, not trial evidence — to be captured in the guidance table, not asserted here) and/or on general neonatal infusion-tolerability practice, not on the trial base identified in this review; the 2–4 hour range used across three trials in two antibody types is offered only as a descriptive data point, not a recommendation.

**GRADE for prophylactic vs rescue timing: No direct evidence identified.** No trial (in Domains A, B, C or D's supplementary search) randomised timing as an independent variable; the apparent association between prophylactic timing and null results in Domain A's two best Rh trials is fully confounded with era and blinding and must not be read as a timing effect, and is further undermined as a generalisable "rescue timing works" inference by Domain B's finding that every rescue-timed ABO study (including its four largest, most recent, non-randomised cohorts) is itself null-or-adverse. A guideline recommendation on timing would have to rest on: (i) the practical observation that the two Rh trials most directly applicable to a modern UK NICU (A6, A7 — both explicitly using intensive/modern-era phototherapy) used prophylactic protocols and were null, which argues against a policy of *routine* prophylactic IVIG under modern phototherapy specifically because it was tested this way and failed to show benefit; (ii) the observation that rescue timing has *also* failed to show benefit in the largest modern ABO datasets (Domain B), so neither timing strategy has trial-quality support under modern conditions; and (iii) extrapolation from the general principle, untested here, that a rescue trigger tied to a defined bilirubin-rise rate (as in A5/B3: ≥0.5 mg/dL/h) at least targets treatment to infants demonstrating ongoing haemolysis — a plausibility argument, not a finding.

**GRADE for repeat-dose strategy and optimal interval: Very low certainty, verging on no direct evidence.** One trial (B5, Tanyer 2001) directly compares multiple-dose vs single-dose vs no-IVIG regimens and found a dose-frequency gradient (0% vs 12% vs 33% exchange transfusion) — but this is a single 61-patient, unblinded, mixed-antibody trial with no RR/CI reported and no replication, so "Very low certainty of a repeat-dosing advantage" is the most this evidence can support, not "no direct evidence identified" outright. No trial compared specific repeat-dosing *intervals* (e.g. every-12h vs every-24h vs every-48h) — for interval specifically, **no direct evidence identified** stands. The other data points bearing on repeat dosing are anecdotal: A5/B3 (Nasseri, 0.5 g/kg every 12h ×3 doses, in a positive but very-low-certainty trial with a late-anaemia signal — 11.8% vs 0%, p=0.48/NS) and Domain C's C6 (Gustavsen 2020, anti-Ku, PMID 31948214 — "repeated infusions" continued until haemoglobin normalised at 3 months, no interval specified). A repeat-dosing recommendation would have to rest on Tanyer's single suggestive trial, extrapolation from single-dose trial regimens generalised upward, and the product SPC's licensed dosing schedule — not from any rigorous comparative repeat-dose trial, because none was found. Given the Nasseri late-anaemia signal (non-significant, abstract-only, but consistent across both the Rh and ABO subgroups of the same trial), a guideline permitting repeat dosing should flag late anaemia as a specific outcome to monitor for, per CLAUDE.md rule 7's requirement that null/harm signals get equal billing.
