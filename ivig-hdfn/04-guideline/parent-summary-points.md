# Parent-facing summary points — IVIG for jaundice caused by blood group differences

For use as raw content only — to be adapted to the unit's locked parent-information-leaflet house style (NHS Identity design, reading age ≤11, PIF TICK/Crystal Mark standards) by the leaflet-production workflow. Not a finished leaflet.

1. Some babies develop yellow skin and eyes (jaundice) because their blood group is different from their mother's. The mother's body can make antibodies that cross to the baby before birth and break down some of the baby's red blood cells faster than normal. This is called haemolytic disease of the newborn.

2. The main treatment is light treatment (phototherapy), which helps the baby's body get rid of the yellow substance (bilirubin) that builds up when red blood cells break down.

3. If the jaundice is severe or getting worse quickly despite light treatment, doctors sometimes give a medicine called IVIG (intravenous immunoglobulin) — this is made from proteins found in donated blood plasma and is given through a drip.

4. The idea behind IVIG is that it might slow down the breakdown of the baby's red blood cells. However, the best and most carefully done research studies — done with proper comparison groups — have not shown that IVIG actually reduces the need for a bigger treatment called an exchange transfusion (see point 6).

5. Because of this newer evidence, doctors here will usually try to optimise light treatment fully first, and will only consider IVIG in specific situations — for example, if bilirubin levels are still rising close to the level where an exchange transfusion would be needed, or if there is likely to be a delay in arranging one.

6. If your baby does need an exchange transfusion (where some of the baby's blood is carefully swapped for donor blood), this will go ahead regardless of whether IVIG has already been given or not — IVIG is never used instead of an exchange transfusion when one is genuinely needed.

7. IVIG carries some possible risks. The main one being watched for is a rare bowel problem called necrotising enterocolitis (NEC). The research on whether IVIG actually causes this is uncertain, but because a link cannot be ruled out, your baby's feeding and tummy will be checked closely for the first day or two after any dose.

8. IVIG can occasionally cause a mild, usually temporary, breakdown of red blood cells of its own, separate from the original jaundice problem — this is why your baby will have a blood test before and after the medicine is given, to check for this.

9. Serious side effects from the infusion itself (such as a drop in blood pressure) appear to be uncommon in the studies looked at, but your baby will be closely watched throughout the drip.

10. Using IVIG for this reason is technically "off-label" — it is not the specific use the medicine was originally licensed for, though it is a recognised and accepted use in UK and international newborn care guidance. Your care team will explain this and answer any questions.

11. Research so far has not looked separately at every situation — for example, there is currently no good evidence either way for babies whose jaundice test (called a DAT or Coombs test) comes back negative despite a known blood-group mismatch, or for babies with rarer blood-group antibodies (such as anti-Kell). Your team will use their clinical judgement in these situations and can talk you through their reasoning.

12. If your baby has had this treatment (with or without IVIG), it's common for red blood cell counts to dip again in the weeks after going home — this is a feature of the underlying condition itself, not something IVIG causes or prevents. Your baby's team will arrange follow-up blood tests to check for this and can arrange treatment (including a top-up blood transfusion) if needed.
