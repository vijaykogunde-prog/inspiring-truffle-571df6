# Steroid injections before an early birth — information for parents

*Plain-English information to support a conversation with your team, not to replace it. Numbers are given as "out of 1000" so they're easier to compare. Based on the largest and most reliable studies available — full technical references are in the accompanying evidence synthesis.*

---

## What are these steroid injections for?

If your baby might be born early, your doctors may offer you an injection of a steroid medicine (either dexamethasone or betamethasone — they work in almost the same way). This is different from steroids used for other medical problems.

The steroid crosses to your baby and speeds up how ready their lungs are to breathe air. It's normally given as **two injections**, usually 24 hours apart (or sometimes as **four smaller injections 12 hours apart**, which works just as well and is the standard, most-studied way of giving one of the two drugs, dexamethasone) into your thigh or bottom.

## What difference does it make at my baby's stage of pregnancy?

For babies born preterm, out of every 1000 babies whose mothers had steroids compared with 1000 who didn't:
- About **23 fewer babies die** around the time of birth.
- About **43 fewer babies** develop breathing problems (respiratory distress syndrome).
- About **14 fewer babies** have bleeding in the brain (IVH).
- About **38 fewer children** have delays in their development.

These numbers are averaged across all preterm gestations studied together — the benefit is generally *larger* the earlier your baby is born, and smaller (though still real) the closer to term.

**If your baby is due in the late preterm period (34–37 weeks):** steroids reduce breathing problems needing help (about 28 fewer babies out of every 1000 needing this kind of support), but they also increase the chance of your baby's blood sugar being low just after birth (roughly 120 more babies out of every 1000 have a low blood sugar reading) — this is usually mild and settles within a day, and doesn't affect how your baby develops later.

## What if I only get one injection, not both?

**One injection is much better than none.** Studies consistently show that even a single dose, or a dose given very close to the time of birth, reduces risk compared with no steroids at all — the team will always try to give the first dose as soon as they think you might deliver early, even if they think there may not be time for a second. A complete course (both injections, ideally given more than a day before birth) works best, but a partial course is far from useless.

## Is there any difference between the two steroid drugs?

The two drugs used (dexamethasone and betamethasone) work about the same for the outcomes that matter most — the biggest study that compared them directly found no meaningful difference in the chance of your baby surviving without a significant disability. Your unit's choice of drug is not a sign that one is "better" — both are considered good options.

One thing is genuinely still uncertain: a possible (not proven) difference in the chance of cerebral palsy between the two drugs. The evidence so far is not strong enough either way to say there's a real difference, or to rule one out. If this matters to you, your team can talk through what is and isn't known.

## What if I had a mix of the two drugs, or an incomplete course?

Sometimes, because of how quickly things happen, you might end up with one dose of one drug and one dose of the other, or just a single dose of either. This is not a mistake, and it's not the same as getting no treatment — your baby will still have benefited from real, meaningful steroid exposure. Because this exact combination hasn't been specifically studied, your team can't say for certain it worked *exactly* as well as the standard two-dose course of a single drug — so it will be documented and discussed with you as a non-standard course, and monitored the same way an incomplete course would be.

## "If my baby doesn't end up being born early after all, could the steroids still affect them?"

This is one of the most common — and most difficult — questions, and we want to answer it honestly rather than just reassure you.

**What we know:** A very large study from Finland, following hundreds of thousands of children, found that children who were exposed to these steroids before birth but were then born at full term had a somewhat higher chance of being diagnosed with a mental health or developmental condition later in childhood, compared with children who weren't exposed. In real numbers: about **89 out of 1000** exposed children had such a diagnosis, compared with about **63 out of 1000** unexposed children — a difference of about 26 extra children per 1000.

**What we don't know — and this matters a great deal:** this kind of study cannot prove the steroids *caused* the difference. Women who are given these steroids are, by definition, women whose pregnancies looked risky enough at the time to justify treatment — threatened early labour, high blood pressure problems, or concerns about the placenta. It's entirely possible that it's the *underlying reason* the steroids were given — not the steroid itself — that explains part or all of the difference seen later. When the Finnish researchers compared brothers and sisters within the same family (a way of partly accounting for this), the difference became smaller, though it didn't disappear completely. And a separate large study, in Taiwan, looking at the same question, found **no difference at all** for babies born at full term. So the honest answer is: there may be a small increase in risk, there may be none, and if there is a real effect of the drug itself (rather than the reason it was given), it is probably smaller than the headline numbers suggest.

### If a parent asks: "Will this affect my baby's brain long-term?" — a suggested honest script

*"The best evidence we have — including a study that followed people for over 30 years after they'd had this treatment as unborn babies — hasn't found a difference in thinking, memory, or mental health in adulthood when the baby was actually born preterm, which is when we'd expect this treatment to be needed and used. There is a separate, harder question about babies who get the injection but then go on to be born at full term — some large studies have found a small increase in certain diagnoses in that group, but we can't be sure whether that's because of the injection itself or because of whatever was making your pregnancy look risky in the first place, which can affect a child's development on its own. We don't want to either dismiss this or overstate it. What we can say clearly is: for a baby who is genuinely born early, the evidence for benefit from this treatment — helping the lungs, reducing bleeding in the brain, improving the chance of a healthy outcome — is very strong and well established."*

## Repeat courses

If your pregnancy continues and you remain at high risk of an early birth more than a week after your first course, your team may discuss a second course. This can further reduce breathing problems, but it also causes your baby to be born slightly smaller (on average, around 100g lighter) — this size difference does not appear to affect long-term health based on the longest follow-up studies available (over 20 years in one study). The clearest caution is for babies who go on to be born at full term after a repeat course — this is the group in which any long-term downside, if it exists, has been seen most clearly, which is why repeat courses are limited to specific situations and a maximum of two courses.

## Questions to ask your team

- Which drug am I being given, and how many doses have I had so far?
- Given how soon I might deliver, is there still benefit in having (another) dose?
- Is there anything about my situation (like signs of infection) that changes the usual approach?
- What should I watch for in my baby after birth as a result of this treatment?
