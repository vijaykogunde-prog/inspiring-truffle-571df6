## Executive summary — headline findings

1. **The commissioning premise is inverted.** The internationally preferred dexamethasone regimen (WHO 2022, ACOG, and offered as a first option by RCOG GTG74) is 6 mg IM **every 12 hours for four doses** (24 mg total) — not 24-hourly. Betamethasone, not dexamethasone, is the drug conventionally given 24-hourly (2 × 12 mg), because only betamethasone's clinical preparation contains a slow-release (acetate) depot fraction. The unit's 12-hourly dexamethasone practice is standard care, not a deviation. *Certainty: High that this reflects current international guidance.*
2. **No trial has ever compared 12-hourly vs 24-hourly dexamethasone with neurodevelopmental follow-up.** The only randomised interval-comparison trial for either drug is a betamethasone trial (Khandelwal 2012, n=228) with no neurodevelopmental data at all.[4] This is a genuine evidence gap, not evidence of safety or of harm, and the two must not be conflated. *Certainty: absence-of-evidence, correctly labelled.*
3. **Dexamethasone vs betamethasone: no proven difference in the primary composite outcome, but cerebral palsy cannot be excluded as a risk.** The only adequately powered, blinded, long-term-follow-up head-to-head RCT (ASTEROID, n=1346/1509)[10] found death-or-neurosensory-disability at 2 years near-identical (adjRR 0.97, 95% CI 0.83–1.13). The pooled Cochrane cerebral palsy estimate is RR 2.50 (95% CI 0.97–6.39, 1 trial, low certainty) — compatible with both no difference and a 6-fold increase in risk with dexamethasone.[3,12] *Certainty: Moderate for the primary composite; Low, and genuinely unresolved, for cerebral palsy.*
4. **The dexamethasone-PVL concern rests on one 1999 retrospective cohort (Baud, n=883) and has not been confirmed by later, larger RCT-derived evidence** (Cochrane IVH aRR 0.71, 95% CI 0.28–1.81, low certainty) — but neither has it been excluded, given wide, low-certainty confidence intervals throughout.[18] *Certainty: Low; directionally consistent, statistically inconclusive.*
5. **Benefit from ACS-to-delivery interval rises continuously with time, with no identified threshold below which benefit disappears** — even intervals under 6 hours show measurable benefit in the largest cohort with granular timing data (NICHD extremely-preterm cohort, median interval 3.8h).[27] A single/partial dose is consistently better than no dose at all in every study identified, closing roughly half the excess risk seen with no ACS.[25] *Certainty: Moderate.*
6. **A single small RCT found equivalent RDS but higher NEC with 12-hourly vs 24-hourly betamethasone (unreplicated); the best RCT evidence on omitting a dose (BETADOSE, n=3244) could not demonstrate that a single dose is non-inferior to two doses.**[4,29] Neither trial answers the unit's specific ~1-hour-before-delivery scenario directly, but the closest available analogue (a small Spanish cohort with mean dose-to-delivery interval ≈1 hour, n=76) showed no harm and a possible benefit signal.[28] *Certainty: Low throughout this question.*
7. **ACS remains beneficial in the presence of chorioamnionitis** across every pooled analysis identified (mortality OR ~0.45–0.51, RDS OR ~0.53–0.59, IVH OR ~0.35–0.41), for both clinical and histological chorioamnionitis, though no RCT has ever enrolled women with active infection to test this directly — this is a genuine structural gap in the evidence, not a demonstrated absence of risk.[32,33] One small, wide-CI study found increased sepsis with "optimally timed" ACS specifically within a chorioamnionitis-positive cohort.[34] *Certainty: Low (all observational).*
8. **No direct evidence exists for mixed-agent (one dose betamethasone, one dose dexamethasone) courses anywhere in the literature.** Pharmacological reasoning (not trial evidence) suggests such a course is best treated administratively as non-standard/incomplete, because it does not reproduce either drug's tested pharmacokinetic profile.[Q5 pharmacology sources]
9. **Repeat-course harm (growth restriction, and a childhood-outcome signal) is concentrated specifically in babies who ended up delivered at term** — the MACS 5-year follow-up found no overall harm (OR 1.02) but a significant harm signal restricted to the ≥37-week subgroup (OR 1.69, 95% CI 1.04–2.77).[39,41]
10. **ACS exposure in pregnancies that ultimately reach term is associated with increased rates of mental/behavioural and neurodevelopmental diagnoses in Finnish national register data** (HR 1.47, attenuating to 1.38 in a sibling-discordant design) — **association, not proven causation**; a Taiwanese national cohort found no such signal in the full-term stratum specifically, illustrating genuine inconsistency in this literature that must be presented honestly to parents.[47,48,49]

## Q0 — Premise check (answered first, per brief)

### Bottom line

**The commissioning premise is inverted for dexamethasone.** The internationally preferred, most widely used and most extensively trial-tested dexamethasone regimen — endorsed by WHO (2022), ACOG, and offered as a first-listed option by RCOG Green-top Guideline No. 74 — is **dexamethasone sodium phosphate 6 mg intramuscularly every 12 hours for four doses (24 mg total)**.[1,2] A 24-hourly, two-dose dexamethasone regimen (12 mg × 2, mimicking betamethasone's schedule) exists as an *alternative* named in RCOG GTG74, on the basis of one trial showing similar efficacy to betamethasone when both drugs were given as two doses 24 hours apart[1] — but it is not the primary, most evidence-dense, or most widely used dexamethasone regimen anywhere. The large WHO ACTION-I RCT (>2800 women, low-resource settings) used the 12-hourly, four-dose dexamethasone regimen without a reported neurodevelopmental safety signal in its primary paper.[14] **The unit's 12-hourly dexamethasone practice is not a deviation from standard care; it is standard care**, and any downstream unit guideline must be written on that basis, not on the assumption that 12-hourly dosing is the aberration requiring correction.

Separately, and this is the more important finding for the neurodevelopmental-safety question actually being asked: **no direct comparative trial of dexamethasone at a 12-hourly versus 24-hourly interval, with matched total dose, has been identified.** The only randomised interval-comparison trial identified anywhere in this synthesis is for **betamethasone**, not dexamethasone (Khandelwal 2012, n=228)[4], and it did not measure neurodevelopmental outcome at all. This is confirmed authoritatively by the 2022 Cochrane review dedicated specifically to comparing different ACS regimens and preparations, which states plainly that only three studies compared any regimen variant, one of which was 12-hourly versus 24-hourly *betamethasone*, and that the certainty of evidence for all three regimen-comparison studies was **very low**.[3] There is therefore no trial evidence — for either agent — linking dosing interval per se to neurodevelopmental outcome. This is a genuine evidence gap, not a demonstrated safety signal, and the two must not be conflated in the guideline.

### Regimens as specified by each source

**Is a "dexamethasone 24-hourly" regimen recommended anywhere as the primary/preferred option?** Only as an RCOG-listed alternative — and this is the exact regimen ASTEROID tested against betamethasone, so it has strong direct-comparison evidence for short-term and 2-year composite outcomes[10] — not as a WHO- or ACOG-preferred regimen. No guideline identified in this search recommends 24-hourly dexamethasone as the *sole* or *first-line* regimen.

### Does dosing interval affect neurodevelopmental outcome? — searched directly

No direct evidence was identified for a 12-hourly vs 24-hourly *dexamethasone* comparison of any outcome, neurodevelopmental or otherwise. For betamethasone, one small single-centre RCT (Khandelwal 2012, n=228 mothers/260 fetuses) found RDS incidence equivalent between 12-hourly and 24-hourly dosing (36.5% vs 37.3%, not significant) but a higher rate of necrotising enterocolitis with the 12-hourly interval (6.2% vs 0%, P=.03) — an unreplicated finding from a small, open-label, single-centre trial, with **no neurodevelopmental follow-up reported at all**.[4] Two subsequent, smaller retrospective cohorts (Bulut 2021, n=423; Saldaña-García 2022, n=275) found **no significant differences** in mortality, severe morbidity, cerebral palsy or sensory deficits by interval, and Saldaña-García — the only study with neurodevelopmental follow-up (ASQ-3 at 24 months) for any interval comparison — found a **non-significant trend toward better scores with the 12-hourly regimen**.[20,21] The 2022 Cochrane review formally concludes: "the certainty of the evidence for the main outcomes from all three [regimen] studies was very low... does not support the use of one particular corticosteroid regimen over another."[3]

**What this means for the guideline (Q0):**
- State explicitly, at the top of the unit guideline, that 12-hourly dexamethasone (6 mg × 4) is the internationally preferred, most trial-tested regimen — not a deviation requiring correction. *Certainty: High that this reflects current WHO/ACOG guidance and is an RCOG-sanctioned option.*
- Do **not** frame the unit's dosing interval as a safety problem to be fixed. There is no trial evidence — for dexamethasone specifically — that either interval carries different neurodevelopmental risk, and the limited betamethasone-interval evidence with neurodevelopmental follow-up is reassuring in direction (though very low certainty). *Certainty: absence-of-evidence, correctly labelled — not the same as proven equivalence.*
- If any change to unit practice is contemplated on dosing-interval grounds, it should be prompted by the (currently unreplicated, betamethasone-specific) NEC signal from Khandelwal 2012, not by neurodevelopmental concern. *Certainty: Very low.*

**What we do not know (Q0):**
- Whether 12-hourly vs 24-hourly dexamethasone, at matched total dose, differs in *any* neonatal or neurodevelopmental outcome — this has simply not been tested in a dedicated trial.
- Whether the Khandelwal NEC signal (betamethasone-specific) is real or a chance finding — it has not been replicated in a larger study.
- The exact evidence basis RCOG used to judge the 24-hourly two-dose dexamethasone alternative as having "similar efficacy" to betamethasone — plausibly this is exactly the ASTEROID trial[10] given the matching regimen, but this specific attribution could not be confirmed against the primary GTG74 text in this session (network access blocked) and should be checked before the guideline is finalised.

## Q1 — Dexamethasone dosing regimen and interval

### Bottom line

Dosing-interval evidence for dexamethasone specifically does not exist. What exists is (a) a small, unreplicated betamethasone interval RCT with a NEC signal and no neurodevelopmental data,[4] (b) two reassuring but very-low-certainty retrospective betamethasone-interval cohorts, one with 24-month neurodevelopmental follow-up,[20,21] (c) mechanistic/animal PK evidence that *duration* of fetal exposure, not interval or peak concentration per se, drives the lung-maturation effect,[Kemp 2020, Takahashi 2022] and (d) a genuinely unresolved, decades-old sulfite/PVL literature that is mechanistically credible but not confirmed as a proven human antenatal harm.

### Direct clinical evidence on interval

Three studies compared 12-hourly vs 24-hourly dosing with matched total dose, **all in betamethasone, not dexamethasone**: Khandelwal 2012 (PMID 22381601, n=228) — the only RCT — found equivalent RDS (36.5% vs 37.3%) but significantly more NEC with 12-hourly dosing (6.2% vs 0%, P=.03; very few events, unreplicated, no neurodevelopmental data).[4] Bulut 2021 (PMID 34544193, retrospective, n=423) and Saldaña-García 2022 (PMID 35268318, retrospective, n=275, with 24-month ASQ-3 neurodevelopmental follow-up) found no significant differences in mortality, severe morbidity, cerebral palsy or sensory deficits; Saldaña-García reported a non-significant trend toward *better* developmental scores with the 12-hourly regimen.[20,21] The 2022 Cochrane regimen-comparison review formally concludes this body of evidence is very-low-certainty and does not support preferring one regimen over another.[3] **No direct comparative evidence was identified for 12-hourly vs 24-hourly dexamethasone specifically, with or without long-term neurodevelopmental outcome.**

Internationally (ACOG, WHO), the standard/most commonly used dexamethasone regimen is **four 6 mg IM doses given 12-hourly**, not 24-hourly — betamethasone is the drug conventionally given 24-hourly. The WHO ACTION-III PK-PD protocol paper explicitly describes "dexamethasone phosphate 6mg every 12 hourly for a maximum of four doses" as "the currently recommended dose" for early preterm birth, and the WHO ACTION-I RCT used this regimen in >2800 women without a reported neurodevelopmental safety signal.[14]

### Indirect pharmacokinetic reasoning — clearly separated from clinical trial evidence

No study directly linking peak fetal glucocorticoid concentration to worse neurodevelopmental outcome in humans was identified. What exists is mechanistic/animal literature: a minimum effective fetal exposure for lung maturation has been modelled across sheep, monkey and human cord-blood data at ~1 ng/mL sustained for 48 hours — far below what current clinical dosing (either interval) achieves — suggesting current regimens likely substantially exceed the minimum needed exposure.[5] Sheep studies show the **duration** and continuity of fetal exposure, not the interval between doses per se, determines durability of the lung-maturation effect, and that interrupting exposure below a threshold (rather than shortening the interval) causes loss of benefit.[Kemp 2020 PMID 31494126; Takahashi 2022 PMID 35380907] General mechanistic reviews describe fetal glucocorticoid programming of the HPA axis and brain as a function of total/cumulative exposure.[Moisiadis & Matthews 2014, PMID 24863382/24863383] **This entire line of reasoning is pharmacological inference, not clinical trial evidence.** Mechanistically, a 12-hourly regimen with matched total dose is expected to produce a modestly higher, earlier peak and less trough decay than 24-hourly dosing, but no retrieved study quantifies this difference in humans or links it to a specific neurodevelopmental endpoint.

### Sulfite preservative concern — origin and status

The concern originates from Baud and colleagues: two mouse/cell-culture studies showed that a *sulfite-containing* commercial dexamethasone preparation ("Soludecadron") and sodium metabisulfite itself — not pure dexamethasone — increased neuronal death in vitro and in vivo, while pure dexamethasone and pure/commercial betamethasone (Celestone) did not.[22,23] A 2005 French-language review by the same group extended this into a hypothesis specifically about **postnatal** (not antenatal) dexamethasone use in preterm infants — this postnatal-harm literature (DART and related) must not be imported into antenatal conclusions, per the brief's own instruction. This is **substantiated at the mechanistic/animal level but not established as a proven human antenatal neurotoxicity mechanism** — no clinical antenatal trial in the material retrieved directly attributes a neurodevelopmental difference to sulfite content per se, as opposed to the drug (dexamethasone vs betamethasone) itself. Based on WebSearch summaries of UK emc listings (not independently verified by direct SPC fetch — network access was blocked), current UK injectable dexamethasone sodium phosphate products may also contain a sulfite preservative, and UK injectable betamethasone (Betnesol) is reported to contain sodium metabisulphite as well — **this finding must be independently confirmed against the current BNF/SPC before drawing clinical conclusions**, since the primary product documents could not be fetched in this session.

### Periventricular leukomalacia (PVL) signal

The primary source is Baud et al., *NEJM* 1999 (PMID 10519896): a retrospective cohort of 883 infants (24–31 weeks) across three Paris centres, 1993–1996 (betamethasone n=361, dexamethasone n=165, no steroid n=357). Adjusted cystic PVL rates: betamethasone 4.4%, dexamethasone 11.0%, none 8.4%; adjusted OR betamethasone-vs-dexamethasone 0.3 (95% CI 0.1–0.7); dexamethasone-vs-none was **not significant** (aOR 1.5, 95% CI 0.8–2.9).[18] Larger, later RCT-derived evidence has **not clearly replicated a dexamethasone-specific IVH/PVL or cerebral-palsy signal at statistical significance**: the 2022 Cochrane review (9 trials, 2319 infants) found IVH risk with dexamethasone vs betamethasone compatible with no difference (aRR 0.71, 95% CI 0.28–1.81, low certainty, substantial heterogeneity), 2-year neurodevelopmental disability RR 1.02 (0.85–1.22, moderate certainty), and cerebral palsy RR 2.50 (0.97–6.39, low certainty, single trial — CI crossing 1, compatible with both large harm and no effect).[3,12] Older, non-randomised NICHD cohorts (Lee 2006, 2008) found trends toward worse neurological outcomes with dexamethasone vs betamethasone, but used a fractionated 4-dose 12-hourly US dexamethasone regimen against a 2-dose betamethasone regimen, confounding drug choice with regimen.[16,17] **The belief rests substantially on a single moderate-sized retrospective cohort from the late 1990s; the larger, later RCT evidence synthesised by Cochrane does not confirm a clear dexamethasone-vs-betamethasone difference in IVH or long-term neurodevelopmental disability, though it also cannot exclude one given wide, low-certainty confidence intervals** — this is an open, unresolved question, not a settled refutation, and not a settled confirmation either.

**What this means for the guideline (Q1):**
- Do not present 12-hourly dexamethasone dosing as carrying a demonstrated neurodevelopmental risk — no such evidence exists for dexamethasone specifically. *Certainty: absence-of-evidence.*
- Flag the sulfite-preservative question as mechanistically plausible but clinically unconfirmed in humans antenatally, and recommend the unit independently check current UK product SPCs for both agents' preservative content before using this as a basis for agent selection. *Certainty: Very low (animal/in vitro only).*
- Present the dexamethasone-PVL/cerebral-palsy question honestly as unresolved — not refuted, not confirmed — rather than either dismissing Baud 1999 or treating it as settled. *Certainty: Low.*

**What we do not know (Q1):**
- Whether 12-hourly vs 24-hourly dexamethasone differs in any outcome — untested.
- Whether current UK dexamethasone and betamethasone preparations differ meaningfully in sulfite content — unconfirmed against primary SPC in this session.
- Whether the cerebral palsy signal (RR 2.50, CI 0.97–6.39) reflects a true drug effect, chance, or residual confounding in the underlying trial data — a single trial with a wide CI cannot resolve this.

## Q2 — Compressed/accelerated courses when delivery is imminent

### Bottom line

Benefit from ACS rises with treatment-to-delivery interval in a graded, continuous fashion with no identified cliff-edge below which benefit vanishes; a single/partial dose is consistently better than none; and the one RCT testing whether a second dose can safely be omitted (BETADOSE) could not prove omission was safe to do routinely.

### Shape of the treatment-to-delivery interval–outcome relationship

The Cochrane review itself does not provide a validated interval-stratified subgroup analysis, so it cannot answer the shape question on its own.[9] Dedicated interval studies show a continuous relationship with real disagreement about the shortest effective window. Melamed's Canadian cohort of 6870 infants found maximum benefit at 1–7 days, but a **<24h ("partial") interval still nearly halved the excess risk** seen with no ACS at all relative to the same reference group (composite-outcome aOR 2.12 with no ACS vs 1.48 with partial <24h ACS).[25] Norman's EPICE cohort of 4594 European infants found mortality declined rapidly with ACS, plateauing at >50% risk reduction by 18–36h, with brain-injury benefit requiring >48h and benefit waning after a week; a simulation suggested a dose given 3h before delivery could still reduce mortality by ~26% — **this is a modelled/simulated estimate, not a directly observed comparison, and should be labelled as such**.[26] The WHO ACTION-I secondary analysis (2638 women, 5 low-resource countries) found peak mortality benefit at days 13–14 and, for the first 24 hours specifically, relative risks "not indicative of any clinical benefit or suggestive of harm" — genuinely uncertain (wide CIs) rather than a demonstrated null effect.[24] The NICHD extremely-preterm cohort (Chawla 2025, n=1806, median interval only 3.8h, IQR 1.4–9.5h) found a **continuous, monotonic** benefit even within this very short window (survival-without-severe-morbidity aRR 1.09 per additional 6h).[27] The 2023 WHO-commissioned systematic review (10 RCTs + 45 cohorts + 2 case-control studies, 37 different interval definitions) concluded that heterogeneity across studies precludes identifying one "optimal" interval.[Q2 agent, McDougall 2023] **Net picture: benefit rises with time-to-delivery in a graded fashion; some studies show measurable benefit even <6–12h; none show a clean cutoff below which ACS confers zero benefit.**

### Minimum useful interval / pharmacological basis

This is genuinely indirect/mechanistic evidence. Sheep studies show lung-maturation efficacy depends on the *duration* of a threshold fetal plasma betamethasone concentration (~1–2 ng/mL) being maintained, not on peak concentration or total dose, and that 12h exposure produced little maturation whereas 26–48h did.[Kemp 2018 PMID 29758177; Fee 2023 PMID 37697929] These are **preclinical animal data, not direct human evidence** about a dose given close to delivery. No human PK study was identified directly measuring time-to-peak fetal glucocorticoid concentration after a single maternal IM dose in relation to a very short delivery window. **No direct clinical evidence was identified for a mechanistic benefit specifically within 1 hour of dosing** — this is inference, not demonstrated fact.

### Harm from a dose very close to delivery

No RCT or cohort specifically isolates harm from a dose given very shortly before delivery. ALPS and related literature report increased neonatal hypoglycaemia with a **completed** late-preterm course (24% vs 15%),[45] but a meta-analysis of 14 studies found no significant overall effect of ACS on cortisol or glucose (hypoglycaemia OR 1.46, 95% CI 0.99–2.17, NS).[Q2 agent, Bharadwaj 2024] A single-centre Chinese cohort found hypoglycaemia was most common after a **complete** course (16.4%) versus partial (3.6%) or none (6.5%), with any glucose-lowering effect confined to the first 12h and resolved by 24h[Zhou 2022, PMID 36440338] — at least consistent with adrenal/metabolic effects requiring more cumulative exposure than a single near-delivery dose would provide, though this does not directly test that scenario. **No direct evidence was identified of harm — maternal or neonatal — specifically attributable to an ACS dose given approximately 1 hour before delivery.**

### Accelerated/compressed regimens and the value of the second dose

A genuine 12h-apart 2-dose betamethasone regimen was studied retrospectively (Saldaña-García 2022, n=275): no difference in mortality or severe morbidity, **less** first-week hypotension with the 12h group (p=0.04), non-significant trends toward lower FiO2/shorter ventilation, and no adverse 24-month neurodevelopmental signal.[21,30] This is hypothesis-generating only, but shows no harm signal from compressing to 12h. The BETADOSE RCT (Schmitz 2022, Lancet, n=3244, 37 French level-3 centres) directly tested **omitting** the second dose (half-dose vs standard 24h-apart full-dose): surfactant need 20.0% (half-dose) vs 17.5% (full-dose), risk difference 2.4% (95% CI −0.3 to 5.2) — **non-inferiority of omitting the second dose was NOT demonstrated**.[29] A post hoc analysis found similar survival-without-severe-morbidity regardless of dose number (66.5% vs 65.8%), but was not powered for this outcome.[Baud 2024, PMID 38341166] **No trial was identified that randomised a 12h-apart interval specifically in an imminent-delivery context.**

### Partial/incomplete course vs none, in absolute terms

Melamed 2015 (n=6870) gives the clearest gestational-stratum-free absolute comparison: relative to the ACS-1-to-7-days reference group, the no-ACS group had adjusted OR 2.56 (95% CI 1.83–3.59) for neonatal death, while the partial-ACS-<24h group had adjusted OR 1.59 (95% CI 1.16–2.18) — a single/partial dose closes roughly half the gap versus no treatment.[25] Costa 2006 (n=411) found single-dose betamethasone conferred benefit specifically at 25–27 weeks but no discernible benefit at 32–34 weeks — **benefit of a partial course is gestational-age-dependent, greatest at the most immature end**.[Q2 agent, Costa 2006 PMID 16797825] Xodo 2022 (n=1543) found a complete course outperformed a 12mg partial course on IVH grade 3–4 (7.81% vs 3.74%) and PVL (7.29% vs 1.99%), though this compares partial-vs-complete rather than partial-vs-none.[31] Saldaña-García's small single-dose-≤3h cohort (n=76, mean dose-to-delivery interval ≈1 hour) found serious-outcome OR 0.2 (95% CI 0.07–0.9) for a single dose vs none.[28] **Bottom line: a single/partial dose is consistently better than none across every study identified, though generally inferior to a properly-timed complete course, with the largest relative benefit seen at the earliest gestations.**

### Guideline positions (flagged as WebSearch-summary, not independently confirmed against primary text)

RCOG GTG74 is reported (via secondary summary) to state that for imminent/precipitous delivery "at least one dose... is recommended, even if completion of the course is not possible," and to cite a single dose up to 3h antepartum as potentially reducing RDS severity (consistent with, and likely citing, the Saldaña-García ~1h cohort[28]). A separate WebSearch-derived claim attributes to RCOG a post hoc analysis of 206 infants delivered 1–24h after dosing in which an additional dose "tended to increase RDS risk and reduce intact survival" — **this specific claim could not be independently verified against primary text in this session and is listed in "Could not verify" below; treat with caution until confirmed.** WHO's 2022 update is reported to state "although 1–7 days... seems beneficial, there could be some benefit within the first 24h, and that benefit might continue after 7 days" — consistent with the WHO ACTION-I secondary analysis.[24] **No additional benefit has been demonstrated for accelerated dosing with intervals shorter than the standard regimens, even when delivery appears imminent**, per ACOG's WebSearch-summarised position — but "no additional benefit demonstrated" is an absence-of-evidence statement, not a demonstrated-no-benefit one, and should be presented as such.

**What this means for the guideline (Q2):**
- Give the first dose regardless of how imminent delivery appears — every study identified shows a partial dose is better than none, with the largest relative benefit at the earliest gestations. *Certainty: Moderate.*
- Do not assume a second dose given very close to delivery is either clearly beneficial or clearly harmful — direct evidence for that specific scenario does not exist. *Certainty: absence-of-evidence, both directions.*
- The best RCT evidence on omitting a second dose (BETADOSE) leans toward keeping it where feasible — non-inferiority of a single dose was not shown. *Certainty: Moderate-high.*

**What we do not know (Q2):**
- Direct outcome data for a dose given ~1 hour before delivery specifically — the closest analogue (Saldaña-García, n=76) is small, retrospective, and not a randomised comparison.
- Whether the RCOG "206-infant" post hoc analysis exists as described — unverified this session.
- Whether accelerating to a 12h-apart 2-dose regimen in an imminent-delivery context (rather than the standard schedule) changes outcome — no trial has tested this directly.

## Q3 — Antenatal corticosteroids in the presence of chorioamnionitis

### Bottom line

Every dataset identified shows ACS associated with lower rates of RDS, IVH/severe IVH and mortality in chorioamnionitis-affected pregnancies, with effect sizes numerically similar to the general ACS effect — but no RCT has ever enrolled women with active chorioamnionitis to test this directly, so all of this evidence is observational and Low certainty.

### Efficacy and sepsis outcomes

Been 2011 (meta-analysis of 7 observational studies) found, in histological chorioamnionitis (HCA), ACS associated with reduced mortality (OR 0.45, 95% CI 0.30–0.68), RDS (OR 0.53, 0.40–0.71), PDA (OR 0.56, 0.37–0.85), IVH (OR 0.35, 0.18–0.66) and severe IVH (OR 0.39, 0.19–0.82); in clinical chorioamnionitis, reduced severe IVH (OR 0.29, 0.10–0.89) and PVL (OR 0.35, 0.14–0.85).[32] Amiya 2016 (9 studies pooled) corroborates: mortality OR 0.49 (0.34–0.73), RDS OR 0.58 (0.44–0.76), IVH OR 0.41 (0.24–0.69).[33] For **neonatal sepsis specifically**, the picture is mixed. Elimian 2000 found no increase in proven sepsis with ACS in HCA-positive infants (18.3% vs 14%, P=.24, NS). In contrast, Ryu 2019 found "optimally timed" ACS (2–7 days pre-delivery) in HCA-positive infants associated with **increased** sepsis: aOR 6.85 (95% CI 1.02–46.07) — a wide, barely-significant interval from a small single-centre cohort, and the only study to report this specific signal.[34] No pooled meta-analysis reported a chorioamnionitis-specific sepsis effect estimate; the Cochrane review's general "systemic infection in first 48h of life" outcome (RR 0.60, 95% CI 0.41–0.88) is drawn from the whole trial population, not a chorioamnionitis subgroup.

### Clinical vs histological chorioamnionitis vs funisitis

The literature draws a real, if imperfect, distinction between clinical and histological chorioamnionitis: histological chorioamnionitis has the larger, more consistent evidence base; a narrative review states plainly that the RDS-reduction benefit is seen with histological but "not clinical" chorioamnionitis.[Thomas & Speer 2010, PMID 20881433] **Funisitis was not identified as a separately-analysed exposure in any retrieved study** — it is uniformly subsumed within "histological chorioamnionitis" cohorts. This confirms the pooling problem the brief anticipated: no study stratified ACS effect by funisitis status independent of chorioamnionitis.

### Evidence of harm

Maternally, the only RCT-based evidence (the general Cochrane review population, not a chorioamnionitis-specific enrolment) found ACS does **not** increase maternal chorioamnionitis (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.69–1.08) or endometritis (RR 1.14, 0.82–1.58).[9] No RCT has enrolled women with *active* chorioamnionitis to directly test harm in that subgroup — a structural evidence gap, not a negative finding. Neonatally, the clearest (though weak) harm signal is Ryu 2019's sepsis association above.[34] Separately, the Lee 2004 RCT found **repeated (weekly) ACS dosing itself increased chorioamnionitis** (49.4% vs 31.7% single-course, P=.04) — relevant to repeat-dosing strategy generally, not a baseline-infection interaction.[Q3 agent, Lee 2004 PMID 14754695]

### Guideline positions (flagged as WebSearch-summary where the primary document could not be fetched)

**RCOG GTG74** is reported by secondary sources to state that where there is clinical chorioamnionitis, a course of ACS "may be started but should not delay delivery if indicated by maternal or fetal condition," with senior-opinion input suggested before delaying delivery for steroid completion — positioning infection as a **caution/timing consideration, not an absolute contraindication**. **NICE NG25**: no explicit chorioamnionitis-specific ACS recommendation could be located via search — this should be treated as unconfirmed absence, not a verified "neither" position. **WHO** lists absence of clinical maternal infection as one of five explicit eligibility preconditions for ACS use at all, reiterated unchanged in the November 2022 update, and secondary sources describe WHO as stating ACS is "not recommended for women with chorioamnionitis who are likely to give birth preterm," prioritising delivery over completing the steroid course — the most restrictive of the three positions, functionally closer to a **caution against use pending delivery** though not framed as an absolute contraindication given the same graded (low-certainty) benefit evidence is acknowledged in WHO's own evidence profile.

### Does chorioamnionitis change the calculus for a second dose given earlier?

No study or guideline statement directly addresses this. The one RCT of rescue/repeat dosing (Garite 2009, n=437) explicitly **excluded** chorioamnionitis from eligibility, so no trial has generated data on this specific question — a genuine, structural evidence gap.[35]

**What this means for the guideline (Q3):**
- Chorioamnionitis (clinical or histological) should not by itself withhold ACS if delivery is not imminent and the woman is otherwise a candidate — the observational evidence consistently favours benefit. *Certainty: Low (all observational; no RCT has directly tested this population).*
- Do not delay delivery to complete an ACS course when chorioamnionitis is driving the clinical picture — timing/delivery decisions take priority over steroid completion, consistent with all three guideline positions reviewed. *Certainty: guideline-consensus, not RCT-derived.*
- Flag the Ryu 2019 sepsis signal (aOR 6.85, wide CI) as a reason for vigilant neonatal sepsis surveillance after ACS in chorioamnionitis-affected pregnancies, without treating it as a reason to withhold ACS. *Certainty: Low, single small study.*

**What we do not know (Q3):**
- Direct RCT evidence of ACS efficacy/safety specifically in chorioamnionitis-affected pregnancies — does not exist and, given the ethics of withholding ACS from a group already known to benefit generally, is unlikely ever to be generated by a placebo-controlled design.
- Whether chorioamnionitis should change second-dose timing — genuine, unaddressed gap.
- Funisitis-specific outcome data, independent of chorioamnionitis — not identified anywhere in this search.

## Q4 — Dexamethasone versus betamethasone

### Bottom line

The best evidence (ASTEROID, the only adequately powered blinded RCT with long-term follow-up) shows no meaningful difference in the primary composite outcome, within a moderately tight confidence interval. For individual short-term outcomes, most comparisons are imprecise or high-certainty-null. The one outcome that cannot be dismissed is cerebral palsy, where the confidence interval remains compatible with an important increase in risk with dexamethasone.

### The head-to-head evidence

ASTEROID (Crowther et al 2019, *Lancet Child Adolesc Health*, PMID 31523039; Australia/NZ, 14 hospitals, 1346 women/1509 fetuses, <34 weeks) directly randomised dexamethasone Na phosphate 12 mg IM ×2 (24h apart) against betamethasone (Celestone Chronodose) 11.4 mg IM ×2 (24h apart) — i.e., the RCOG-listed "alternative" 24-hourly dexamethasone regimen, not the WHO/ACOG-preferred 12-hourly regimen.[10] Primary outcome (death or neurosensory disability at 2 years): dexamethasone 33% vs betamethasone 32%, **adjRR 0.97, 95% CI 0.83–1.13, p=0.66** — an interval excluding any relative difference larger than a 17% reduction or a 13% increase in risk with dexamethasone, supporting genuine clinical equivalence for this composite outcome (Moderate certainty: single trial, ~79% outcome ascertainment, no independent RCT replication). A repeat-dose secondary analysis of the same trial (n=168) found no difference (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.39–2.06).[11]

For individual short-term outcomes, the updated Cochrane review (largely driven by ASTEROID's data, 9 trials, 2096 women/2319 infants) found RDS risk essentially unchanged (RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.91–1.22, **High certainty** — a fairly narrow band excluding large differences either way), neonatal death imprecise (RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.66–1.63, Moderate, genuinely uninformative), IVH with unexplained heterogeneity (RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.28–1.81, I²=62%, Low), and NEC with almost no events (RR 5.08, 95% CI 0.25–105.15, Low).[3,12] A Bayesian network meta-analysis (Ciapponi 2021, 45 trials, 11,227 women/11,878 infants) corroborates no meaningful difference for neonatal death (OR 1.05, 95% CI 0.62–1.84) or IVH (OR 1.04, 0.56–1.78), both Moderate/Low certainty.[13]

**The one signal that cannot be dismissed is cerebral palsy.** ASTEROID/Cochrane report RR 2.50, 95% CI 0.97–6.39 (1 trial, 1223 children, **Low certainty**) — an interval compatible with both no difference and a 6-fold increase in risk with dexamethasone.[3,12] This is underpowered, not statistically significant, and based on a single trial, but the point estimate, together with the older non-randomised Baud 1999 cystic-PVL cohort (adjusted OR betamethasone-vs-dexamethasone 0.3, 95% CI 0.1–0.7)[18] and the Lee 2006/2008 NICHD cohorts (trend to increased neonatal death, PDI<70, and hearing impairment with dexamethasone vs betamethasone),[16,17] forms a directionally consistent but statistically inconclusive body of observational and underpowered RCT evidence suggesting a possible — not proven — neurological disadvantage for dexamethasone. Feldman 2007 (lower RDS/BPD with betamethasone) and Bar-Lev 2004 (no difference) are discordant, non-randomised, and confounded by treatment era.[Q4 agent] **Absence of proof of harm is not proof of absence: the guideline should flag cerebral palsy as an outcome where meaningful harm from dexamethasone has not been excluded (Low certainty), even though the primary composite 2-year outcome shows no difference (Moderate certainty).**

### Maternal outcomes and practical considerations

Chorioamnionitis trended lower with dexamethasone (RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.48–1.06, Moderate, 1 trial) and maternal adverse effects trended lower with dexamethasone (RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.35–1.13, Moderate, 2 trials), neither reaching significance.[3] ASTEROID found less injection-site discomfort with dexamethasone (1% vs 3%, p=0.02).[10] In matched regimens (both drugs 2 injections 24h apart, as in ASTEROID and UK practice), the number of injections is not a genuine differentiator; much of the older observational literature used a 4-dose 12-hourly dexamethasone regimen against a 2-dose betamethasone regimen, meaning some historical "dexamethasone is worse" signal may reflect regimen fractionation rather than drug choice.[16,17] Both agents cause substantial transient maternal hyperglycaemia; a small Malaysian RCT in gestational-diabetic women found dexamethasone produced fewer hyperglycaemic episodes than betamethasone on days 2–3, but this is a single small trial with a surrogate endpoint (Low certainty).[Q4 agent, Shareef 2025 PMID 40473019] **Cost and supply**: WHO chose dexamethasone for ACTION-I specifically because it is cheaper, more heat-stable, and more reliably available in low-resource settings — agent choice in LMIC settings is explicitly **supply-driven, not efficacy-driven**, a materially different rationale from a UK Level 3 unit where both drugs are reliably available.

**Critical distinction for this guideline**: WHO ACTION-I is dexamethasone **vs placebo** in low-resource settings — it demonstrates dexamethasone's efficacy over no treatment there, and says nothing about dexamethasone vs betamethasone; it should not be cited as head-to-head agent-comparison evidence, and its low-resource control-arm mortality is not applicable to a UK Level 3 unit.[14]

**GRADE summary**: Overall survival-without-disability equivalence — **Moderate**. RDS equivalence — **High**. Neonatal death, IVH, NEC — **Low-Moderate** (imprecision, heterogeneity, few events). Cerebral palsy — **Low**, and specifically **cannot exclude clinically important harm with dexamethasone**. Maternal chorioamnionitis/side-effects — **Moderate**, favourable trend to dexamethasone, not significant.

**What this means for the guideline (Q4):**
- Either agent is an acceptable first-line choice; the evidence does not establish superiority of one over the other for the primary composite outcome. *Certainty: Moderate.*
- The guideline should not claim the two agents are proven equally safe for cerebral palsy risk — say explicitly that this specific outcome remains statistically unresolved. *Certainty: Low.*
- Agent choice may reasonably be guided by local formulary, UK preparation availability (given RCOG's specific caveat about betamethasone-phosphate-alone pharmacokinetics), and practical factors rather than proven differential efficacy/safety. *Certainty: guideline-consensus.*

**What we do not know (Q4):**
- Whether the cerebral palsy signal (RR 2.50, CI 0.97–6.39) is real — a single trial cannot resolve this; independent replication is needed.
- Long-term (beyond 2 years childhood) outcomes for either agent head-to-head — no trial has followed an ASTEROID-type comparison beyond early childhood.
- Whether the WHO 12-hourly dexamethasone regimen (as opposed to ASTEROID's 24-hourly regimen) would show the same equivalence to betamethasone — ASTEROID did not test the 12-hourly regimen against betamethasone.

## Q5 — Mixed courses (one dose betamethasone, one dose dexamethasone)

### Bottom line

**No direct clinical evidence exists for mixed-agent ACS courses.** No trial, cohort, or case series was identified anywhere in which women received one dose of betamethasone followed by a dose of dexamethasone (or vice versa) as a course. This is a genuine, confirmed evidence gap. The following reasons from pharmacology — **expert pharmacological inference, not a graded body of clinical trial evidence** — labelled as such throughout.

### Pharmacological basis (inference, not evidence)

Betamethasone and dexamethasone are near-identical synthetic glucocorticoids on standard potency tables: anti-inflammatory potency ~20–30 for both, negligible mineralocorticoid activity for both, and a biological (HPA-axis-suppression) half-life in the 36–54h range for both. Ex vivo placental perfusion shows near-identical transfer of both drugs to the fetal circulation (betamethasone 15.7% vs dexamethasone 14.4% of maternal concentration within 3 hours).[7] Both are metabolised by placental 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2); a 2026 systematic review found the human in-vivo evidence for any differential effect of the two drugs on 11β-HSD2 activity remains inconclusive, so no claim of differential fetal "protection" via this enzyme is supportable.

**The clinically important difference is pharmaceutical formulation, not glucocorticoid pharmacology.** Betamethasone as used clinically (e.g. Celestone Chronodose) is a 1:1 mixture of betamethasone sodium phosphate (soluble, immediately bioavailable) and betamethasone acetate (poorly soluble, forming a depot at the injection site that sustains release over days). Dexamethasone as used clinically (dexamethasone sodium phosphate) has no depot ester — a plain, rapidly-absorbed, rapidly-cleared solution (plasma half-life 3–4h). This is precisely why the standard dexamethasone regimen uses four 12-hourly doses rather than two 24-hourly doses: to sustain fetal exposure across a comparable window to that achieved by the betamethasone depot with only two doses. Head-to-head PK data confirm betamethasone's terminal half-life (~11h) is roughly double dexamethasone's (~5.5h), with betamethasone measurable in plasma for up to 14 days after a single dose because of the depot fraction.[6] Fetal sheep data corroborate that the depot betamethasone mixture sustains a lung-maturation signal more effectively than dexamethasone phosphate or betamethasone phosphate alone, attributed specifically to the depot's prolonged exposure.[8]

### Is a mixed course complete, incomplete, or unclassifiable?

**Answer the pharmacology supports (inference only — confidence: low-to-moderate mechanistic reasoning, no clinical outcome data to confirm or refute it): a mixed course is best regarded as (c) unclassifiable as a standard complete course**, and should be managed clinically as equivalent to a non-standard/incomplete course, for two mechanistic reasons that depend on sequence:

- **Betamethasone first, dexamethasone second:** the first betamethasone dose's acetate depot continues releasing drug for days independent of what is given next. A dexamethasone dose at ~24h supplements this with a further short-lived peak but does not replicate the second betamethasone dose's own depot contribution. Net sustained exposure across ~48h is plausibly intermediate between a single betamethasone dose alone and a complete two-dose betamethasone course.
- **Dexamethasone first, betamethasone second:** dexamethasone's short plasma half-life means levels have largely cleared before 24h. A betamethasone dose at 24h then functions essentially as if it were a *first* betamethasone dose (with its own depot) preceded by a brief, largely-cleared dexamethasone pulse — closer, functionally, to a single-agent incomplete course.

In neither sequence does the mixed course reproduce the pharmacokinetic profile that the trials underlying current guidelines actually tested. Total glucocorticoid *mass* delivered is roughly equivalent (both single doses are ~11.4–12 mg), so this is a kinetic-profile mismatch, not an underdosing problem in simple milligram terms.

**What this means for the guideline (Q5):**
- Document a mixed-agent course explicitly as non-standard — name both agents, doses, and the interval between them, and flag it for the same clinical consideration as an incomplete course. *Certainty: this is a documentation/process recommendation drawn from mechanistic reasoning, not a graded clinical-evidence recommendation.*
- Tell parents plainly: the baby received two doses of steroid from the same drug family, but because the two drugs behave slightly differently in the body, the team cannot say with confidence that this combination worked exactly as well as the standard two-dose course of a single agent — there is no evidence it caused harm, but equally no direct study of this specific combination exists.

**What we do not know (Q5):**
- The actual clinical (as opposed to pharmacokinetic-modelled) outcome of a mixed course — zero clinical data of any kind was found.
- Whether sequence (beta-then-dex vs dex-then-beta) makes a clinically meaningful difference — the reasoning above is directional but untested.

## Q6 — Single dose, then delivery at ~13 hours: repeat at 12 hours or not?

### Framing

The second dose would be given at 12 hours, i.e. approximately **1 hour before delivery**. The question is whether a dose given ~1 hour pre-delivery adds anything, not whether a two-dose course beats a one-dose course in general.

### What is known about the minimum useful interval

Sheep mechanistic data indicate lung-maturation efficacy depends on *sustained* fetal exposure above a threshold concentration (~1–2 ng/mL) for many hours (26–48h in the cited models), not on a single injection's peak.[Kemp 2018, Fee 2023 — Q2 agent] Genomic glucocorticoid-receptor-mediated transcriptional effects on surfactant protein genes plausibly take hours to manifest. **No human pharmacokinetic study was identified measuring time-to-peak fetal glucocorticoid concentration after a single maternal IM dose specifically in relation to a ~1-hour delivery window** — this connection is mechanistic inference, not measured fact.

### Evidence of benefit from a dose <6 hours before delivery

The closest direct analogue identified is Saldaña-García 2021 (n=76, Spain): a cohort in which the mean betamethasone-to-delivery interval was **approximately 1 hour**, showing serious-outcome OR 0.2 (95% CI 0.07–0.9) versus no dose, and reduced mechanical-ventilation need (54% vs 68%).[28] This is small, retrospective, single-centre evidence, and cannot be considered definitive, but it is the only study identified that essentially matches the unit's specific scenario, and it shows no signal of harm and a possible benefit. The NICHD extremely-preterm cohort (median interval 3.8h) found continuous, monotonic benefit even at very short intervals, with no threshold effect identified.[27] Norman's EPICE-cohort simulation estimated a ~26% mortality reduction from a dose given 3h before delivery — again, a modelled estimate, not a direct observation.[26]

### Evidence of harm from an additional dose given very close to delivery

**No direct evidence was identified of harm** — hypoglycaemia, hypotension, altered HPA-axis response, or impaired postnatal adaptation — specifically attributable to a dose given ~1 hour before delivery. The hypoglycaemia and adrenal-suppression signals reported in the literature (ALPS trial; Zhou 2022) are consistently associated with a **completed** course over a day or more, not a single recent injection, and mechanistically a dose that has not yet had time to exert its intended lung-maturation effect is equally unlikely to have had time to exert the harms that require sustained exposure.[45; Q2 agent Zhou 2022]

### Relevance to postnatal management

No study identified specifically ties a maternal dose given ~1 hour before delivery to a distinct neonatal monitoring protocol. The closest relevant evidence (Zhou 2022) found hypoglycaemia signals concentrated in complete-course infants and resolving by 24h, suggesting a dose given essentially at delivery is unlikely by itself to be a specific trigger for enhanced glucose or adrenal-function surveillance beyond what would already be standard for any preterm/ACS-exposed infant — but **no direct evidence was identified either confirming or excluding a need for such targeted monitoring** after a near-delivery dose specifically.

**What this means for the guideline (Q6):**
- **Give the scheduled second dose even if delivery is expected within the hour.** The mechanistic and closest-analogue evidence available shows no harm signal, a possible (unproven) benefit, and no basis for a monitoring change; the theoretical case for withholding it (that it "can't have worked" in time) is not itself a reason to withhold a treatment with no demonstrated downside. *Certainty: Low — based on a single small cohort and mechanistic inference, not a dedicated trial of this exact scenario.*
- **What would change this answer:** a dedicated trial or larger cohort directly comparing outcomes by very-short (<2h) vs longer second-dose-to-delivery interval; direct human PK data on time-to-peak fetal concentration; or evidence that a near-delivery dose measurably perturbs neonatal glucose/HPA-axis function in the first hours of life.

**What we do not know (Q6):**
- Direct trial evidence for this exact scenario — does not exist.
- Whether postnatal monitoring should be adjusted for infants who received a dose in the final hour before birth — genuinely unaddressed.

## Q7 — Long-term outcomes and what to say to parents

### Single complete course, appropriate gestation

The index trial is Liggins & Howie, *Pediatrics* 1972 (PMID 4561295, no DOI recorded for this pre-DOI-era paper) — the first RCT of antenatal betamethasone for RDS prevention, Auckland, New Zealand.[36] Its cohort was followed by Dalziel and colleagues to 30–31 years: cardiovascular/metabolic outcomes showed no group difference in body size, lipids, blood pressure, cortisol, diabetes prevalence or cardiovascular disease history, but higher 30-minute post-OGTT insulin (ratio of geometric means 1.16, 95% CI 1.03–1.31, p=0.02) and lower 120-minute glucose (difference −0.26 mmol/L, 95% CI −0.53 to 0.00, p=0.05) in the betamethasone-exposed group — interpreted as a possible mild insulin-resistance signal without clinical cardiovascular consequence at 30 years.[37] Psychological functioning and health-related quality of life at 31 years showed no differences in cognition, memory/attention, psychiatric morbidity, or handedness.[38] A separate analysis of the same cohort found that *prematurity itself* — not steroid exposure — was associated with higher systolic BP and insulin resistance, and with fewer depressive symptoms and better quality-of-life in several domains, when preterm-born participants were compared with term-born participants regardless of trial arm — a useful distinction between the effect of prematurity and the effect of the drug.[Q7 agent, Dalziel 2007 x2] A separate, unrelated Dutch RCT (Amsterdam, Dessens et al) followed a different single-course betamethasone trial to 20–22 years and likewise found no adverse medical or psychological differences, with lower systolic BP in the steroid-exposed group.[Q7 agent] For extremely preterm infants specifically, the modern pooled meta-analytic estimate for neurodevelopmental impairment after single-course ACS exposure is *protective* (adjusted OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.57–0.84, low certainty).[50]

### Repeat courses

MACS (n=1858, 25–32 weeks entry) randomised 14-day repeat courses vs placebo: the primary composite morbidity/mortality outcome was unchanged (12.9% vs 12.5%) but birthweight (2216g vs 2330g, p=0.0026), length (44.5 vs 45.4cm, p<0.001) and head circumference (31.1 vs 31.7cm, p<0.001) were all reduced.[39] At 5-year follow-up, death/neurodevelopmental disability was statistically unchanged overall (24.9% vs 24.8%, OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.81–1.29),[40] but a gestational-age-stratified secondary analysis found that among children **born ≥37 weeks**, multi-course exposure carried significantly higher risk of the composite outcome (22.5% vs 15.3%, OR 1.69, 95% CI 1.04–2.77, p=0.037) — **harm concentrated in the subgroup that ended up not being preterm**.[41] ACTORDS (n=982, weekly repeat dosing) found reduced RDS (33% vs 41%, RR 0.82) and severe lung disease (12% vs 20%, RR 0.60) with no difference in birthweight/head-circumference z-scores by discharge.[42] Mid-childhood (6–8 yr) ACTORDS follow-ups found no adverse effect on cardiometabolic function, insulin sensitivity, blood pressure, bone mass, or neurocognitive function, including in growth-restricted subgroups.[43] The newest ACTORDS report extends follow-up to a mean of 20.5 years: asthma diagnosis was similar between groups (54% vs 47%, RR 1.13, 95% CI 0.87–1.46), and other secondary adult outcomes showed small differences with confidence intervals crossing the null.[44]

**UK guideline position** (WebSearch-summary, not independently confirmed against primary text): RCOG GTG74 and NICE NG25 are reported to allow a single repeat course for women <34+0 weeks who received an initial course more than 7 days earlier and remain at high risk of imminent birth, with NICE explicitly capping this at a maximum of two courses and instructing clinicians to weigh possible growth effects, particularly under 30 weeks or with suspected fetal growth restriction. Neither guideline is reported to recommend routine multi-course (e.g., weekly) regimens.

### Late preterm (34–36+6 weeks): ALPS

ALPS (n=2827, betamethasone standard 2-dose course) found the neonatal respiratory composite in 11.6% (betamethasone) vs 14.4% (placebo) (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.66–0.97, p=0.02); neonatal hypoglycaemia was significantly more common with betamethasone: 24.0% vs 15.0% (RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.37–1.87, p<0.001).[45] A dedicated hypoglycaemia sub-study found the excess was mostly transient, resolving within 24 hours in the large majority of both groups, with prolonged hypoglycaemia (>72h) rare and similar between groups (2.4% vs 1.9%).[Q7 agent] The 2024 neurodevelopmental follow-up at a median of 7 years found no difference in the primary outcome (DAS-II General Conceptual Ability <85: 17.1% vs 18.5%, adjusted RR 0.94, 95% CI 0.73–1.22) or secondary neurodevelopmental measures — reassuring given the earlier hypoglycaemia signal.[46] An external-validity re-analysis found the real-world absolute risk reduction for respiratory morbidity, reweighted to a population-based cohort, was smaller than in the trial (−2.2 vs −2.8 cases per 100), with NNT 46 real-world vs 35 in-trial — relevant for realistic parent counselling on benefit size.[Q7 agent, Hutcheon & Liauw]

### ASTECS: betamethasone before elective caesarean at term

Stutchfield et al (n=998, 10 UK maternity units) found SCBU admission for respiratory distress 0.051 (control) vs 0.024 (betamethasone) (RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.23–0.93, p=0.02).[Q7 agent] Long-term follow-up at median 12.2 years (4 UK centres, 51% questionnaire response) found no differences in behavioural (SDQ), educational, or respiratory/atopic outcomes — reassuring, though certainty is Low given the 51% response rate.[Q7 agent]

### ACS given but baby born at term — the most sensitive counselling question

The principal evidence is a Finnish national register-based cohort of all singleton Finnish births 2006–2017 (670,097 children; 14,868 exposed). In term-born children, exposure was associated with any mental/behavioural disorder at HR 1.47 (95% CI 1.36–1.69; 8.89% exposed vs 6.31% unexposed, absolute difference 2.58%, 95% CI 1.92–3.29%); a within-sibling-pair comparison gave HR 1.38 (95% CI 1.21–1.58) — attenuated from, but still significantly above, the whole-cohort estimate.[47] The companion paper reported elevated adjusted hazard ratios across several specific developmental-disorder categories in term-born children, up to aHR 2.18 (95% CI 1.47–3.23) for cerebral palsy and aHR 1.57 (95% CI 1.22–2.01) for epilepsy.[48] A Danish national register study using an active-comparator design (comparing exposed vs unexposed offspring of mothers with the *same* underlying condition) found smaller but still elevated risks (ASD RR 1.5, 95% CI 1.2–1.9; ADHD RR 1.3, 95% CI 1.0–1.7), and the authors explicitly stated confounding by disease severity could not be ruled out.[Q7 agent, Andersen 2025]

**For balance, two studies point the other way.** A Taiwanese national cohort found ACS before 34 weeks was associated with more ADHD/developmental delay in *early-term* (37–38 wk) births, but found **no significant association in full-term births** — the opposite pattern from the Finnish data in the full-term stratum specifically.[49] A small German cross-sectional study comparing term-born children of mothers treated for threatened preterm labour, untreated at-risk mothers, and low-risk controls found children of at-risk mothers scored 6–7 IQ points below controls **regardless of whether they received steroids**, leading the authors to conclude the underlying risk condition — not the drug — drove the deficit.[Q7 agent, Fichtner 2016]

**Association vs causation — stated explicitly.** All of the human term-exposure evidence above is observational; none is randomised for the term-birth stratum, because no trial deliberately randomises ACS with the intention of term delivery. The reason a woman received ACS but delivered at term is itself the crux of the confounding problem: threatened preterm labour, pre-eclampsia, placental insufficiency, fetal growth restriction, and the maternal physiological/psychological stress of a hospital admission for suspected preterm birth are all plausible independent contributors to the child's later outcomes, and none is fully removed by adjustment or by a sibling design (which controls shared genetics/environment but not the pregnancy-specific indication). The sibling-comparison attenuation from HR 1.47 to 1.38, and the Taiwanese/German null findings in the full-term stratum, suggest the true causal effect of the drug itself in babies who reach term, if any, is likely smaller than the crude associations suggest — but a modest true effect cannot be excluded, and a purely confounding explanation cannot be excluded either. **Certainty for every study in this section is Low per GRADE, driven by observational design and un-removable confounding by indication.**

### Absolute risks per 1000 (only where directly stated or computable from the source)

- ALPS respiratory composite: 116/1000 (betamethasone) vs 144/1000 (placebo).
- ALPS hypoglycaemia: 293/1000 vs 173/1000 (computed from stated numerators/denominators).
- ASTECS SCBU respiratory admission: 24/1000 (treated) vs 51/1000 (control).
- MACS-5 term-birth subgroup composite outcome: 225/1000 (multi-course) vs 153/1000 (single-course) (computed from stated numerators/denominators).
- Finnish term-birth mental/behavioural disorder: 88.9/1000 exposed vs 63.1/1000 unexposed.
- Real-world (reweighted) ALPS absolute risk reduction: 22/1000 (95% CI −46 to 0/1000).
- McGoldrick Cochrane review (general ACS vs none, all preterm strata pooled): perinatal death 23/1000 fewer (95% CI 11–36 fewer); neonatal death 26/1000 fewer (15–36 fewer); RDS 43/1000 fewer (32–52 fewer); IVH 14/1000 fewer (8–18 fewer); developmental delay 38/1000 fewer (2–57 fewer).[2]

**What this means for the guideline (Q7):**
- A single, appropriately-timed course has a well-established, favourable long-term safety profile out to 30–31 years in the original trial cohort. *Certainty: Moderate.*
- Repeat courses should remain restricted per current NICE/RCOG limits (maximum two, ≥7 days apart), with particular caution about growth effects if the pregnancy may end at term — the clearest harm signal (MACS-5 GA-stratified) is specific to that scenario. *Certainty: Moderate.*
- Late-preterm ACS (ALPS) carries a real respiratory benefit and a real, mostly transient, hypoglycaemia cost, with no signal of longer-term harm at 7 years. *Certainty: High/Moderate.*
- The parent-facing "does it affect my baby's brain if born at term" question must be answered honestly with both the Finnish association and its causal uncertainty, and the balancing Taiwanese/German null findings — not minimised, not overstated. *Certainty: Low (observational, confounded).*

**What we do not know (Q7):**
- Whether the term-exposure mental/behavioural-disorder association reflects a true drug effect, confounding by indication, or a mixture — current designs cannot fully resolve this.
- Long-term (>20 years) outcomes after repeat-course exposure specifically in the term-birth subgroup — not available beyond the MACS-5 5-year data.
- Whether the ASTECS reassurance at 12 years generalises given the 51% follow-up response rate (selection-bias risk).

## What we do not know — consolidated

- Dexamethasone-specific dosing-interval effects on any outcome, including neurodevelopment (Q0/Q1) — untested.
- Whether the betamethasone-interval NEC signal (Khandelwal 2012) and the dexamethasone-PVL signal (Baud 1999) are real, chance, or confounded findings from small/older studies (Q1).
- Direct outcome data for an ACS dose given ~1 hour before delivery (Q2/Q6) — the closest analogue is a single small retrospective cohort.
- Direct RCT evidence for ACS efficacy/safety specifically in chorioamnionitis-affected pregnancies (Q3) — unlikely ever to be generated given the ethics of withholding ACS from a group already known to benefit generally.
- Whether the ASTEROID cerebral palsy signal (RR 2.50, 95% CI 0.97–6.39) reflects a true drug effect (Q4) — needs independent replication.
- Any clinical outcome data for mixed-agent courses (Q5) — zero data exist.
- Whether the Finnish term-exposure mental/behavioural-disorder association is causal (Q7) — current designs cannot resolve this, and a contemporaneous Taiwanese cohort found no such signal in the full-term stratum.
