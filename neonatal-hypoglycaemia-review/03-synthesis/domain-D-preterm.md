# Domain D — Preterm-specific glucose thresholds and long-term outcome evidence

**Directness:** This domain concerns infants born preterm (<37 weeks), with particular attention to very preterm (<32 weeks) and extremely preterm (<28 weeks) infants — the population that dominates a Level 3 NICU caseload. This is explicitly **not** the population enrolled in the CHYLD/Sugar Babies/hPOD programme (Domain E), which recruited from ~34–35 weeks upward. Nothing in this domain should be read as validated by, or interchangeable with, the Domain E evidence base; every point below states its own population and flags extrapolation where it occurs.

## (a) What the evidence shows

### The landmark positive finding: Duvanel et al. 1999

Duvanel et al. (*J Pediatr* 1999, PMID 10190926, DOI 10.1016/s0022-3476(99)70209-x) remains the single most-cited preterm-specific hypoglycaemia–outcome study and, on current evidence, the most influential positive finding in this entire domain. The cohort was 85 **small-for-gestational-age (SGA) preterm** neonates (Centre Hospitalier Universitaire Vaudois, Lausanne) undergoing systematic glucose detection (hypoglycaemia defined as <2.6 mmol/L, 47 mg/dL), with prospective serial evaluation of physical growth and psychomotor development, infants retrospectively grouped by glycaemic status.

- Incidence of hypoglycaemia in this SGA preterm cohort: **72.9%**.
- Infants with **repeated episodes** of hypoglycaemia had significantly **reduced head circumference** and **lower scores on specific psychometric tests at 3.5 years**.
- Hypoglycaemia was also associated with reduced head circumference at **18 months** and lower psychometric scores at **5 years**.
- Infants with **moderate recurrent** hypoglycaemia had **lower scores at 3.5 and 5 years** than infants who experienced a **single severe** hypoglycaemic episode — i.e., recurrence, not peak severity, was the stronger predictor.
- The authors' own conclusion: recurrent hypoglycaemia was strongly correlated with persistent neurodevelopmental and growth deficits to 5 years, was a more reliable predictor of long-term effect than single-episode severity, and on this basis they recommended repetitive glucose monitoring and rapid treatment of even mild hypoglycaemia specifically in SGA infants.

This finding — recurrence outweighing single-episode severity — is structurally the same dose-response pattern later described in the term/late-preterm CHYLD cohort (Domain E), which lends it some cross-population coherence, but Duvanel 1999 is a **26-year-old, single-centre, retrospective-grouping cohort of 85 infants with no contemporaneous untreated comparator arm and no CGM/interstitial data** — it predates modern neonatal intensive care practice (including current parenteral nutrition, thermoregulation, and glucose-monitoring standards) by nearly three decades. It is a genuine, oft-repeated finding, not a myth — but it is old, and, as covered in (d) below, most of the modern literature testing the same question in the same population (SGA preterm) has since returned null.

### The modern nulls, given equal prominence: Palazzo 2024 and Palazzo/Correani 2025

Two companion papers from the same Italian group (Polytechnic University of Marche/G. Salesi Children's Hospital, Ancona) directly re-test the Duvanel question in a much larger, contemporary <32-week cohort, splitting by SGA/AGA status, and **both return null results**. These are reported here with equal prominence to Duvanel, not subordinated to it, per the brief's non-negotiable rule on nulls.

**Palazzo et al. 2024** (*Eur J Pediatr*, PMID 39707054, DOI 10.1007/s00431-024-05936-2) — **SGA preterm infants <32 weeks**: retrospective review of 1364 preterm infants (gestational age 24–31 weeks/days), classified by blood glucose in the first 6 hours of life as <40 mg/dL (2.2 mmol/L) or ≥40 mg/dL (2.2 mmol/L), and by birthweight z-score as SGA or AGA, with propensity-score matching. Of 747 infants meeting inclusion criteria, 173 (23.2%) had early hypoglycaemia (<40 mg/dL) and 574 (76.8%) did not. SGA infants had significantly higher 2-year cognitive impairment (Bayley-III <85) than matched AGA infants regardless of glycaemic group (+20 percentage points, p=0.040, in the hypoglycaemic group; +17 percentage points, p=0.029, in the non-hypoglycaemic group) — i.e., **SGA status itself, not hypoglycaemia, was the driver of excess risk**. Critically, early hypoglycaemia was **not** independently associated with 2-year cognitive impairment either in the whole cohort or restricted to SGA infants specifically, after adjustment for gestational age, sex, 5-minute Apgar <7, SGA status, prematurity complications, prolonged (>7 day) ventilation, cumulative energy intake to 36 weeks, and maternal education (adjusted OR 1.077, p=0.768, whole cohort; adjusted OR 0.993, p=0.935, SGA subgroup). The authors' conclusion, stated plainly: in SGA preterm infants 24–31 weeks, early hypoglycaemia (first 6 hours) was **not an independent risk factor** for 2-year cognitive impairment.

**Palazzo, Bonanni, Correani et al. 2025** (*Brain Dev*, PMID 40609275, DOI 10.1016/j.braindev.2025.104391) — the **AGA companion**, newly identified in this session's search (not in the original brief seed list): retrospective analysis of 598 **appropriate-for-gestational-age** preterm infants, same gestational age band (24–31 weeks/days), same <40 mg/dL (2.2 mmol/L) hypoglycaemia threshold within the first 6 hours of life. 129/598 (21.6%) were classified as hypoglycaemic, 469/598 (78.4%) were not; time from birth to intravenous glucose infusion did not differ significantly between groups. After propensity-score matching (79 matched pairs) and multiple logistic regression, early hypoglycaemia was **not** associated with a significantly higher risk of either 2-year cognitive (ExpB 1.150, p=0.656) or motor (ExpB 0.982, p=0.834) impairment. The authors' conclusion: in a clinical setting with strict glycaemic monitoring and standardised glucose management, early hypoglycaemia in the first 6 hours was not an independent risk factor for 2-year cognitive or motor impairment in AGA preterm infants 24–31 weeks.

Read together, these two 2024–2025 companion papers constitute the **most direct, most contemporary, and largest** evidence available specifically for the <32-week population on the question Duvanel 1999 first raised, and both are null for early (first-6-hour) hypoglycaemia as an independent predictor — in both SGA and AGA infants. This does not overturn Duvanel's finding (a different exposure window — first 6 hours only, versus Duvanel's serial/recurrent monitoring approach across the neonatal course) but it materially narrows how far the Duvanel finding can be generalised to a modern, strictly glucose-monitored NICU population, and both papers deserve equal billing with Duvanel in any account of this domain, not a footnote status.

### The historical preterm null: Goode et al. 2016

Goode et al. (*Pediatrics* 2016, PMID 27940690, DOI 10.1542/peds.2016-1424) is a secondary analysis of the Infant Health and Development Program (a US multisite cohort recruited in the **1980s**), 745/985 preterm infants with recorded glucose, stratified into four glucose-level strata, assessed at 3, 8, and 18 years. No significant differences in cognitive or academic outcomes at any age between hypoglycaemia-exposed and unexposed preterm children; more severe hypoglycaemia was associated with *fewer* reported behaviour problems at 18 years, a finding the authors themselves did not judge clinically meaningful. Full extraction detail is carried in the shared evidence matrix as row **E17** (`02-extraction/extraction.csv`) and is not duplicated here; it is discussed in this domain because it bears directly on whether the term/late-preterm null-and-dose-response pattern (Domain E) generalises to a preterm population.

**Double directness caveat, flagged explicitly:** Goode 2016 carries two independent directness problems that must both be stated, not just the more obvious one. First, it is genuinely preterm-population evidence (unlike most of Domain E), which is a strength. Second, and easily overlooked, the cohort was recruited in the **1980s** — glucose-monitoring frequency, treatment thresholds, and general neonatal intensive care practice at that time differ substantially from current standards (compare: Palazzo 2024/2025's "strict glycaemic monitoring and standardized glucose management" in a modern NICU). A null finding in a 1980s-era, non-standardised-monitoring cohort is not equivalent in strength to a null finding in a 2020s cohort using contemporary protocolised glucose management — the historical null could reflect either true absence of effect, or noisier/less complete glucose ascertainment obscuring a real effect. Both caveats apply simultaneously and neither substitutes for the other.

### Glycaemic variability/lability as a distinct preterm-relevant construct: the CGM/interstitial monitoring literature

A structurally different evidence base addresses preterm glycaemic instability as a *variability* problem rather than a single-threshold-breach problem — directly responsive to the brief's Domain D instruction to treat this as a distinct concept. This literature is a live, active RCT research area in preterm infants, in contrast to the absence of threshold-outcome RCTs (see (d)):

- **Galderisi et al. 2017** (*Pediatrics*, PMID 28916591, DOI 10.1542/peds.2017-1162): RCT of 50 infants ≤32 weeks or ≤1500 g, randomised within 48 hours of birth to computer-guided glucose infusion rate with real-time continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) versus standard-of-care blinded-CGM glucometer-guided titration. CGM-guided infants spent more time in the euglycaemic range (72–144 mg/dL; median 84% vs 68%, p<.001), less time in mild (p=.04) and severe (p=.007) hypoglycaemia and severe hyperglycaemia (p=.04), and had lower glycaemic variability (SD 21.6±5.4 vs 27±7.2 mg/dL, p=.01; coefficient of variation 22.8%±4.2% vs 27.9%±5.0%, p<.001). This is a **process/monitoring-modality RCT**, not a threshold-outcome RCT — it establishes that CGM changes glycaemic exposure patterns, not that any particular threshold or variability metric changes neurodevelopmental outcome.

- **REACT (Beardsall et al. 2021)** (*Lancet Child Adolesc Health*, PMID 33577770, DOI 10.1016/S2352-4642(20)30367-9): international, open-label RCT across 13 NICUs (UK, Spain, Netherlands), 180 infants randomised, birthweight ≤1200 g, gestational age up to 33+6 weeks, to real-time CGM versus standard care (masked CGM for comparison). Primary efficacy outcome (proportion of time sensor glucose 2.6–10 mmol/L in week 1) favoured real-time CGM (mean 84% vs 94% — reported as such in the abstract; adjusted mean difference 8.9%, 95% CI 3.4–14.4, equivalent to 13 hours). Fewer infants in the real-time CGM group had a prolonged (>1h) episode of sensor glucose <2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) (4/70, 6%, vs 13/85, 15%, standard care). No serious device-related adverse events. The paper's own conclusion states explicitly that "further studies using CGM are required to determine optimal glucose targets, strategies to obtain them, and the potential effect on long-term health outcomes" — i.e., REACT is silent on long-term neurodevelopmental outcome by design, and its authors say so.

- **Battaglini et al. 2026** (*Neonatology*, PMID 41984740, DOI 10.1159/000550797): single-centre RCT, 53 infants, real-time CGM (n=26) versus blinded-CGM standard care (n=27), randomised within 6 hours of life. Significantly fewer hypoglycaemic and hyperglycaemic episodes in the CGM group in week 1 (hypoglycaemia 339 vs 934 episodes, p=0.0001; hyperglycaemia 145 vs 830, p=0.0001) and at 32 weeks postmenstrual age (hypoglycaemia 260 vs 785, p=0.0001; hyperglycaemia 1 vs 38, p=0.0001). This is the **only one of the three CGM RCTs that reports a long-term neurodevelopmental outcome at all** — assessed at 2 years corrected age with the Griffiths Scales — and it found **no significant difference in neurodevelopmental outcome** between groups, a null result reported here with equal prominence to the positive glycaemic-control finding. At n=53 (26 vs 27), the trial is self-evidently underpowered to detect anything but a large neurodevelopmental effect, and should be read as hypothesis-generating on the long-term question, not as evidence of safety-equivalence.

- **Cochrane review (Galderisi, Trevisanuto, Russo, Hall, Bruschettini)**, current edition 2021 (*Cochrane Database Syst Rev*, PMID 34931697, DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD013309.pub3 — supersedes the 2020 pub2 edition, PMID 33348448): pooled four RCTs, 300 preterm infants at risk of hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia. The review's own primary outcome was neurodevelopmental impairment — **none of the four included trials reported it**, nor did any report seizures. Effect on in-hospital mortality was uncertain (RR 0.59, 95% CI 0.16–2.13; very low certainty). The review's explicit conclusion: "there is insufficient evidence to determine if CGM affects preterm infant mortality or morbidities," certainty very low across all outcomes for risk of bias and imprecision, and **no studies at all were identified in preterm infants with proven (rather than at-risk-of) hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia**. This is a Cochrane-level confirmation, independent of this domain's own primary search, that the preterm CGM literature has not yet closed the gap to long-term outcome evidence.

Two further preterm CGM/variability papers were identified and are noted for completeness rather than given full extraction: **Gutierrez-Rosa et al.** (PMID 41894434) established glycaemic-variability reference percentiles in VLBW infants via CGM (a descriptive/normative contribution, not an outcome trial), and **Jagła et al. 2019** (*Dev Period Med*, PMID 30954975) found CGM-derived variability metrics (SD/CV/MAGE) associated with severe IVH and treated ROP on univariate analysis in 74 VLBW infants, but the association did not survive adjustment in logistic regression — a further null-on-adjustment finding consistent with the pattern that preterm glycaemic-variability signals seen in unadjusted analyses tend to attenuate once confounders (largely, degree of prematurity and illness severity) are accounted for.

### The near-miss: May et al. 2026 addresses "variability" but not the preterm population

May et al. 2026 (*Pediatr Res*, PMID 42050117, DOI 10.1038/s41390-026-04958-w) — fully detailed in Domain E — is worth flagging here specifically because its research question (does glycaemic *variability*, as opposed to simple threshold-breach, better predict neurodevelopmental outcome?) is precisely the Domain D-relevant question the CGM trials above are unable to answer with long-term outcome data. May et al. pooled 18,947 blood glucose readings from 2,894 infants across three Auckland-programme cohorts and found dichotomous threshold measures (severe/recurrent hypoglycaemia) more strongly predictive of 2-year Bayley motor scores than glycaemic gradient variability. **This is directly relevant in method and question to the Domain D variability construct, but its population is the same late-preterm/term at-risk cohort as the rest of the CHYLD/hPOD programme (from ~34–35 weeks) — not a preterm or very preterm population.** It cannot be used as preterm-specific evidence on the variability-versus-threshold question, however tempting the topical overlap; it is flagged here precisely because a reader assembling the "variability" literature across domains could easily and wrongly treat it as filling this domain's gap.

## (b) Why — physiological and methodological rationale

Preterm infants, and SGA preterm infants specifically, are physiologically distinct from the term/late-preterm at-risk population in ways that are directly relevant to why this domain cannot simply borrow Domain E's conclusions. Reduced hepatic glycogen stores, immature counter-regulatory hormonal responses (catecholamine, glucagon, cortisol), a higher brain-to-body mass ratio increasing relative glucose demand, and — in SGA infants — additional intrauterine growth restriction affecting substrate reserve, together plausibly increase both the frequency and the potential neurological consequence of hypoglycaemic episodes relative to an appropriately grown late-preterm/term infant. This is the physiological rationale historically cited for extrapolating a lower tolerance threshold downward in gestation. However, the same immaturity that increases physiological vulnerability also means preterm infants in a modern NICU are subject to far more intensive glucose surveillance and more standardised, protocolised correction (as the Palazzo 2024/2025 papers explicitly note) than either the 1980s IHDP cohort (Goode) or, arguably, than a bedside-glucometer-monitored late-preterm/term infant on a postnatal ward. This may partly explain why the two most contemporary, well-monitored preterm cohorts (Palazzo 2024, 2025) return null findings for early hypoglycaemia specifically: aggressive, protocolised correction may be preventing the exposure gradient (recurrent/prolonged hypoglycaemia) that Duvanel's 1999 cohort — managed under 1990s standards — was able to demonstrate.

## (d) Where the evidence is weak — the central finding of this domain

This is the most important section of this domain, and the brief's prediction is confirmed, not merely suspected: **across an exhaustive search (five PubMed search strings plus two Consensus queries, approximately 247 raw PubMed hits and 40 Consensus results, all screened — full log in `01-search-log/domain-D-searches.md`), no randomised controlled trial testing a specific glucose treatment threshold against a long-term neurodevelopmental outcome in infants <32 weeks gestation was identified. None exists for <28 weeks either.** This is stated plainly, as the brief requires, as a genuine and clinically important evidence gap — not a search failure, and not something to be papered over with extrapolated term/late-preterm data presented as if it were preterm-specific.

What does exist, and what does not, should be kept sharply distinct:

1. **What exists:** a landmark but 26-year-old single-centre cohort (Duvanel 1999) showing a positive association between recurrent hypoglycaemia and adverse outcome in SGA preterm infants; two large, contemporary, well-conducted retrospective cohorts from the same modern Italian NICU network (Palazzo 2024, Palazzo/Correani 2025) testing the *same population* (<32 weeks, SGA and AGA respectively) and finding *no* independent association for early (first 6 hours) hypoglycaemia; a 1980s historical US cohort (Goode 2016) also null but doubly confounded by era; and a small but active RCT literature (Galderisi 2017, REACT 2021, Battaglini 2026, Cochrane review) testing whether a *monitoring modality* (CGM) rather than a *threshold* improves glycaemic exposure — which it does — without yet being able to show, by the Cochrane review's own explicit statement, whether this translates into improved neurodevelopmental outcome.

2. **What does not exist:** an RCT (or even a large prospective cohort using a *pre-specified*, standardised threshold definition tested against blinded long-term follow-up) that establishes what glucose threshold, sustained for what duration, at what gestational age within the <32-week or <28-week bands, causes or predicts long-term neurodevelopmental harm. The two Palazzo papers come closest methodologically (large n, propensity matching, standardised modern protocol) but are retrospective, cover only the first 6 hours of life (not recurrent/prolonged exposure across the NICU stay, which is precisely the exposure pattern Duvanel's positive finding turned on), and are single-region (one Italian network), so their null result — while the best currently available evidence for the <32-week population — cannot be treated as definitively closing the question Duvanel raised.

3. **The <28-week gap is total.** Search D1 (specifically targeting "28 weeks"/"extremely preterm"/ELBW combined with hypoglycaemia) returned 55 hits, screened in full, with no threshold-outcome paper surfacing at all — almost entirely gestational-diabetes, growth-chart, thermoregulation, transport, and vaccine-safety literature with hypoglycaemia as an incidental listed outcome. The Consensus cross-check (search D4) independently confirmed a striking asymmetry: at <28 weeks, the glucose-and-outcome literature that does exist is predominantly about **hyperglycaemia and insulin therapy** (Domain G territory), not hypoglycaemia — extremely preterm infants appear in the literature more often as subjects of hyperglycaemia/insulin studies than of hypoglycaemia-threshold studies. This asymmetry is itself a finding: a clinician searching for "what glucose threshold matters for my 25-week infant's hypoglycaemia" will find comparatively little, while a search on hyperglycaemia in the same population is comparatively richer.

4. **Practical consequence for extrapolation.** Every glucose threshold currently applied to a <32-week or <28-week infant in routine UK practice — including any threshold derived by downward extrapolation from BAPM's term framework (Domain A/C) or from the CHYLD/Sugar Babies late-preterm/term RCT evidence (Domain E) — is, on this evidence, an **extrapolation without direct RCT support**, not a directly evidenced, gestation-specific standard. This should be stated to trainees and in any governance document as a plain fact about the evidence base, not softened.

## (e) Practical implication for a UK Level 3 unit

For infants <32 weeks, and especially <28 weeks, current practice (protocolised glucose monitoring, correction thresholds broadly consistent with the general neonatal hypoglycaemia treatment threshold of 2.6 mmol/L/47 mg/dL, adapted locally) rests on physiological plausibility, an aggregation of a small and heterogeneous historical/modern cohort literature, and downward extrapolation from term/late-preterm RCT evidence — **not** on a preterm-specific RCT evidence base, because none exists. Three points should inform unit practice and how this gap is communicated: (i) the strongest single positive signal (Duvanel 1999) implicates *recurrence*, not single-episode severity, as the more clinically important exposure pattern — consistent with, and reinforced by, the analogous recurrence-driven dose-response later shown in the term/late-preterm CHYLD cohort (Domain E), which is at least indirect corroboration of the underlying biological plausibility even though the populations differ; (ii) the most contemporary, best-conducted preterm-specific cohorts (Palazzo 2024, 2025) are reassuringly null for isolated early (first-6-hour) hypoglycaemia under current standardised, closely monitored management — which supports continued confidence in existing protocolised early correction, without extending that reassurance to recurrent or prolonged hypoglycaemia later in the NICU stay, which those papers did not test; (iii) continuous/interstitial glucose monitoring is an active and promising preterm-specific research area that demonstrably improves glycaemic exposure metrics, but the field itself (via its own Cochrane review) states it cannot yet answer whether this improves long-term neurodevelopmental outcome — CGM should be understood, and presented to families and trainees, as a monitoring-quality improvement of unproven long-term neuroprotective benefit, not yet as an evidence-based means of preventing neurodevelopmental harm. The absence of any <32-week or <28-week RCT-based threshold-outcome evidence should be stated explicitly in any unit guideline derived from this synthesis, rather than left implicit behind a threshold number presented as if it carried the same evidentiary weight as the term BAPM/CHYLD threshold.
