# Domain G — Hyperglycaemia and long-term follow-up

**Directness:** This domain is fundamentally more heterogeneous in population than Domain E. Three distinct clinical scenarios are conflated in casual use of the word "neonatal hyperglycaemia" and are kept separate throughout: **(i) primary/stress hyperglycaemia in extremely and very preterm infants** (relative insulin deficiency, high parenteral glucose load — the dominant Level 3 NICU scenario), **(ii) parenteral-nutrition-related hyperglycaemia in preterm infants** (a related but mechanistically distinguishable nutritional-management phenomenon), and **(iii) secondary hyperglycaemia in term/near-term infants with neonatal encephalopathy** (a stress-response phenomenon in a sick-term population, not a primary metabolic disorder of prematurity). Unlike Domain E, there is **no equivalent of CHYLD or Sugar Babies** for hyperglycaemia — no adequately powered randomised trial has evaluated a hyperglycaemia treatment threshold against long-term neurodevelopmental outcome in any population. Every claim below states which of the three scenarios it applies to.

## (a) What the evidence shows

### Operational definitions — and the confirmed absence of UK/BAPM or NICE guidance

No single numeric threshold for neonatal hyperglycaemia has consensus or guideline status. The most current attempt at a synthesised operational definition comes from the secondary review at the centre of this domain:

- **Lagacé & Tam 2024** (*Pediatr Res*, PMID 38972961, DOI 10.1038/s41390-024-03411-0): drawing on the accumulated clinical/physiological/epidemiological literature, propose ≤47 mg/dL (2.6 mmol/L) for hypoglycaemia and **>150–180 mg/dL (>8.3–10.0 mmol/L) for hyperglycaemia** as "safe operational definitions... to delineate" initiation of treatment, and describe a **"u-shape" relationship between dysglycaemia and adverse neurological outcomes** — i.e., both ends of the glucose distribution, not hyperglycaemia or hypoglycaemia alone, carry risk. The review explicitly states that **glycaemic lability has been recognised as a key factor in adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes**, over and above either threshold considered in isolation. This is a review's synthesis, not new primary data — it is cited directly here, and its constituent claims are traced back below.

A dedicated search of BAPM and NICE grey literature this session (see `01-search-log/domain-G-searches.md` §4) found **no BAPM Framework for Practice addressing hyperglycaemia**. Every version of BAPM's glycaemia-related Framework located, including the January 2024 update, is titled "Identification and Management of Neonatal **Hypoglycaemia** in the Full Term Infant (Birth–72 hours)" — hyperglycaemia is outside its scope entirely. No NICE guideline sets a neonatal hyperglycaemia treatment threshold; NICE NG3 addresses maternal glycaemic control in diabetic pregnancy and its neonatal *hypo*glycaemia risk, not a neonatal hyperglycaemia cut-off. **This is a confirmed absence, stated plainly rather than papered over**: UK national guidance on neonatal hyperglycaemia does not exist, and unit-level practice (e.g., the individual NHS regional guidelines identified in web search, typically using treatment triggers in the 8–10 mmol/L/144–180 mg/dL range) represents local consensus, not national policy.

### Population 1 — primary/stress hyperglycaemia in extremely/very preterm infants: the core long-term outcome evidence

- **Zamir et al. 2021** (*Arch Dis Child Fetal Neonatal Ed*, PMID 33863775, DOI 10.1136/archdischild-2020-319926, PMC8394751): the EXPRESS national cohort, 533 Swedish infants born <27 completed gestational weeks (2004–2007), 436 (82%) assessed at 6.5 years. **Duration** of hyperglycaemia >8 mmol/L (144 mg/dL) — not a single threshold breach — was associated with WISC-IV FSIQ: each additional day carried a **0.33-point decrease** (95% CI 0.03–0.62). Hyperglycaemia >8 mmol/L on **3 consecutive days** was associated with lower MABC-2 motor scores (adjusted mean difference **−4.90**, 95% CI −8.90 to −0.89), and each day with hyperglycaemia carried a further **0.55-point** MABC-2 decrease (95% CI 0.17–0.93). **Insulin treatment was not associated with either worsened or improved neurodevelopmental outcomes on any measure** — a null finding given equal prominence here, and one the authors themselves use to call explicitly for RCTs to clarify insulin's role. This is the single most directly relevant primary study for the extremely preterm population and is the clearest trace-back for Lagacé & Tam's U-shape claim in this gestational band: it demonstrates a genuine dose-response (duration-dependent) relationship between hyperglycaemia and adverse cognitive/motor outcome in extremely preterm infants specifically.

- **Boscarino et al. 2021** (*Nutrients*, PMID 34199741, DOI 10.3390/nu13061930, PMC8227040): prospective cohort, Rome, infants <32 weeks GA or <1500 g birth weight, n=108 (32 exposed/76 unexposed) assessed with Bayley-III at 24 months corrected age. **Parenteral-nutrition-related** hyperglycaemia (glucose >180 mg/dL, 10.0 mmol/L, in the first week) was associated with cognitive delay (44% vs 22%, p=0.024) and motor delay (38% vs 8%, p<0.001); hyperglycaemia **remained an independent risk factor for motor delay after adjustment** for background characteristics (the cognitive-delay association's adjusted result is not separately reported in the abstract). This is the clearest evidence specifically implicating the **nutritional-management route** to hyperglycaemia (high early macronutrient/PN intake, as distinct from illness-severity-driven stress hyperglycaemia), with a plausible mechanistic story: excess early PN load produces glucose intolerance in an immature endocrine-pancreatic system, and the resulting hyperglycaemia (or a shared upstream driver) tracks with motor and cognitive delay.

- **van der Lugt et al. 2010** (*BMC Pediatr*, PMID 20646308, DOI 10.1186/1471-2431-10-52, PMC2915976): retrospective cohort, Leiden, 859 infants ≤32 weeks screened over 2002–2006; 66/859 (8%) developed hyperglycaemia (glucose ≥10 mmol/L, 180 mg/dL, treated with insulin ≥12 hours). **Mortality was strikingly higher in the hyperglycaemia group** — 41% (27/66) versus 8% (62/793) in those without hyperglycaemia (p<0.001), concentrated in infants ≤1000 g or 24–28 weeks. Among the 33 hyperglycaemia survivors assessed at 2 years against 63 matched controls, growth was similar, but **neurological (p=0.036) and behavioural (p=0.021) development were more frequently abnormal**. This is a genuinely important companion finding to the neurodevelopmental signal: the mortality difference is so large that survivor-bias (the sickest infants died, so surviving "hyperglycaemia" infants may be a residually higher-risk group even after matching on the variables the authors could match on) is a real threat to interpreting the neurodevelopmental result as a hyperglycaemia-specific effect rather than an illness-severity marker — flagged explicitly, not resolved.

### Population 1b — the pivotal interventional trial, and why the brief's "COIN trial" does not exist

A dedicated search this session for the "COIN trial" named in the research brief found **no trial by that name in the peer-reviewed literature** (see search log §4 for the specific negative search). The trial that actually matches the brief's description — a multicentre RCT of early insulin therapy for preterm hyperglycaemia — is:

- **Beardsall et al. 2008, NIRTURE trial** ("Neonatal Insulin Replacement Therapy in Europe"; *N Engl J Med*, PMID 18971490, DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa0803725): multicentre RCT, 10 centres UK/Europe, 389 VLBW infants (<1500 g) randomised to continuous insulin infusion (0.05 U/kg/h with 20% dextrose support, days 1–7) versus standard care. **The trial was stopped early for futility on its primary outcome (mortality at expected date of delivery) and concern about potential harm.** Early insulin did reduce hyperglycaemia (mean glucose 6.2 vs 6.7 mmol/L, p=0.007; hyperglycaemia >10% of week 1 in 21% vs 33%, p=0.008) and improved short-term nutritional/growth markers (more carbohydrate infused, less weight loss). But it **significantly increased hypoglycaemia** (<2.6 mmol/L for >1 hour: 29% vs 17%, p=0.005), and **28-day mortality was higher in the early-insulin group** (p=0.04) in intention-to-treat analysis. **No published long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up of the NIRTURE cohort was located this session** — a targeted author-based search returned nothing, and this is reported as a genuine evidence gap rather than a search failure: the single largest RCT of a hyperglycaemia intervention in the population most relevant to a Level 3 NICU has no long-term outcome data, and was in any case stopped before it could answer its own primary question. **This null/harm finding is given equal prominence here to the positive observational associations above** — the observational studies show hyperglycaemia is associated with worse outcome, but the one adequately randomised attempt to correct it by lowering glucose with insulin found no mortality benefit and a signal of harm, with hypoglycaemia traded for hyperglycaemia rather than either being solved. Zamir et al.'s own null finding for insulin treatment (above) is consistent with this — insulin exposure itself does not appear to be the answer, in either direction.

### Glycaemic variability/lability for hyperglycaemia — a distinct construct, tested directly, and generally not confirmed as an independent predictor

The task brief and Lagacé & Tam both flag glycaemic lability as potentially more clinically important than either threshold in isolation. Three primary studies test this directly for hyperglycaemia/variability specifically (as distinct from Domain D/E's hypoglycaemia-focused variability literature):

- **Tottman et al. 2017** (*J Pediatr*, PMID 28647271, DOI 10.1016/j.jpeds.2017.05.052): retrospective cohort, Auckland, 443 infants <1500 g or <30 weeks, classified into normoglycaemic/hypoglycaemic/hyperglycaemic/"unstable" groups by first-week glucose profile, with glucose variability separately quantified as the SD of log-transformed glucose values. **Unadjusted**, hyperglycaemic and unstable infants were less likely to survive without neonatal morbidity or without neurodevelopmental impairment at 2 years, and higher mean glucose tracked with worse outcomes at both timepoints. **Critically, none of these associations — including glucose variability itself — remained significant after adjustment for gestation, birth-weight z-score, and socioeconomic status.** The authors' own conclusion is that measures of neonatal glycaemia in this population are **markers of gestational age and intrauterine growth restriction, not independent predictors** of illness or 2-year outcome. This is the domain's most direct test of the variability-as-independent-predictor hypothesis, and it returns a **null after adjustment** — reported with full prominence, not subordinated to the more striking unadjusted numbers.

- **Battaglini et al. 2026** (*Neonatology*, PMID 41984740, DOI 10.1159/000550797): single-centre RCT, Genoa, 53 preterm infants randomised within 6 hours of life to real-time CGM-guided versus blinded-CGM/intermittent-glucose-guided management. CGM guidance **massively and significantly reduced both hyperglycaemic and hypoglycaemic episode counts** (e.g., hyperglycaemia episodes in week 1: 145 vs 830, p=0.0001) — i.e., it is an effective way of reducing glycaemic instability. But **neurodevelopmental outcome at 2 years (Griffiths Scales) did not differ significantly between groups.** This is the most direct interventional test available of whether *reducing* glycaemic lability improves neurodevelopmental outcome, and in this small trial the answer is no — though the trial's sample size (n=53), adequate for the glycaemic-control primary outcome, was very likely underpowered for a neurodevelopmental secondary outcome, so this null should not be read as a definitive refutation of the lability hypothesis, only as failing to confirm it.

- **Lasagni et al. 2026** (*Sci Rep*, PMID 41702956, DOI 10.1038/s41598-026-37286-4): small prospective study, Padova, 60 very preterm infants (43 completed 12-month follow-up) with CGM in the first postnatal week versus 31 full-term controls, using both standardised developmental scales and lab-based visual attention/memory tasks at 12 months corrected age. VPT infants performed worse than term infants across cognitive, motor, and language domains (an expected, non-specific prematurity effect). The one glycaemia-specific finding: **tighter glycaemic control at birth was associated with better memory performance at 12 months** — a positive but small, single-outcome, non-replicated signal from a small sample, reported here as suggestive rather than confirmatory.

Taken together, this three-study set does not confirm glycaemic variability as an independent driver of long-term outcome distinct from gestational immaturity itself — the best-adjusted analysis (Tottman) attributes the unadjusted association to confounding by gestation/growth, and the one RCT that successfully reduced variability (Battaglini) did not detect a neurodevelopmental benefit. This tempers, without discarding, the "lability matters more than threshold" framing found in Lagacé & Tam and elsewhere in the review literature — it is a genuinely open question, not a settled one in either direction.

### Population 2 — neonatal encephalopathy with secondary hyperglycaemia (a distinct population)

- **Puzone et al. 2023** (*Arch Dis Child Fetal Neonatal Ed*, PMID 37316160, DOI 10.1136/archdischild-2023-325592, PROSPERO CRD42022368870): systematic review and meta-analysis, 12 studies included after screening 82. In infants with **neonatal encephalopathy**, exposure to hyperglycaemia was associated with death or neurodisability at ≥18 months in 7 studies/807 infants (46.1% vs 28.0%; **OR 3.07, 95% CI 2.17–4.35, p<0.00001**) — the largest pooled effect size for hyperglycaemia and adverse outcome anywhere in this domain. Hypoglycaemia in the same NE population showed a comparable, slightly smaller association (6 studies/685 infants, OR 2.17, 95% CI 1.46–3.25, p=0.0001). Both findings were confirmed in the subgroup restricted to infants who underwent therapeutic hypothermia. Risk of bias was assessed with ROBINS-I (pooling observational studies) and GRADE-rated per included study.

  **This is flagged as a distinct population, per the task's explicit instruction, and must not be read alongside the extremely-preterm evidence above as though it were the same phenomenon.** In NE, hyperglycaemia is most plausibly a **secondary stress response** — a marker of the severity of the underlying hypoxic-ischaemic insult and its counter-regulatory hormonal response — rather than the primary nutritional/endocrine-immaturity phenomenon seen in preterm infants. The strong OR here may in substantial part reflect confounding by encephalopathy severity itself (sicker brains produce more stress hyperglycaemia and also have worse intrinsic prognosis) rather than an independent glucotoxic effect of the hyperglycaemia. The review's own conclusion is appropriately cautious ("these data suggest... may be associated with"), and the directness caveat here is as important as the effect size: this is term/near-term NE evidence, not preterm evidence, and not evidence about primary/isolated hyperglycaemia.

### Background/context (reviews, not independently extracted)

- **Ramel & Rao 2020** (*NeoReviews*, PMID 32005719, DOI 10.1542/neo.21-2-e89): narrative review of hyperglycaemia in extremely preterm infants (<28 weeks). Confirms the epidemiology (lower GA/birth weight, illness severity, and higher parenteral glucose intake all increase risk; higher amino acid/lipid provision and earlier full enteral feeding reduce it) and states explicitly that data on long-term neurodevelopmental, growth, and cardiovascular/metabolic effects are **"limited"** — a statement this domain's search substantially confirms rather than overturns. It also names the core management tension explored above: lowering glucose infusion rate risks caloric deficit and its own adverse neurodevelopmental consequences, while insulin use (per NIRTURE/Beardsall) increases hypoglycaemia risk — there is no clearly superior strategy in the current evidence base, a point the review makes and this synthesis's primary-source tracing confirms.

## (b) Why — physiological and methodological rationale

Three distinct mechanisms are plausible and not mutually exclusive, mapping onto the three populations above. In extremely/very preterm infants, relative pancreatic β-cell immaturity and peripheral insulin resistance limit glucose disposal in the face of standard parenteral glucose loads, producing hyperglycaemia as a marker of physiological immaturity itself (consistent with Tottman's finding that glycaemia measures are markers of gestation/growth rather than independent predictors) — but hyperglycaemia occurring on top of this may also cause harm directly through osmotic diuresis, intraventricular haemorrhage risk, and possibly direct glucotoxic/oxidative injury to an immature, still-myelinating brain, which is the mechanism implicit in Zamir's duration-dependent dose-response. In PN-related hyperglycaemia specifically (Boscarino), excess early macronutrient delivery is the proximate driver, distinguishing this route from illness-severity-driven stress hyperglycaemia even though both converge on the same biochemical endpoint. In NE (Puzone), hyperglycaemia is best understood as a marker/mediator of the catecholamine and cortisol stress response to hypoxic-ischaemic injury, plausibly compounding (through additional osmotic/oxidative stress on already-injured tissue) rather than solely marking the primary insult — the review literature does not yet distinguish these possibilities. The consistent finding across CGM studies (Galderisi 2017 RCT, cross-referenced from Domain D; Battaglini 2026) that CGM-guided management can substantially reduce both hyper- and hypoglycaemic episode burden, without yet demonstrating a neurodevelopmental benefit from doing so, is consistent with either (i) glycaemia being predominantly a marker rather than a modifiable causal pathway in this population, or (ii) current trials being underpowered to detect a real but modest neurodevelopmental effect of tighter control — the evidence does not yet distinguish between these.

## (d) Where the evidence is weak, contested, or extrapolated

1. **There is no RCT evidence, in any population, that treating neonatal hyperglycaemia to any specific threshold improves long-term neurodevelopmental outcome.** This is the single most important gap in this domain and mirrors, in an even starker form, the preterm evidence gap already flagged in Domain D for hypoglycaemia. The one large RCT that attempted an interventional answer (NIRTURE/Beardsall) was stopped early for futility/harm on its primary mortality outcome before it could inform a neurodevelopmental question, and no follow-up of that cohort exists. Every other piece of evidence in this domain is observational, and every observational association between hyperglycaemia and adverse outcome in preterm infants carries the same structural threat to causal interpretation: hyperglycaemia is itself substantially a marker of gestational immaturity, growth restriction, and illness severity (as Tottman's adjusted analysis demonstrates directly), so associations with adverse outcome may in unknown proportion reflect confounding by the underlying vulnerability rather than an independent glucotoxic effect. **State this plainly: the field does not currently know whether treating preterm hyperglycaemia (by any means — GIR reduction or insulin) improves neurodevelopmental outcome, and the one trial that tried to answer this found a signal of harm from the insulin approach specifically.**

2. **The "COIN trial" named in the original research brief does not exist under that name.** This synthesis traced the brief's description (a multicentre RCT of insulin therapy for preterm hyperglycaemia) to the correctly-named NIRTURE trial (Beardsall et al. 2008). This is reported transparently as a correction rather than silently substituted, since a governance document that repeats an unverifiable trial name would itself become an unverifiable claim.

3. **The glycaemic-lability/variability hypothesis is plausible, explicitly endorsed by the secondary review literature (Lagacé & Tam), but not currently confirmed by direct primary evidence for hyperglycaemia specifically.** The best-designed adjustment analysis (Tottman) attributes apparent variability associations to confounding by gestation and growth restriction, and the one randomised attempt to reduce variability and measure neurodevelopmental outcome (Battaglini) found no benefit, albeit in a trial almost certainly underpowered for that specific question. This should be presented as a genuinely open question — worth continued investigation, particularly with adequately powered trials, but not yet as an established clinical construct that changes management.

4. **PN-related, illness/stress-related, and NE-secondary hyperglycaemia are mechanistically distinct phenomena that the literature does not cleanly separate.** Boscarino's PN-specific finding, Zamir's/van der Lugt's illness-severity-adjacent extremely/very preterm cohorts, and Puzone's NE population all report broadly similar directions of association (hyperglycaemia with worse outcome), which is reassuring for external validity in one sense but also risks the reader collapsing three different clinical scenarios, with different plausible causal structures and different management implications, into a single "hyperglycaemia is bad" heuristic. This synthesis has deliberately kept them separate; a Level 3 unit's clinical response to PN-related hyperglycaemia in a stable growing preterm infant should not be assumed identical to its response to stress hyperglycaemia in a sick ventilated infant or secondary hyperglycaemia during therapeutic hypothermia for NE.

5. **The U-shaped dysglycaemia curve (Lagacé & Tam)** is a reasonable synthesis of the fact that both hypoglycaemia (Domains D/E) and hyperglycaemia (this domain) are independently associated with adverse outcome in various populations, but **no single study in this domain's search directly plots outcome against the full glucose range within one cohort to demonstrate a U-shape empirically** — the U-shape is an inference from separately-conducted hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia literatures, not a directly observed within-study curve. This is stated because the brief specifically asked for the claim to be traced to primary evidence rather than accepted from the review; the primary evidence supports "both tails are bad" but does not, in the studies located this session, supply a single dose-response curve spanning both directions in the same population.

**Directness caveat applying to this entire domain:** the extremely/very preterm evidence (Zamir, Boscarino, van der Lugt, Beardsall, Tottman, Battaglini) is direct for the population that dominates a Level 3 NICU caseload, which is a meaningfully better position than Domain D's hypoglycaemia evidence gap for the same population — but it remains entirely observational (bar one stopped-early, harm-signalling RCT with no long-term outcome data) and none of it establishes causality or a treatment threshold with any confidence. The NE evidence (Puzone) is direct for that specific population and must not be extrapolated to primary/isolated preterm hyperglycaemia, or vice versa.

## (e) Practical implication for a UK Level 3 unit

Four points follow for practice and for parent counselling, stated with the same care about certainty as above. First, **no BAPM or NICE guidance exists to defer to** for a hyperglycaemia treatment threshold — a unit using a numeric trigger (commonly in the 8–10 mmol/L/144–180 mg/dL range, consistent with Lagacé & Tam's proposed >150–180 mg/dL) is following reasonable extrapolation from the observational outcome literature and physiological reasoning, not a national standard, and this should be stated as such in local guidelines rather than presented as guideline-derived. Second, **the observational evidence that hyperglycaemia (however caused) tracks with worse motor and cognitive outcome in extremely/very preterm infants is reasonably consistent across independent cohorts (Zamir, Boscarino, van der Lugt)** and is enough to justify continued clinical attention to hyperglycaemia as a marker worth minimising where practical — but not enough, on its own, to justify aggressive insulin-based correction, given NIRTURE's harm signal. Third, **populations that the literature identifies as needing long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up specifically because of neonatal hyperglycaemia** are: (i) extremely and very preterm infants with sustained or recurrent hyperglycaemia in the neonatal period, particularly where PN-related or prolonged (Zamir, Boscarino, van der Lugt); (ii) infants of any gestation who received insulin therapy for neonatal hyperglycaemia, given the absence of reassuring long-term data and the presence of a harm signal in the pivotal RCT (Beardsall/NIRTURE; also the population in whom Zamir found no insulin-outcome association either way, which does not resolve the question); and (iii) infants with neonatal encephalopathy who develop secondary hyperglycaemia, who already warrant follow-up for their primary diagnosis but in whom hyperglycaemia is an independently-associated marker of higher risk (Puzone) that should sharpen surveillance intensity. Fourth, **glycaemic variability/lability should be tracked as a research question, not yet treated as an established independent target for intervention** — the current direct evidence (Tottman, Battaglini) does not confirm that reducing variability, over and above treating frank hyperglycaemia/hypoglycaemia, changes neurodevelopmental outcome, notwithstanding its prominent billing in the secondary review literature.
