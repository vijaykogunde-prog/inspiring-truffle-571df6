# Domain A — Definitions and operational thresholds (term, 0–72h and beyond; preterm)

**Directness:** The conceptual and numeric-threshold literature in this domain is a mixture of (i) a foundational US/UK consensus statement (Cornblath et al. 2000), (ii) the current BAPM 2017 Framework, which is explicitly and narrowly scoped to **term infants, birth to 72 hours** — its own resource title states this — and (iii) peer-reviewed guideline-comparison papers used here as verified secondary sources for BAPM's exact numeric thresholds, because direct retrieval of bapm.org was blocked by this session's network egress proxy (see search log). No part of the BAPM Framework itself addresses preterm infants; any application of its thresholds to preterm infants is an extrapolation not endorsed by the document's own scope statement, a point made explicit throughout this section.

## (a) What the definition/threshold is and how it was derived

### The operational-threshold concept (Cornblath et al. 2000)

Cornblath, Hawdon, Williams, Aynsley-Green, Ward-Platt, Schwartz and Kalhan's 2000 *Pediatrics* paper, "Controversies regarding definition of neonatal hypoglycemia: suggested operational thresholds" (PMID 10790476, DOI 10.1542/peds.105.5.1141), is the paper that introduced the term "operational threshold" into mainstream neonatology. Its abstract states the problem and the paper's purpose directly: "The definition of clinically significant hypoglycemia remains one of the most confused and contentious issues in contemporary neonatology... Pragmatic recommendations for operational thresholds, i.e., blood glucose levels at which clinical interventions should be considered, are offered in light of current knowledge to aid health care providers in neonatal medicine." Two things about this framing matter for everything that follows in this synthesis and in Domain C: first, the paper is explicit that it is proposing thresholds for *when to act*, not a biochemical definition of *when harm occurs* — the screening/diagnostic-threshold distinction that RESEARCH-BRIEF.md §4 requires be kept separate throughout. Second, the paper is a review/consensus statement, not a primary trial — it is expert synthesis of the available evidence at the time, not itself a source of effect-size data, and this is why it carries a GRADE label appropriate to expert consensus, not to intervention trial evidence (see (c) below). One of its co-authors, J.M. Hawdon, is a UK neonatologist whose subsequent work fed directly into what became the BAPM Framework — the direct intellectual line from Cornblath's "operational threshold" framing to BAPM's own operational thresholds two decades later is real, not inferred.

### BAPM 2017 Framework — current numeric thresholds

BAPM's Framework for Practice, "Identification and Management of Neonatal Hypoglycaemia in the Full Term Infant" (2017), is grey literature — a guideline document, not a peer-reviewed primary study — and is labelled as such throughout. Because direct access to bapm.org was blocked in this session (see search log), the numeric thresholds below are reported via **Giouleka et al. 2023** (*Children*, PMID 37508719, DOI 10.3390/children10071220), a peer-reviewed comparative review of six national/international guidelines (AAP 2011, BAPM 2017, EFCNI 2018, Queensland/AUS 2019, CPS 2020, PES 2015) whose full text was retrieved and verified this session. Numbers below are transcribed exactly as this secondary source reports them; the parent synthesis should treat this as **BAPM-as-characterised-by-Giouleka-2023** and re-verify against bapm.org primary text directly if and when that access becomes available.

- **Physiological ("normal") transitional nadir**: BAPM and AAP describe physiological hypoglycaemia as a normal nadir as low as 30 mg/dL (1.6 mmol/L) in the first 2–4 hours of life, with blood glucose usually stabilising at 2.5–4.4 mmol/L (45–79 mg/dL) by 4–6 hours.
- **First screening cut-off (asymptomatic infant, first pre-feed check)**: blood glucose 1.0–1.9 mmol/L (18–34 mg/dL) with no abnormal clinical signs.
- **Action at this first cut-off**: 40% oral dextrose gel plus feeding; recheck before the third feed and no later than 8 hours of age.
- **Second-tier threshold requiring escalation**: blood glucose remaining <2.0 mmol/L (36 mg/dL) after initial management, or a single value <2.5 mmol/L (45 mg/dL) in a symptomatic infant, or any value <1.0 mmol/L (18 mg/dL) at any time — BAPM's own stated "most important threshold concentrations at which clinicians should consider intervention."
- **Discontinuing monitoring / "normal" pathway exit**: pre-feed glucose >2.0 mmol/L (36 mg/dL) on two or more consecutive measurements in an infant with no clinical signs.
- **Therapeutic (treatment) goal once intervention has started**: BAPM's stated goal is blood glucose >2.0 mmol/L (36 mg/dL) — **this is numerically lower** than the ≥2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) treatment target used in the CHYLD/Sugar Babies trial programme (Domain C/E) and the Australian Queensland guideline's >2.6 mmol/L target for infants <48h. This divergence — BAPM's own stated therapeutic goal (>2.0 mmol/L/36 mg/dL) sitting below the trial-derived treatment threshold that its screening pathway is often described as being "based on" (2.6 mmol/L/47 mg/dL) — is a genuine internal tension in how the Framework is characterised in the secondary literature and is flagged explicitly in (d) below; it is central to the "permissive hypoglycaemia" debate that Domain C addresses in full.
- **Severe hypoglycaemia**: BAPM defines this as blood glucose <1.0 mmol/L (18 mg/dL) — a lower cut-off than the Australian Queensland guideline's <1.5 mmol/L (27 mg/dL).
- **Confirmed/suspected hypoglycaemic disorder (post-critical-sample) target**: BAPM's operational threshold rises to >3.0 mmol/L, compared with >70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L) recommended by the Pediatric Endocrine Society and 3.3 mmol/L by the Australian guideline — all substantially higher than the transitional-period thresholds above, consistent with the different (endocrine/metabolic) differential diagnosis once hypoglycaemia persists (see next section).
- **Point-of-care device-accuracy caution range**: BAPM and EFCNI both flag 0–2.0 mmol/L (0–36 mg/dL) as the range in which bedside reagent-strip glucometer accuracy is most limited (cross-refer Domain B).

### Does the BAPM threshold change after the transitional window? "Prolonged"/"persistent" hypoglycaemia as a distinct category

Yes, and this is a distinct, higher-concern category with materially different management, not simply "the same low threshold continuing for longer." Per Giouleka et al. 2023 (PMID 37508719), **BAPM's own definitional split uses 48 hours, not 72 hours, as its boundary**: "transient" neonatal hypoglycaemia (during the first 48 hours of life) is characterised by blood glucose 1.0–1.9 mmol/L (18–34 mg/dL) on one or two occasions, whereas "persistent" neonatal hypoglycaemia (beyond the first 48 hours) is characterised by blood glucose <2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) on more than three occasions in a row. This is a materially different definition from the transitional-period thresholds above — both in the glucose cut-off used (2.6 mmol/L/47 mg/dL, not 1.0–1.9 mmol/L/18–34 mg/dL) and in requiring repeated (>3) documented episodes rather than one or two. Rusu et al. 2026 (*J Clin Med*, PMID 42194883, DOI 10.3390/jcm15103921), a 2026 systematic comparison of major hypoglycaemia guideline frameworks whose full text was retrieved this session, independently confirms the existence of this category across guidelines generally (not BAPM-specifically in the extracted text) using a **48–72h** boundary and describes its management as qualitatively different: "Persistent Hypoglycemia (>48–72 h): Obtain critical sample; target higher glucose levels; initiate endocrine–metabolic evaluation." This matches the higher post-critical-sample BAPM target (>3.0 mmol/L) noted above and is consistent with RESEARCH-BRIEF.md §4's framing that hypoglycaemia persisting beyond this window represents a different differential diagnosis (congenital hyperinsulinism, metabolic disease, endocrinopathy) requiring a different pathway.

Kebede et al. 2025 (*PLoS One*, PMID 39913386, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0316464) — a systematic review and meta-analysis explicitly titled around "prolonged transitional neonatal hypoglycemia" (PTNHG) — operationalises this same concept independently, defining PTNHG as "a random blood sugar (RBS) concentration of <47 mg/dL measured 48-72 hours after birth." Numerically, <47 mg/dL is the same value as 2.6 mmol/L (47 ÷ 18.0 = 2.61 mmol/L), so Kebede's PTNHG threshold is consistent with (not an independent, different value from) the persistent-hypoglycaemia threshold reported for BAPM by Giouleka — both land on the same ~2.6 mmol/L/47 mg/dL cut-off applied beyond the acute transitional window, which is reassuring convergence across an otherwise heterogeneous guideline landscape. Pooling eight studies and 3,686 neonates, Kebede et al. report a pooled prevalence of PTNHG of 19.71% (95% CI 16.85–22.56%; I² = 79.20%, p<0.001) among hospitalised neonates screened in the included studies, with preterm birth (adjusted OR 3.31, 95% CI 2.57–4.04), hypothermia (AOR 3.41, 95% CI 2.19–4.62), infant of a diabetic mother (AOR 4.71, 95% CI 2.15–7.26), delayed breastfeeding initiation beyond one hour (AOR 3.26, 95% CI 2.03–4.49), and pathological jaundice (AOR 2.37, 95% CI 1.91–2.84) as significant associated factors. **Directness caveat, stated explicitly per the task instruction**: all eight pooled studies are Ethiopian cohorts of hospitalised neonates (the review's own PROSPERO-registered protocol and search were conducted within Ethiopian systematic-review infrastructure); this is not a UK population, healthcare context, or case-mix, and a ~20% prevalence figure from this population should not be read as an estimate of UK prolonged-hypoglycaemia prevalence. Its principal value to this synthesis is definitional (an independently-derived, numerically consistent, operational definition of "prolonged transitional hypoglycaemia" at 48–72h) and as confirmation that preterm birth is a *risk factor* for prolonged transitional hypoglycaemia even in a population studied under different perinatal-care conditions than the UK — not as a source of a UK-applicable prevalence estimate.

### Does BAPM use one threshold across all gestational ages, or handle preterm separately?

**Neither, precisely — BAPM does not extrapolate a term threshold to preterm infants; it is simply silent on preterm infants.** The current bapm.org resource page for this Framework is titled "Identification and Management of Neonatal Hypoglycaemia in the **Full Term** Infant (Birth – 72 hours)" (confirmed via WebSearch of bapm.org's own indexed page title, since direct WebFetch to bapm.org was blocked in this session — see search log), and every secondary characterisation of its content located this session (Giouleka et al. 2023, Rusu et al. 2026, Luo et al. 2023) describes it and the other five guidelines it is compared against (AAP, EFCNI, Queensland/AUS, CPS, PES) as each defining their own scope by gestational-age/risk-factor criteria for screening eligibility, not as providing a single numeric threshold that is explicitly extended to preterm infants by BAPM itself. In other words: **there is no BAPM-endorsed preterm threshold, extrapolated or otherwise** — a UK unit applying BAPM-derived numeric values (1.0–1.9 mmol/L screening range, 2.0 mmol/L or 2.6 mmol/L treatment target) to a preterm infant is making an extrapolation that the Framework's own scope statement does not claim to support. This is the single most important practical point in Domain A for a Level 3 unit and is stated plainly, not softened: see Domain D for what evidence (thin, and dominated by a single 1999 cohort — Duvanel et al., PMID 10190926) exists to justify or qualify that extrapolation in practice.

### Real-world impact: the NNRD evaluation of the BAPM Framework

Nezafat Maldonado et al., "Term neonatal admissions for hypoglycaemia in England and Wales, 2012–2020: a population-based study using the National Neonatal Research Database" (*Arch Dis Child Fetal Neonatal Ed* 2026, PMID 41338963, DOI 10.1136/archdischild-2025-328872), is a retrospective observational cohort study using the UK National Neonatal Research Database, comparing term-infant neonatal-unit (NNU) admission rates for hypoglycaemia in the 36 months before ("epoch 1") versus the 36 months after ("epoch 2") the BAPM Framework's April 2017 publication. Its full abstract, retrieved and verified this session, reports: term admissions primarily for hypoglycaemia decreased from **4.9 (95% CI 4.8–5.1) to 3.3 (95% CI 3.1–3.4) admissions per 1,000 term live births**; the proportion of "potentially avoidable" hypoglycaemia admissions (defined as those managed with enteral feeds and special care only, i.e. not needing IV therapy or higher-acuity care) decreased from **12.8% (95% CI 12.1–13.4%) to 8.9% (95% CI 8.3–9.5%)**; time to NNU admission and median length of stay were **unchanged** between the two epochs (a reassurance that the reduction in admissions was not achieved at the cost of delayed escalation for infants who did need admission). Of infants admitted primarily for hypoglycaemia, **46.5% (11,825/25,406) had one or more BAPM-defined hypoglycaemia risk factors**, meaning the remaining slight majority did not — and of hypoglycaemic infants *without* a BAPM risk factor, 44% (5,990/13,581) had a birth weight above the 90th centile or between the 2nd and 9.9th centiles, which the authors flag as a candidate additional risk-stratification factor not currently captured by the Framework's risk-factor list. This is the paper's own explicitly stated implication, not an inference added here: "Future research should explore birth weight between the 2nd centile and the 9.9th centile and above the 90th centile as additional factors that may further enhance hypoglycaemia risk stratification."

### The screening/diagnostic-threshold distinction and why hypoglycaemia screening does not cleanly satisfy screening-test principles

Alsweiler, Heather, Harris and McKinlay, "Application of the screening test principles to screening for neonatal hypoglycemia" (*Front Pediatr* 2022, PMID 36568425, DOI 10.3389/fped.2022.1048897), is the paper the task brief and RESEARCH-BRIEF.md §4 identify as central to this conceptual point, and its full abstract was retrieved this session. It states the problem precisely: "to prevent brain injury, the gold standard would be to determine if an infant has neuroglycopenia, for which currently there is not a diagnostic test. Therefore, screening of infants at risk for neonatal hypoglycemia with blood glucose monitoring does not meet several screening test principles. Specifically, the long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes of transient neonatal hypoglycemia are not well understood and there is no direct evidence from randomized controlled trials that treatment of hypoglycemia improves long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes. There have been no studies that have compared the long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes of at-risk infants screened for neonatal hypoglycemia and those not screened." The paper's own qualified reassurance is narrower than it might first appear: "screening infants at risk of hypoglycaemia and treating those with hypoglycaemic episodes to maintain the blood glucose concentrations ≥2.6 mmol/L appears to preserve cognitive function compared to those without episodes" — i.e., the evidence base for the value of screening is an association from treated cohorts (this is the CHYLD/Sugar Babies evidence detailed in Domain E), not a randomised screened-versus-unscreened comparison, which the authors state plainly does not exist. This is the paper's core message for governance purposes: **the field screens for a proxy (blood/plasma glucose) for a condition with no direct diagnostic test (neuroglycopenia), has never randomised screening itself, and the operational thresholds discussed above are therefore action thresholds calibrated on treated-cohort outcome data, not thresholds validated as the point at which neuroglycopenic injury begins.**

## (b) Why — physiological and methodological rationale

The operational-threshold approach exists because a single population-derived statistical cut-off for "abnormal" blood glucose (e.g., a percentile of the normal neonatal distribution) does not correspond to a known injury threshold, and no bedside or laboratory test can directly measure brain glucose availability (neuroglycopenia) in a living infant — Alsweiler et al. 2022 (PMID 36568425) state this explicitly, and Cornblath et al. 2000 (PMID 10790476) framed the entire "operational threshold" concept as a pragmatic response to exactly this gap between a statistical definition and a mechanistic one. The consequence, by analogy with phototherapy thresholds for neonatal jaundice (an analogy the task brief and Alsweiler et al. both invoke), is that operational thresholds are deliberately set with a safety margin above any plausible injury threshold, are revised periodically as outcome evidence accumulates (which is why BAPM's own transitional-period threshold, 1.0–1.9 mmol/L/18–34 mg/dL for asymptomatic first-tier screening, sits below the 2.6 mmol/L/47 mg/dL treat-to target used in the CHYLD/Sugar Babies trial evidence base — they are answering different questions, "when to start the care pathway" versus "what level to treat to once on it"), and are inherently contestable precisely because the evidence linking a specific glucose value to a specific degree of neurodevelopmental harm is weak at the margins (this is the substance of the "permissive hypoglycaemia" controversy addressed fully in Domain C). The 48–72h boundary for "persistent"/"prolonged" hypoglycaemia is physiologically motivated by normal transitional glucose physiology: healthy term neonates undergo a well-characterised nadir in the first hours of life followed by counter-regulatory stabilisation by 4–6 hours and full establishment of feeding-dependent glucose homeostasis within the first two to three days; hypoglycaemia that persists or recurs beyond this window is no longer plausibly explained by normal transitional physiology and instead raises the differential diagnosis of a persistent hyperinsulinaemic, endocrine, or metabolic disorder — which is why BAPM's and other frameworks' post-72h target rises (to >3.0 mmol/L per BAPM as characterised by Giouleka et al. 2023) and management shifts to a critical sample and specialist endocrine referral pathway (Rusu et al. 2026, PMID 42194883), rather than continuing the transitional-period feeding/dextrose-gel pathway.

## (d) Where the evidence is weak, contested, or extrapolated

Three points are flagged here, none resolved by fiat:

1. **Primary-source access gap.** This session could not retrieve bapm.org directly (network egress blocked for `www.bapm.org`, `pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov`, and `www.mdpi.com` — see search log for the exact error). Every specific numeric threshold attributed to BAPM in this section is transcribed from a peer-reviewed secondary characterisation (principally Giouleka et al. 2023, PMID 37508719, corroborated in part by Rusu et al. 2026, PMID 42194883), not from BAPM's own text retrieved this session. Both secondary sources are internally consistent with each other on the points where they overlap (the 48–72h persistent-hypoglycaemia boundary and its qualitatively different management), which supports transcription accuracy, but this is not equivalent to primary verification. **This is flagged as the single most important limitation of this domain's output and should be the first thing the parent session re-checks if bapm.org access is available from a different environment.**
2. **An apparent internal tension in how BAPM's own numbers are characterised.** Giouleka et al. 2023 report BAPM's stated therapeutic goal as blood glucose >2.0 mmol/L (36 mg/dL), which is numerically *lower* than the ≥2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) treatment threshold that underpins the CHYLD/Sugar Babies RCT evidence base (Domain C/E) — evidence that BAPM's Framework is widely understood to be informed by. Whether this reflects (i) a genuine, deliberate divergence by BAPM's guideline group from the trial-derived value, (ii) a difference between an initial "at least get above this before easing off close monitoring" operational floor (2.0 mmol/L) and a separate, higher "confirmed treatment target once escalated" value not captured in the secondary source's summary, or (iii) an imprecision introduced by the secondary source's synthesis, **cannot be adjudicated from the material retrieved this session** and is stated here as an open question rather than resolved — it is directly relevant to, and should be read alongside, the fuller "permissive hypoglycaemia" controversy documented in Domain C.
3. **No BAPM-endorsed preterm threshold exists at all — this is a silence, not a stated extrapolation.** As established in (a), the BAPM Framework's own scope is term infants only. A Level 3 unit that applies any BAPM-derived numeric value to a preterm infant is doing so as an unendorsed extrapolation, not as guidance-sanctioned generalisation; the practical and evidentiary consequences of this are addressed in full in Domain D, which finds the modern preterm-specific threshold evidence base thin and dominated by a single 1999 cohort (Duvanel et al., PMID 10190926) alongside a contradicting modern null (Palazzo et al. 2024, PMID 39707054). Domain A's contribution is to state plainly that this extrapolation gap begins at the level of the guidance document's own stated scope, not only at the level of the underlying trial evidence.

## (e) Practical implication for a UK Level 3 unit

For term infants in the first 48–72 hours, BAPM's Framework provides a real, measurable governance benefit: the NNRD evaluation shows a genuine reduction in term NNU admissions for hypoglycaemia (4.9 to 3.3 per 1,000 term live births) coincident with its 2017 publication, without evidence of harm from delayed escalation (unchanged time-to-admission and length of stay) — a legitimate basis for continued confidence in the pathway as implemented, while noting the before/after design cannot itself prove causation. Two things should shape how the numeric thresholds are used and taught locally: first, the transitional-period screening/action thresholds (1.0–1.9 mmol/L range, >2.0 mmol/L therapeutic goal) and the persistent/prolonged-hypoglycaemia definition and pathway (<2.6 mmol/L beyond 48h on >3 occasions, critical sample, endocrine referral, higher target >3.0 mmol/L) are conceptually and numerically distinct, and staff should be taught the 48–72h boundary explicitly rather than treating "the hypoglycaemia threshold" as a single unchanging number; second, and most importantly for a Level 3 caseload, **BAPM's Framework does not cover preterm infants at all** — its own title restricts scope to the full-term infant — so any local preterm pathway that borrows BAPM's numeric values is an extrapolation the unit is making itself, not one BAPM has sanctioned, and this should be stated explicitly in local guideline documentation rather than left implicit. Given almost half (46.5%) of infants admitted for hypoglycaemia in the Nezafat Maldonado NNRD cohort had *no* BAPM-defined risk factor, and a further subset of that risk-factor-negative group had birth weights in ranges not currently captured by BAPM's risk-factor list (2nd–9.9th centile; >90th centile), risk-based screening alone — as opposed to broader or universal early screening — should be recognised locally as an imperfect filter, consistent with, not contradicted by, the admissions-reduction finding.
