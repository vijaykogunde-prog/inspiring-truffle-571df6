# Domain E — Term and late-preterm neurodevelopmental outcomes after neonatal hypoglycaemia

**Directness:** All primary evidence in this domain is from the New Zealand Liggins Institute/Auckland programme (CHYLD, Sugar Babies, hPOD/pre-hPOD) and a handful of independent European cohorts (Sweden, Denmark, Germany). Populations are **late preterm (from ~34–35 weeks) and term infants born at risk of hypoglycaemia** (infant of diabetic mother, small/large for gestational age, late preterm, acute illness) — not unselected term infants, and not the very/extremely preterm population that dominates a Level 3 NICU. Every extrapolation beyond this population is flagged explicitly below (see Domain D for the preterm-specific evidence, which is materially weaker).

## (a) What the evidence shows

### The core cohort: CHYLD

The Children with Hypoglycaemia and Their Later Development (CHYLD) study is a prospective cohort of 614 neonates ≥35 weeks' gestation with ≥1 risk factor for hypoglycaemia, recruited at Waikato Hospital, Hamilton, New Zealand (2006–2010). Clinicians were masked to interstitial glucose; all infants with hypoglycaemia (blood glucose <2.6 mmol/L, 47 mg/dL) were treated to maintain glucose ≥2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) — i.e., the cohort tests outcomes **under a treat-to-threshold protocol**, not outcomes of untreated hypoglycaemia. Severe hypoglycaemia was defined as <2.0 mmol/L (36 mg/dL); recurrent as ≥3 episodes; an "interstitial" (occult/clinically undetected) episode as continuous glucose monitoring reading <2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) for ≥10 minutes despite normal contemporaneous blood glucose.

- **2 years** (McKinlay et al. 2015, *NEJM*, PMID 26465984, DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa1504909): 404/528 eligible children assessed (77%). 216/404 (53%) had neonatal hypoglycaemia. Treated hypoglycaemia was **not** associated with increased risk of the primary outcomes: neurosensory impairment (risk ratio 0.95, 95% CI 0.75–1.20, p=0.67) or processing difficulty (risk ratio 0.92, 95% CI 0.56–1.51, p=0.74). Unrecognised (interstitial-only) hypoglycaemia, lowest glucose, number of episodes, and negative interstitial increment (area under 2.6 mmol/L) also did not predict outcome. **This is a null primary result and is reported with the same weight as the positive findings below** — it is the basis for the current BAPM/CHYLD-derived position that treated hypoglycaemia to a 2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) floor is not associated with early neurosensory harm.

- **4.5 years** (McKinlay et al. 2017, *JAMA Pediatr*, PMID 28783802, DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2017.1579): 477/604 eligible children assessed (79.0%); 280/477 (58.7%) exposed to hypoglycaemia. The **composite** primary outcome (combined neurosensory impairment) remained null (risk difference 0.01, 95% CI −0.07 to 0.10; risk ratio 0.96, 95% CI 0.77–1.21). However, hypoglycaemia was associated with a **dose-dependent** increase in two specific domains: low executive function (risk difference 0.05, 95% CI 0.01–0.10; risk ratio 2.32, 95% CI 1.17–4.59) and visual-motor function (risk difference 0.03, 95% CI 0.01–0.06; risk ratio 3.67, 95% CI 1.15–11.69), with the highest risk in children with **severe**, **recurrent** (≥3 episodes), or **clinically undetected (interstitial-only)** hypoglycaemia. This is the central dose-response finding in the literature: severity and recurrence gradient, and detectability, all independently associated with domain-specific (not global) impairment. The "occult hypoglycaemia is still hypoglycaemia" finding — that interstitial-only episodes carried comparable risk to clinically detected ones — is one of the most practically important and most easily overlooked results in this evidence base.

- **9–10 years, academic performance** (Shah et al. 2022, *JAMA*, PMID 35315886, DOI 10.1001/jama.2022.0992): 480/587 eligible children assessed (82%), mean age 9.4 years. The primary outcome, low educational achievement (reading or mathematics below curriculum-normative level), was **not** significantly different between exposed and unexposed children (47% vs 48%; adjusted risk difference −2%, 95% CI −11% to 8%; adjusted relative risk 0.95, 95% CI 0.78–1.15) — a second null primary result at a later timepoint. Of 47 pre-specified secondary outcomes, one reached significance in a **counter-intuitive direction**: children exposed to neonatal hypoglycaemia were teacher-rated as *less* likely to be below curriculum level for reading (24% vs 31%; adjusted risk difference −9%, 95% CI −17% to −1%; adjusted relative risk 0.72, 95% CI 0.53–0.99, p=0.04). With 47 secondary comparisons tested, this single nominally significant result in the direction opposite to the hypothesised harm is most parsimoniously explained by multiplicity/chance rather than a genuine protective effect of hypoglycaemia, and the paper itself does not claim causal protection. **This finding is reported here specifically because the brief requires nulls and counter-intuitive secondary signals to be given equal billing, not because it is believed to be real.**

- **Structural correlate — caudate volume** (Kennedy et al. 2022, *Pediatr Res*, PMID 36513807, DOI 10.1038/s41390-022-02410-3): in a subset of 101 CHYLD children with MRI and neurodevelopmental data at 9–10 years (70 with prior neonatal hypoglycaemia), smaller caudate volume was associated with emotional/behavioural difficulties and poorer prosocial behaviour, and (in children **not** exposed to neonatal hypoglycaemia only) with visual memory. There was no significant caudate-volume-by-hypoglycaemia interaction on the main outcomes. **This is explicitly a parallel, not-yet-mechanistically-linked finding**: the paper's own conclusion is that neonatal hypoglycaemia is associated with smaller caudate volume (from earlier work) but that this "does not appear to contribute to clinically relevant neurodevelopmental deficits" in this cohort — i.e., a structural correlate exists but has not been shown to explain the behavioural/cognitive associations reported elsewhere in CHYLD.

- **Glycaemic variability vs dichotomous threshold measures** (May et al. 2026, *Pediatr Res*, PMID 42050117, DOI 10.1038/s41390-026-04958-w): pooled analysis of 18,947 blood glucose concentrations from 2,894 infants across three of the Auckland-programme cohorts. Higher glycaemic gradient variability was associated with poorer neurodevelopment at 2 years, but **dichotomous threshold-based measures remained more strongly predictive**: a 1 SD increase in gradient variability was associated with a −0.9-point adjusted mean Bayley motor score (95% CI −1.4 to −0.3), versus −3.1 (95% CI −4.8 to −1.5) for severe hypoglycaemia (<2.0 mmol/L, 36 mg/dL) and −2.1 (95% CI −3.6 to −0.6) for recurrent hypoglycaemia (≥3 episodes). This is a very recent paper (2026) and is the first head-to-head test, in this evidence base, of whether glycaemic variability adds predictive information beyond simple threshold-breach counting; on this analysis it does not clearly outperform the established dichotomous severe/recurrent categories, though the authors state variability "warrants further investigation" rather than dismissing it — treat as an open rather than closed question.

### Dextrose gel treatment follow-up: Sugar Babies

The Sugar Babies trial randomised infants ≥35 weeks with confirmed hypoglycaemia (<2.6 mmol/L, 47 mg/dL) in the first 48 hours to 40% dextrose gel or placebo gel (both alongside feeding), with the primary acute outcome (published separately, not re-verified here as it is outside this domain's ND-outcome scope) being reversal of hypoglycaemia.

- **2 years** (Harris et al. 2015, *J Pediatr*, PMID 26613985, DOI 10.1016/j.jpeds.2015.10.066): 184/237 randomised children assessed. Neurosensory impairment: 38% (dextrose) vs 34% (placebo), relative risk 1.11 (95% CI 0.75–1.63) — no significant difference, but a **notably high absolute rate of impairment in both arms (36% overall: 1 severe, 6 moderate, 59 mild)**, which the paper itself flags as showing "neurosensory impairment is common among these children" regardless of gel allocation — i.e., this cohort's baseline risk (from the underlying risk factors for hypoglycaemia, not from gel treatment) is substantial. Processing difficulty also did not differ (10% vs 18%, relative risk 0.52, 95% CI 0.23–1.15).
- **4.5 years** (Harris et al. 2022, *Arch Dis Child Fetal Neonatal Ed*, PMID 35940872, DOI 10.1136/archdischild-2022-324148): 185/237 assessed (78%). Neurosensory impairment remained similar (dextrose 38% vs placebo 39%, relative risk 0.96, 95% CI 0.66–1.34, p=0.83). **Secondary signal, reported here with equal prominence to the null primary result per the brief's non-negotiable rule**: children randomised to dextrose gel had *worse* visual processing scores (mean 94.5 vs 99.8, p=0.02), though the proportion scoring in the impaired range (<85) did not differ. Dextrose-gel children were also taller and heavier at follow-up (z-scores 0.18 vs −0.17, p=0.001; 0.57 vs 0.29, p=0.01) — an anthropometric signal of uncertain long-term significance.
- **9–10 years, mid-childhood** (St Clair et al. 2022, *Neonatology*, PMID 36516806, DOI 10.1159/000527715): 184/227 eligible assessed (81%). Low educational achievement was similar between groups (42% dextrose vs 45% placebo, relative risk 1.04, 95% CI 0.76–1.44, p=0.79). **The visual-processing signal persisted and sharpened at this timepoint**: children allocated to dextrose gel had lower visual perception standard scores (95.2 vs 100.6, mean difference −5.68, 95% CI −9.79 to −1.57, p=0.006) and a greater proportion scoring below 85 (23% vs 11%, relative risk 2.23, 95% CI 1.13–4.37, p=0.02). This is now a **replicated adverse secondary signal across two follow-up timepoints (4.5 and 9–10 years) in the same trial**, and per the brief's explicit instruction is reported with the same prominence as the reassuring primary-outcome nulls, not subordinated to them. The trial's own interpretation is that dextrose gel "does not appear to result in any clinically significant differences in educational achievement or other neurodevelopmental outcomes" but that the visual-processing finding is real enough to warrant continued scrutiny, not dismissal.

### Prophylactic dextrose gel: hPOD / pre-hPOD

- **hPOD, 2 years, intention-to-treat prophylaxis effect** (Edwards et al. 2022, *JAMA*, PMID 35315885, DOI 10.1001/jama.2022.2363): 1197/1321 (91%) of infants randomised in the 9 New Zealand hPOD centres to prophylactic 40% dextrose gel (n=681) vs placebo (n=678) at 1 hour of age. The primary outcome, neurosensory impairment, did not differ significantly (20.8% vs 18.7%; adjusted risk ratio 1.13, 95% CI 0.90–1.41). **Adverse secondary signal, given equal prominence per the brief's rule**: the dextrose-gel group had a significantly *higher* risk of motor delay (2.5% vs 0.7%; adjusted risk ratio 3.79, 95% CI 1.27–11.32) and significantly lower composite Bayley-III scores for cognitive (adjusted mean difference −1.30, 95% CI −2.55 to −0.05), language (−2.16, 95% CI −3.86 to −0.46), and motor (−1.40, 95% CI −2.60 to −0.20) domains, with no differences on the other 27 secondary outcomes. The authors explicitly flag that the trial "may have been underpowered to detect a small but potentially clinically important increase in risk" — this is not a reassuring null on the secondary outcomes and should not be read as one.
- **hPOD, 2 years, observational analysis by hypoglycaemia exposure (not treatment allocation)** (Edwards et al. 2022, *JAMA Netw Open*, PMID 36219444, DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.35989): a distinct, cohort-style (non-randomised-comparison) analysis of the same hPOD population, comparing children who did versus did not experience neonatal hypoglycaemia regardless of prophylaxis allocation. Here hypoglycaemia **was** associated with higher neurosensory impairment (23% vs 18%; adjusted risk ratio 1.28, 95% CI 1.01–1.60), with the highest risk after severe episodes (28% vs 18%; adjusted risk ratio 1.68, 95% CI 1.20–2.36) but not after recurrent episodes (19% vs 18%; adjusted risk ratio 1.06, 95% CI 0.63–1.80). This finding sits in apparent tension with the null CHYLD primary result above; the key methodological difference is that this is an **observational, confounded-by-indication comparison** (children who became hypoglycaemic despite prophylaxis vs those who did not) rather than a randomised comparison, so it is appropriately graded lower (see GRADE table) and should not be read as contradicting the CHYLD treat-to-threshold trial evidence.
- **pre-hPOD dosage trial, early school age (6–7 years), prophylaxis dosing** (Wei et al. 2024, *Arch Dis Child Fetal Neonatal Ed*, PMID 38307710, DOI 10.1136/archdischild-2023-326452): 309/392 eligible children assessed. No significant difference in the primary outcome (neurocognitive impairment) between placebo (56%) and any dextrose-gel dose (46–50% across four dosing regimens). Combined dextrose-gel exposure was associated with **lower** risk of motor impairment (3% vs 14%, adjusted risk difference −11%, 95% CI −19% to −3%) and **higher** mean cognitive scores (106.0 vs 101.1, adjusted mean difference 5.4, 95% CI 1.8–8.9) — a positive secondary signal for prophylaxis at school age, in contrast to the adverse motor/cognitive secondary signal seen in the hPOD 2-year ITT analysis above. The two results are not directly comparable (different outcome batteries, different ages, dose-ranging vs single-dose design) and the tension between them is not resolved in the current literature — flagged here rather than adjudicated.
- **pre-hPOD, early school age, hypoglycaemia exposure (observational)** (Wei et al. 2024, *J Pediatr*, PMID 38815750, DOI 10.1016/j.jpeds.2024.114119): 315/392 (80%) assessed; 47% experienced hypoglycaemia. Neurocognitive impairment was similar by exposure (51% vs 50%; adjusted risk difference −4%, 95% CI −15% to 7%) — another null primary result — but children with **severe or recurrent** hypoglycaemia specifically had worse visual motion perception and higher risk of emotional-behavioural difficulty, consistent with the CHYLD dose-response pattern (severity/recurrence, not simple exposure, driving risk).

### Independent (non-Auckland) cohorts

- **Shah et al. 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis** (*Neonatology*, PMID 30408811, DOI 10.1159/000492859): 11 studies (12 publications) pooled with ROBINS-I risk-of-bias assessment and GRADE. In early childhood, hypoglycaemia exposure was not associated with pooled neurodevelopmental impairment (n=1657, OR 1.16, 95% CI 0.86–1.57) but was associated with visual-motor impairment (n=508, OR 3.46, 95% CI 1.13–10.57) and executive dysfunction (n=463, OR 2.50, 95% CI 1.20–5.22) — the same domain-specific pattern as CHYLD, now cross-validated across a broader evidence base. In mid-childhood, pooled data (much sparser, n=54 for the impairment outcome) showed an association with neurodevelopmental impairment (OR 3.62, 95% CI 1.05–12.42) and with low literacy (n=1395, OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.20–3.47) and numeracy (OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.21–3.44) — in tension with the later, larger, single-cohort CHYLD academic-achievement null (Shah 2022 JAMA, above); the meta-analysis predates that null result, illustrating how a single large well-conducted cohort study can shift the picture that a smaller pooled evidence base suggested.
- **Wickström et al. 2018** (*Eur J Epidemiol*, PMID 30030683, DOI 10.1007/s10654-018-0425-5): Swedish population-based register cohort, 101,060 singletons, moderate hypoglycaemia in otherwise healthy term infants (diabetic mothers and NICU admissions excluded). Any neurological/neurodevelopmental outcome: adjusted OR 1.48 (95% CI 1.17–1.88); any developmental delay OR 2.53 (95% CI 1.71–3.73); motor delay OR 1.91 (95% CI 1.06–3.44); cognitive delay OR 2.85 (95% CI 1.70–4.76). Early hypoglycaemia (<6h) carried the highest risk (any outcome OR 1.94, 95% CI 1.30–2.89; cognitive delay OR 3.17, 95% CI 1.35–7.43). This is a **large, register-based, unselected-population** study (not an at-risk-only cohort like CHYLD) and is one of the few sources of population-attributable evidence rather than at-risk-cohort evidence; it is retrospective/register-based (not a masked prospective cohort with continuous glucose monitoring) and cannot establish the same dose-response granularity as CHYLD, but its scale is a genuine strength.
- **Roeper et al. 2024** (*JAMA Netw Open*, PMID 38530314, DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.243683 [pii 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.3683]): German single-centre, retrospectively recruited, frequency-matched cohort, 140 children assessed at mean 9.1 years. Severe transitional hypoglycaemia (≥1 reading ≤1.7 mmol/L, 30 mg/dL) was associated with a 4.8-point lower mean full-scale IQ (107.0 vs 111.8), a 4.9-fold increased odds of abnormal fine motor function (95% CI 1.5–15.5), a 5.3-fold increased odds of abnormal visual-motor integration (95% CI 2.1–13.3), and higher parent-reported attention-problem and ADHD-symptom T-scores. This is the **lowest glucose threshold used to define the exposure group** of any study in this domain (≤1.7 mmol/L/30 mg/dL, well below the 2.6 mmol/L/47 mg/dL BAPM/CHYLD treatment threshold) and its findings — a clear, consistent IQ and motor deficit — are most directly relevant to the question of what happens when glucose is allowed to fall well below the treatment threshold, rather than to the treat-to-2.6-mmol/L policy question CHYLD addresses.
- **Rasmussen et al. 2020** (*Eur J Pediatr*, PMID 32666280, DOI 10.1007/s00431-020-03729-x): Danish cohort, 71 children with neonatal hypoglycaemia (<1.7 mmol/L, 30 mg/dL, treated to >2.5 mmol/L, 45 mg/dL) versus 32 healthy sibling controls, assessed at mean 7.75–9.17 years. No difference in cognitive function (Wechsler IV) or behaviour (Child Behavior Checklist). Fine motor scores were lower in the hypoglycaemia group (age-adjusted percentile 42.6 vs 57.2, p=0.03; sibling-paired analysis p=0.008), particularly in boys (β=−16.4, p=0.048). A **mixed** result — null on cognition/behaviour, positive (adverse) on fine motor function — reported in full rather than reduced to a single headline direction.
- **Goode et al. 2016** (*Pediatrics*, PMID 27940690, DOI 10.1542/peds.2016-1424) — **preterm-relevant, cross-referenced from Domain D**: secondary analysis of the Infant Health and Development Program (745/985 infants with recorded glucose), stratified by glucose level, assessed at 3, 8, and 18 years. No significant differences in cognitive or academic skills at any age between hypoglycaemia-exposed and unexposed preterm children; children with more severe hypoglycaemia reported *fewer* problem behaviours at 18 years (not judged clinically meaningful by the authors). This is a **null finding in a preterm population** using an old (1980s-recruited) cohort with historical glucose-monitoring and treatment practices, and is discussed in full in Domain D; it is cross-referenced here because it is one of the few sources bearing directly on whether the term/late-preterm null and dose-response pattern generalises to preterm infants — inconsistent in this instance, since Goode found no dose-response at all.

## (b) Why — physiological and methodological rationale

The domain-specific pattern (impairment concentrated in executive function and visual-motor function, sparing global cognitive/neurosensory composites in most studies) is consistent with the known vulnerability of parieto-occipital white matter and watershed cortical regions to neuroglycopenic injury (see Domain F, Burns et al. 2008), which subserve visuomotor integration and are anatomically and functionally linked to fronto-executive circuits. The consistent dose-response signal by severity and recurrence (not by simple exposure) across CHYLD, the Auckland pre-hPOD cohort, and (for IQ/motor outcomes) Roeper et al. is mechanistically coherent with a cumulative-insult model of neuroglycopenic injury rather than a single-threshold-crossing model — reinforcing the conceptual point established in the protocol (§4) that "hypoglycaemia" is a proxy for neuroglycopenia, and the degree and duration of the proxy signal's abnormality tracks injury risk better than a binary threshold crossing.

## (d) Where the evidence is weak, contested, or extrapolated

Three genuine tensions exist within this domain, and none is resolved by the current literature — all are reported as open, per the brief's rule against artificial resolution:

1. **CHYLD's reassuring treat-to-threshold null versus the hPOD observational hypoglycaemia-exposure signal.** The randomised CHYLD/Sugar Babies evidence (treat hypoglycaemia to ≥2.6 mmol/L, no excess neurosensory impairment) and the *observational, non-randomised* hPOD exposure analysis (hypoglycaemia associated with 28% excess neurosensory impairment after severe episodes) are not strictly contradictory — they answer different questions (effect of a treatment policy vs. association of experiencing hypoglycaemia despite that policy, which is confounded by whatever made prophylaxis fail) — but a reader skimming both papers could reasonably come away with opposite headline impressions. This synthesis treats the randomised/treat-to-threshold evidence as the higher-certainty answer to the clinical policy question ("does treating to 2.6 mmol/L prevent harm?") and the observational exposure analyses as hypothesis-generating for which infants remain at residual risk despite treatment.

2. **The Shah 2018 meta-analysis's mid-childhood signal versus the larger, later CHYLD academic-achievement null.** The 2018 pooled estimate (OR 3.62 for mid-childhood impairment, based on only 54 children pooled across small studies) preceded the CHYLD cohort's own 9–10 year academic-achievement result (Shah 2022 JAMA, n=480, null). This is a case where accumulating evidence in a single well-powered cohort moved the estimate toward the null relative to an earlier, much smaller pooled estimate — worth stating explicitly rather than citing the 2018 meta-analysis's mid-childhood OR as if it were still the best current estimate.

3. **Dextrose gel's secondary visual-processing/motor signal, replicated but unexplained.** Both the *treatment* (Sugar Babies, confirmed hypoglycaemia) and *prophylaxis* (hPOD, at-risk-only) dextrose gel trials show an adverse secondary signal — worse visual processing in Sugar Babies at 4.5 and 9–10 years, worse motor/cognitive/language composite scores in hPOD at 2 years — that neither trial's authors are able to explain mechanistically, and that sits in tension with the positive motor/cognitive secondary signal in the dose-ranging pre-hPOD school-age follow-up. Whether this reflects a genuine biological effect of buccal dextrose gel (osmotic, glycaemic-swing, or other mechanism), chance findings from underpowered secondary-outcome batteries, or population/design differences between the trials cannot be adjudicated from the current literature. **This is flagged, not resolved** — it is one of the more clinically important open questions given how widely dextrose gel has been adopted as first-line UK practice (see Domain C).

**Directness caveat applying to this entire domain:** every trial and cohort above enrolled infants ≥32–35 weeks' gestation (late preterm and term). None of this evidence is generated in, and none of it should be silently assumed to apply to, very preterm (<32 weeks) or extremely preterm (<28 weeks) infants — the population that dominates a Level 3 NICU caseload. See Domain D for the separate, and much thinner, preterm-specific evidence base, and the cross-cutting preterm evidence-gap statement in the final synthesis.

## (e) Practical implication for a UK Level 3 unit

For the late-preterm/term at-risk population this domain actually covers, the CHYLD/Sugar Babies programme provides reassuring randomised evidence that a treat-to-2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) policy does not produce excess *global* neurosensory impairment by mid-childhood, which underpins confidence in the BAPM treatment threshold (Domain C). But three points should inform how this evidence is used in practice and in parent counselling: (i) the *absence* of global impairment does not mean the absence of *any* effect — executive function and visual-motor function are consistently, if modestly, affected in a severity/recurrence-dependent way, including when hypoglycaemia is clinically undetected (interstitial-only); (ii) dextrose gel, now first-line UK practice, carries a replicated but mechanistically unexplained adverse visual-processing secondary signal at two follow-up timepoints in the trial that established its use, which is a legitimate basis for continued outcome surveillance rather than for changing current practice; (iii) none of this reassurance transfers to the very/extremely preterm infant without an explicit, acknowledged extrapolation.
