# Domain F — Hypoglycaemia + seizure: is MRI indicated?

**Directness:** The central evidence base is a single UK cohort (Burns et al. 2008, n=35, Hammersmith Hospital — the same trust as this synthesis). Everything else is small, retrospective, non-UK case-series material (China, India, Turkey, Australia, Canada, Japan) describing the injury-pattern phenotype and, more recently, MRI timing. There is no RCT and cannot ethically be one (imaging cannot be randomised against a "no scan" arm in a child who has had a hypoglycaemic seizure). No BAPM- or NICE-authored guidance on MRI in this specific scenario was found. One 2025 multi-society practice guideline (Spanish Neonatal Brain Group, first author Hammersmith/Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust-affiliated) addresses MRI timing across neonatal pathologies including hypoglycaemia-associated brain damage, but its condition-specific recommendation could not be extracted beyond the abstract this session. Every recommendation below is flagged accordingly.

## (a) What the evidence shows

### The central paper: Burns et al. 2008

Burns, Rutherford, Boardman & Cowan (*Pediatrics* 2008, PMID 18595988, DOI 10.1542/peds.2007-2822) studied 35 term infants with early brain MRI after symptomatic neonatal hypoglycaemia (median glucose 1 mmol/L, 18 mg/dL) without evidence of hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy, at Hammersmith Hospital, Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust — the same trust this synthesis is written for, which is worth stating plainly rather than treating as incidental. Findings: white matter abnormalities in 94% (severe in 43%, a posterior-predominant pattern in 29%); cortical abnormalities in 51%; white matter haemorrhage in 30%; basal ganglia/thalamic lesions in 40%; abnormal posterior limb of the internal capsule (PLIC) in 11%; and — the finding most relevant to differential diagnosis, below — **3 of 35 infants had middle cerebral artery (MCA) territory infarctions**. At a minimum of 18 months, 65% (23/35) had neurodevelopmental impairment, related to the severity of white matter injury and PLIC involvement. The paper's own conclusion, stated verbatim because it is the single most important sentence in this domain: **"Early MRI findings were more instructive than the severity or duration of hypoglycemia for predicting neurodevelopmental outcomes."** This directly answers the brief's central question — in this cohort, MRI pattern outperformed biochemical severity/duration as a prognostic tool.

### Injury pattern: broader and more heterogeneous than "posterior-only"

Burns 2008 itself corrects an oversimplified prior teaching point: injury is not confined to the parieto-occipital region. It described white matter injury (not confined posteriorly), haemorrhage, basal ganglia/thalamic lesions, and even arterial infarction. Independent case-series material — none of it UK, all screened and verified this session — is broadly consistent with a posterior-predominant *predilection* while confirming this heterogeneity:

- **Gu et al. 2019** (*Clin Med Insights Pediatr*, PMID 31447599, DOI 10.1177/1179556519867953): retrospective cohort of 66 hypoglycaemic neonates undergoing MRI (China). Abnormal MRI in 54.5% (36/66), most commonly parietal/occipital. **Key finding, verified against the abstract text as instructed:** the number of days with hypoglycaemia — not the lowest glucose value — was significantly associated with abnormal MRI (P<.001), and prolonged/recurrent hypoglycaemia was likewise associated with abnormal MRI (P<.001); lowest glucose did **not** differ between infants with and without abnormal MRI (P>.05). However, lowest glucose *was* significantly lower specifically in the subgroup who had seizures (P<.01) — i.e., glucose nadir tracks with seizure occurrence, but duration/recurrence, not nadir, tracks with imaging abnormality. This is a genuinely nuanced (not simply "duration beats severity") finding and is reported as such.
- **Mao et al. 2008** (Chinese, *Zhongguo Dang Dai Er Ke Za Zhi*, PMID 18433524 — **[abstract only]**, Chinese language, English abstract retrieved via PubMed metadata, full text not accessed) and **Mao et al. 2007** (Chinese, *Zhonghua Er Ke Za Zhi*, PMID 17953809 — **[abstract only]**, same limitation): two small serial-MRI case series (n=16 and n=6) from the same Chinese group, both showing occipital/parietal cortex as most vulnerable, both showing basal ganglia/thalamic and white matter involvement in the more severe cases, and both showing DWI hyperintensity as the earliest detectable sign, evolving to T1/T2 changes later. The 2008 series found minimum glucose was significantly lower in infants with widespread cortical injury than those with occipital-parietal injury alone (0.71±0.35 vs 1.19±0.42 mmol/L, P<0.05) — in this small, non-UK series, severity of the glucose nadir *did* track with the extent of injury, which sits in tension with Gu 2019's larger finding above about duration vs nadir; both are reported rather than adjudicated, since neither is a UK-comparable, adequately powered source.
- **Hu et al. 2014** (*Acta Paediatr*, PMID 24798709, DOI 10.1111/apa.12673): case-control study (15 hypoglycaemia cases vs 11 controls, China) using flash visual evoked potentials (FVEP) alongside MRI. 80% of the hypoglycaemia group had occipital-lobe injury on MRI; FVEP was 100% sensitive but only 25% specific for parieto-occipital injury, so cannot substitute for MRI. Notably, the infants who went on to have seizures were detected as hypoglycaemic later (median 51 hours of age) than those who did not (median 22 hours, P=0.003) — consistent with a picture in which delayed recognition, not simply glucose depth, contributes to the seizure phenotype.
- **Kurahashi et al. 2023** (*World J Clin Cases*, PMID 37383118, DOI 10.12998/wjcc.v11.i16.3899): a single case report of an 8-year-old with parieto-temporo-occipital injury from neonatal hypoglycaemia/seizures, notable for demonstrating a **non-visual** long-term deficit (slowed reading and impaired phonological awareness, without cortical blindness or visual field defect) — a useful reminder that the outcome phenotype from this injury topography extends beyond the commonly emphasised cortical visual impairment.

### Later epilepsy phenotype: variable, and occipital-predominant

- **Fong & Harvey 2014** (*Dev Med Child Neurol*, PMID 24861161, DOI 10.1111/dmcn.12496): retrospective case series of 11 children (Melbourne) with seizures beyond infancy after neonatal hypoglycaemia (perinatal asphyxia excluded). All 11 had MRI evidence of occipital ± parietal gliosis/atrophy. Seizure phenotype was genuinely variable: focal occipital seizures in 9 (infrequent or remitted in 6 of these 9), generalised tonic seizures in 2 (both frequent and refractory). The paper's own framing — mild occipital, refractory occipital, and symptomatic generalised epilepsy all recognised from the same antecedent injury — is the headline finding and is reported as a genuine null-to-mixed result on the question of whether injury pattern alone predicts epilepsy severity.
- **Kapoor et al. 2020** (*Seizure*, PMID 32446209, DOI 10.1016/j.seizure.2020.05.010): the largest case series in this domain by a wide margin — 170 children with epilepsy secondary to documented neonatal hypoglycaemic brain injury, over a 10-year period at a single Indian tertiary epilepsy clinic explicitly described by the authors as a **low-resource setting**. MRI showed occipital ± parietal gliosis/encephalomalacia in 96.5%. Epileptic spasms were the dominant seizure type (76.5%), and 68.2% (116/170) had drug-resistant epilepsy. Comorbidity was substantial: global developmental delay 91.2%, cerebral palsy 48.7%, vision impairment 48.2%, microcephaly 38.2%. **This PMID was flagged as unresolvable in the parent scoping session's could-not-verify list; it resolved cleanly this session both as a solo `get_article_metadata` call and via an independent title search — see §"Kapoor PMID" below.** This series sits at the most severe end of the spectrum (LMIC referral-clinic population with established, often refractory epilepsy) and its very high comorbidity rates should not be read as representative of the outcome distribution in a UK cohort with earlier detection and treatment.
- **Yalçın et al. 2022** (*Noro Psikiyatri Arsivi*, PMID 36514522, DOI 10.29399/npa.27997): independent Turkish replication (n=21 with documented hypoglycaemia and typical parieto-occipital injury) showing the same outcome cluster — 85.7% developed epilepsy, 38.1% refractory, 33.3% cerebral palsy, 100% of those tested (18/18) had abnormal VEP. Prolonged hypoglycaemia (recurrent episodes >12h) was associated with significantly greater parieto-occipital involvement and spasticity (P<0.01). This is now a **third independent non-UK cohort** (after Fong 2014 and Kapoor 2020) cross-validating the injury-pattern-to-epilepsy/CP/visual-impairment linkage, which strengthens confidence in the phenotype description even though none of the underlying cohorts is UK-based or population-representative.

### MRI timing: when does the scan need to happen, and what does it show at that point?

This is the sub-question the brief specifically asked to be chased for 2015+ literature, since Burns 2008 (2008) does not itself resolve it.

- **Tam et al. 2008** (*Pediatrics*, PMID 18762519, DOI 10.1542/peds.2007-2002) — pre-2015 but foundational and retained per the brief's rule that foundational imaging papers are not date-filtered. In 45 neonates (term and preterm) with diffusion-weighted MRI (DWI) after hypoglycaemia: among 25 infants scanned **within 6 days** of hypoglycaemia onset, occipital diffusion restriction was seen in 8/16 (50%) **term** infants but in **zero** preterm infants; among 20 infants scanned **beyond 6 days**, no occipital diffusion restriction was seen at all, even where hypoglycaemia was ongoing. This is a directly quantified timing-window finding: DWI is sensitive for the classic injury signature only within roughly the first week, and — critically for directness — **this sensitivity does not transfer to preterm infants at all** in this dataset. Diffusion restriction correlated with abnormal visual evoked potentials and later cortical visual deficit.
- **Zhang et al. 2022** (*Eur J Pediatr*, PMID 36166098, DOI 10.1007/s00431-022-04637-y) — the most direct 2015+ MRI-timing paper found. Retrospective analysis of 139 DWI scans in 86 Chinese neonates with hypoglycaemic brain injury: 84 scans showed injury-site DWI hyperintensity **within 7 days** of hypoglycaemia; 39 scans showed the signal evolving to abnormal T1/T2-weighted changes (with DWI often pseudonormalising) at **11–23 days**; repeat scans in 13 infants at 9–20 days showed no abnormal signal at all. Vulnerable sites: occipital lobe 98%, splenium of the corpus callosum 60%, parietal lobe 30%. Extensive injury (involving internal capsule, basal ganglia, thalamus; n=20) carried significantly higher rates of recurrent convulsions, respiratory failure, coma, and death/severe neurodevelopmental impairment than focal injury (n=66) — the authors explicitly note this extensive pattern may partly reflect coexisting hypoxia-ischaemia rather than hypoglycaemia in isolation, a caveat directly relevant to the differential-diagnosis point below.
- Taken together, Tam 2008 and Zhang 2022 — a decade and a different country apart, using the same DWI methodology — converge on a consistent early-DWI-positive, later-pseudonormalising timing pattern, with the injury becoming harder to detect on DWI after roughly one to two weeks even as conventional T1/T2 sequences may still show or newly show abnormality. This is the empirical basis for the "scan early" recommendation in §(e), and it is corroborated (though not independently timed) by both Mao 2007 and Mao 2008 reporting DWI as the earliest-detectable abnormality in their own small serial-imaging series.

### Differential diagnosis and coexistence: the stroke-mimic point

Burns 2008's finding that **3 of 35 infants (8.6%) undergoing MRI for hypoglycaemic seizure workup had MCA territory infarctions** is the direct evidential basis for treating MRI as having a differential-diagnosis role that is separable from — and does not depend on — its prognostic role. A dedicated PubMed search for literature specifically pairing "hypoglycaemic seizure" with "stroke mimic" terminology returned no hits: this point rests on the internal finding within the Burns cohort itself, not on a dedicated literature, and should be presented as such rather than implying a larger evidence base exists. The practical implication is straightforward regardless: a clinical picture of "hypoglycaemia plus seizure" cannot be assumed on biochemistry alone to be caused by hypoglycaemia — MRI (not glucose values, not EEG alone) is the modality that would detect a coexisting or confounding arterial infarction, and by extension would also be the modality to detect coexisting HIE, a structural lesion, or an atypical/multifocal pattern that might prompt a metabolic or genetic work-up rather than (or in addition to) a hypoglycaemia-only diagnosis. This is a qualitatively different indication for MRI than the prognostic one, and the two should not be conflated: even where prognosis for a straightforward hypoglycaemic injury tracks the imaging pattern well (per Burns 2008's headline finding), MRI retains a separate, undiminished role in excluding or detecting a coexisting alternative diagnosis.

### A 2025 guideline addressing MRI timing across neonatal pathologies, including hypoglycaemia

**Martínez-Biarge et al. 2025** (*An Pediatr* [Engl Ed], PMID 41022518, DOI 10.1016/j.anpede.2025.503935): a practice-guideline document from the Spanish Neonatal Brain Group and paediatric neuroradiologists, covering MRI logistics, condition-specific protocols, and — of direct relevance here — "clinical indications and optimal timing of MRI... to maximize its diagnostic and prognostic value in different pathologies such as... brain damage associated with hypoglycemia." The first author is affiliated with **Hammersmith Hospital, Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust** — the same trust as Burns 2008 and as this synthesis's intended use, which is a genuinely useful practice link even though the guideline itself is Spanish multi-society output, not a UK/BAPM/NICE document. No PMC full text was available (confirmed via `convert_article_ids` this session), so the specific timing value recommended for the hypoglycaemia section could not be extracted beyond the abstract — this is marked **[abstract only]** and should be re-obtained in full text before being used to write a specific timing rule into unit guidance. This is nonetheless the only guideline-tier (as opposed to case-series-tier) source located anywhere in this domain that names hypoglycaemia-associated brain damage as a condition with its own MRI timing considerations, and is treated throughout as **grey literature**, not folded into the peer-reviewed evidence count, per the project's non-negotiable rules.

## (b) Why — physiological and methodological rationale

The posterior (parieto-occipital) predilection described across this literature is consistent with the developmental metabolic-demand gradient of the neonatal brain: the occipital and parietal cortices are undergoing particularly active synaptogenesis and have high regional glucose utilisation in the perinatal period, making them selectively vulnerable to substrate (glucose) deprivation — the same "neuroglycopenia as the true injury mechanism, glucose as an imperfect proxy" framing established in the project's conceptual framing (RESEARCH-BRIEF.md §4) applies directly here. Burns 2008's finding that injury is not confined to this region — extending to white matter, basal ganglia/thalamus, haemorrhage, and even arterial infarction — indicates that severe or prolonged neuroglycopenic insult can produce a watershed-type or more globally distributed pattern once the posterior predilection is overwhelmed, analogous to the way basal ganglia/thalamic and white matter/watershed patterns are described in hypoxic-ischaemic injury; several of the case series here explicitly flag that their most severe, most extensive-injury cases may reflect coexisting hypoxia-ischaemia rather than hypoglycaemia in isolation (Zhang 2022), which is itself an argument for MRI's differential-diagnosis role rather than an argument against it.

The MRI-timing pattern (Tam 2008, Zhang 2022) is consistent with the known evolution of cytotoxic oedema on diffusion-weighted imaging generally: DWI hyperintensity from restricted diffusion appears early (in this literature, within roughly 6–7 days) and pseudonormalises over one to three weeks as the oedema resolves or the tissue becomes established/gliotic, at which point conventional T1/T2-weighted sequences — not DWI — carry the diagnostic signal. This is the same DWI evolution principle seen in other perinatal cerebral injury (e.g. arterial ischaemic stroke, HIE), and is the physiological basis for recommending imaging in the first week rather than at an arbitrarily later, more "convenient" time.

## (d) Where the evidence is weak, contested, or extrapolated

1. **The entire evidence base outside Burns 2008 is small, retrospective, and non-UK.** No study in this domain other than Burns 2008 was conducted in a UK population or setting comparable to a modern UK Level 3 unit's glycaemic monitoring and treatment practice. The largest series (Kapoor 2020, n=170) is explicitly from a low-resource LMIC referral clinic with established, often drug-resistant epilepsy — its very high comorbidity rates (91.2% global developmental delay, 48.7% cerebral palsy) almost certainly overstate the risk that would be seen in a UK cohort with earlier glucose detection, treatment, and imaging access, and should not be quoted to parents or in governance documents as if representative of UK-expected outcomes.

2. **Severity vs duration as the driver of injury is not settled.** Gu 2019 (n=66, the larger and more recent of the two duration-focused series) found duration/recurrence, not glucose nadir, predicted abnormal MRI; Mao 2008 (n=16, smaller, earlier) found glucose nadir did track with injury extent in the more severe subgroup. Both are non-UK, retrospective, and modestly sized. This is reported as an open tension, not resolved — the honest summary is that both duration and severity plausibly matter, and no single study in this domain is powered or designed to adjudicate between them.

3. **The MRI-timing evidence (Tam 2008, Zhang 2022), while internally consistent, rests on only two studies, 14 years apart, both from single non-UK tertiary centres**, using retrospective and likely non-protocolised (clinically-driven) scan timing. The recommendation to scan within the first week is well-supported *qualitatively* by DWI physiology and by these two studies' concordance, but the exact optimal day (day 3? day 5? day 7?) cannot be pinned down more precisely than "within roughly a week, and preferably not later" from the current literature.

4. **The stroke-mimic/coexistence point has essentially one data point.** The 3/35 MCA infarction finding in Burns 2008 is the entire evidentiary basis for this argument in the peer-reviewed literature identified this session — no dedicated study of stroke-hypoglycaemia coexistence rates was found. This does not make the underlying clinical logic (MRI, not biochemistry, is the tool that detects a coexisting stroke) any less sound, but the specific 8.6% figure should be treated as illustrative of the phenomenon existing, not as a stable estimate of its population frequency.

5. **The 2025 Spanish guideline (Martínez-Biarge et al.) could not be fully interrogated this session** — its abstract confirms that hypoglycaemia-associated brain damage is one of the conditions for which the document gives specific timing/indication recommendations, but the actual recommended timing window was not extractable without full text (no PMC copy available). This is the single most promising near-term source for upgrading the "when to scan" recommendation from case-series-derived to guideline-derived, and should be a priority follow-up (obtain via the journal directly, or via the first author's NHS trust) before this domain's recommendation is finalised for governance use.

6. **The preterm population is essentially unaddressed.** Tam 2008 is the only study in this domain reporting on preterm infants specifically, and its finding is negative (no preterm infant showed the expected DWI signature even within the sensitive window) — this could mean preterm infants are genuinely less susceptible to this injury pattern, or (more likely, given the small preterm subgroup and DWI's known technical limitations at preterm gestations) that DWI is simply less sensitive in the preterm brain for reasons unrelated to whether injury occurred. Neither explanation can be distinguished from the current literature, and no other study in this domain reports preterm-specific data at all. This should be stated as an evidence gap, not silently extrapolated over.

## (e) Practical implication for a UK Level 3 unit

**Working recommendation:** In an infant who has had a hypoglycaemic seizure, brain MRI is indicated for two distinct reasons that should both be named explicitly when requesting it: (i) **differential diagnosis** — to detect or exclude a coexisting or alternative cause (arterial ischaemic stroke, HIE, structural lesion, or a pattern atypical enough to prompt metabolic/genetic work-up), a role that holds regardless of how straightforward the biochemical picture seems, given Burns 2008's own cohort included unexpected MCA infarctions; and (ii) **prognostication**, where the evidence (principally Burns 2008, the only UK/directly-relevant source) indicates that the MRI pattern — specifically white matter injury severity and PLIC involvement — is a better predictor of 18-month neurodevelopmental outcome than the glucose value or duration reached during the acute episode. **Timing:** on the balance of the two DWI-timing studies located (Tam 2008, Zhang 2022), MRI should be obtained as early as practicable and, if at all possible, within the first week of the hypoglycaemic seizure — DWI sensitivity for the characteristic occipital/parieto-occipital signature declines sharply beyond roughly 6–7 days, after which conventional T1/T2-weighted sequences rather than DWI carry the diagnostic information, and a scan performed purely for logistical convenience at 2–3 weeks risks a falsely reassuring or uninterpretable DWI even where earlier injury occurred.

**Which infants should be scanned:** the literature located this session does not support restricting imaging only to the most severe or prolonged cases as a validated triage rule — no study in this domain tested or validated a severity/duration threshold below which MRI can safely be omitted. Given that (a) the differential-diagnosis rationale applies to essentially any infant with a seizure regardless of biochemical severity, and (b) Burns 2008 found imaging outperformed biochemical severity for prognosis specifically because biochemical severity was a poor discriminator, the working position taken here is that **any infant with a clinical seizure attributed to (or occurring alongside) neonatal hypoglycaemia warrants brain MRI**, not only those with the most severe or longest biochemical derangement — but this is a reasoned extrapolation from the available evidence's logic, not a validated triage threshold from a trial or guideline.

**Directness caveat — state this plainly, not as a footnote:** this entire recommendation, other than the central Burns 2008 finding, is extrapolated from small, heterogeneous, largely non-UK case series (China, India, Turkey, Canada, Japan, Australia) rather than from a guideline-level source. No BAPM or NICE document addressing MRI in hypoglycaemic seizure specifically was found. The one guideline-tier document located (Martínez-Biarge et al. 2025, Spanish Neonatal Brain Group) is promising — not least for its first author's Hammersmith/Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust affiliation, the same trust as Burns 2008 and as this synthesis — but its specific hypoglycaemia-timing recommendation could not be extracted from the abstract alone this session and should be obtained in full text before this recommendation is written into unit guidance as anything more authoritative than "an evidence-informed working position, extrapolated from case-series-level data, pending guideline confirmation."
