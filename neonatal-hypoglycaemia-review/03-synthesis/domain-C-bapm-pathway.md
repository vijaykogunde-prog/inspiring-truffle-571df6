# Domain C — BAPM core management pathway for term infants (first 48 hours), its evidence base, the permissive-hypoglycaemia controversy, and dextrose gel Cochrane status

**Directness:** This domain concerns the **term and late-preterm (from ~34–35 weeks) infant, birth to 72 hours** — the exact population the BAPM Framework targets and the exact population enrolled in the CHYLD/Sugar Babies programme (see Domain E). Every RCT-grade element below is direct evidence for this population; every extrapolation to preterm infants (Lucas 1988, and the general question of whether BAPM's numeric thresholds apply below 34 weeks) is flagged explicitly. Grey literature (BAPM, NICE) is labelled as such throughout and never folded into a peer-reviewed evidence count.

## (a) What the pathway is and how it was derived

### The BAPM Framework itself — grey literature, labelled

The British Association of Perinatal Medicine's **"Identification and Management of Neonatal Hypoglycaemia in the Full Term Infant"** was convened as a joint BAPM/NHS Improvement working group in 2016 in response to wide, unexplained national variation in the identification, management, and admission thresholds for term hypoglycaemia, and to reduce unnecessary mother-baby separation. It was first published in **April 2017** and has since been revised, with the current version — retitled "...(Birth – 72 hours)" — dated **24 January 2024** (a public consultation on the revision ran in June 2023). **This session could not directly retrieve either bapm.org document** (WebFetch to bapm.org returned `EGRESS_BLOCKED`); the pathway detail below is triangulated from BAPM's own page as indexed by web search, cross-checked against five independent NHS trust clinical guidelines that explicitly cite BAPM as their source (Royal Cornwall, Ashford & St Peter's, North West Neonatal ODN, NHSGGC, Milton Keynes) — used here as convergent secondary confirmation, not as independent primary sources, and the numeric values below should be treated as triangulated-but-not-independently-document-verified. This limitation is stated plainly rather than papered over.

Element by element, as triangulated:

1. **Risk-factor identification.** Infant of a diabetic mother (any type, including gestational), small-for-gestational-age (SGA), large-for-gestational-age (LGA), late preterm (34–36+6 weeks), and other clinical risk factors (e.g., perinatal stress/acute illness, maternal beta-blocker use). Feeding difficulty and clinical instability are additional flagged concerns.
2. **First-line management: feeding.** Early feeding (within the first hour) and frequent (3-hourly) feeding for all at-risk infants, with capillary blood glucose checked immediately before each feed.
3. **Screening timing/frequency.** First check before the second feed or by 4 hours of age (whichever is first), then pre-feed 3-hourly, continuing until stable normoglycaemia is demonstrated on serial readings.
4. **Treatment threshold(s), tiered by severity.** A target/screening floor of blood glucose ≥2.0 mmol/L (36 mg/dL) with active management triggered at <2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) in most transcribing guidelines: asymptomatic blood glucose 1.0–1.9 mmol/L (18–34 mg/dL) → buccal 40% dextrose gel (200 mg/kg) as an adjunct to continued feeding, up to two doses.
5. **Escalation to IV dextrose.** Blood glucose <1.0 mmol/L (18 mg/dL), and/or any clinical signs of hypoglycaemia at any glucose level, and/or persistent hypoglycaemia despite two doses of dextrose gel → IV 10% dextrose bolus (approximately 2.5 mL/kg) followed by a maintenance infusion (approximately 60 mL/kg/day), with admission to a neonatal unit.
6. **Discharge criteria.** Not before 24 hours of age, and not before at least three consecutive pre-feed glucose readings ≥2.0 mmol/L (36 mg/dL) with effective feeding established and a completed breastfeeding assessment.

### NICE cross-reference — also grey literature

NICE's operative numeric infant-glucose recommendation lives in **NG3** ("Diabetes in pregnancy," most recently updated as a living guideline), not in the general postnatal care guideline **NG194** (published 2021, most recently updated June 2026 per NICE's own site), which this session's web search did not find to carry its own numeric hypoglycaemia threshold — NG194 appears to defer operational detail to BAPM/local guidance. NG3's recommendation, for infants of diabetic mothers specifically: blood glucose testing every 2–3 hours until pre-feed levels are maintained ≥2.0 mmol/L (36 mg/dL); escalation to tube feeding or IV dextrose only if <2.0 mmol/L (36 mg/dL) on two consecutive readings despite maximal feeding support, or immediately if the infant is symptomatic; minimum 24-hour postnatal stay. **This is worth flagging as a genuine cross-guidance divergence**: NICE NG3's single-threshold, two-consecutive-readings rule for infants of diabetic mothers is simpler than BAPM's tiered 1.0–1.9/<1.0/symptomatic structure for the broader at-risk population, and a unit following both documents needs to reconcile them explicitly for infants of diabetic mothers (who are covered by both) rather than assume they say the same thing. Neither document was independently document-verified this session (WebFetch blocked); both are cited as grey literature per web-search triangulation.

## (b) Why — physiological and methodological rationale

The Framework's design directly implements the screening/diagnostic threshold distinction established by Cornblath et al. (2000, PMID 10790476, DOI 10.1542/peds.105.5.1141): "the definition of clinically significant hypoglycemia remains one of the most confused and contentious issues in contemporary neonatology," and the paper's explicit purpose is to offer "pragmatic recommendations for operational thresholds, i.e., blood glucose levels at which clinical interventions should be considered" — deliberately not a claim about the exact level at which neuroglycopenic injury occurs. BAPM's tiered structure (a lower floor for asymptomatic infants, a higher and immediate trigger for symptomatic infants or very low readings) operationalises this Cornblath principle directly: a genuinely diagnostic threshold for injury does not exist (there is no bedside test for neuroglycopenia — see Alsweiler et al. 2022, PMID 36568425), so the Framework instead sets an intervention threshold with a margin of safety above it, by the same logic used for phototherapy thresholds in neonatal jaundice. The rationale for the specific 2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) figure traces to neurophysiological evidence, not RCT evidence: Koh et al. (1988, PMID 3202642, DOI 10.1136/adc.63.11.1353) found abnormal sensory evoked potentials in 10 of 11 children (including 5 who were clinically asymptomatic) whose blood glucose fell below 2.6 mmol/L, and no evoked-potential abnormality in the 6 who stayed above it — this is the primary neurophysiological evidence for the number itself, predating any RCT outcome data by nearly three decades.

## (c) What evidence supports each pathway element, graded

| Pathway element | Primary evidence | GRADE certainty | Downgrade domain(s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Treatment threshold (≥2.6 mmol/L/47 mg/dL as the treat-to target)** | CHYLD: treating hypoglycaemia to maintain glucose ≥2.6 mmol/L was not associated with excess combined neurosensory impairment at 2y (McKinlay et al. 2015, PMID 26465984, RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.75–1.20) or 4.5y (McKinlay et al. 2017, PMID 28783802, composite RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.77–1.21) — see Domain E for full detail, cited directly from that verified source | **Moderate** | Imprecision; no untreated comparator exists or could ethically be created — the RCT-grade evidence tests a *treat-to-threshold policy*, not the threshold's exact numeric optimality |
| **Dextrose gel as first-line/adjunct treatment of confirmed hypoglycaemia** | Sugar Babies RCT (Harris et al. 2015 2y, PMID 26613985; Harris et al. 2022 4.5y, PMID 35940872; St Clair et al. 2022 9–10y, PMID 36516806 — cited directly from Domain E); current Cochrane treatment review (Edwards et al. 2022, PMID 35302645, DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD011027.pub3): gel probably increases correction of hypoglycaemia (rate ratio 1.08, 95% CI 0.98–1.20), probably reduces mother-infant separation (RR 0.54, 95% CI 0.31–0.93), increases exclusive breastfeeding (RR 1.10, 95% CI 1.01–1.18) | **Moderate** (correction, separation, breastfeeding) / **Low** (long-term neurological disability, RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.09–2.47 — spans large benefit and large harm) / **Low** (the replicated visual-processing adverse secondary signal at 4.5y and 9–10y — see Domain E) | Imprecision (only 2 trials pooled in the Cochrane review; one included study is abstract-only with unclear-to-high risk of bias); the long-term-outcome CI is uninformatively wide |
| **Prophylactic dextrose gel (contrast case — NOT part of the BAPM treatment pathway, included for completeness)** | Current Cochrane prophylaxis review (Roberts et al. 2023, PMID 38014716, DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD012152.pub4): high-certainty reduction in hypoglycaemia incidence (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.79–0.95) but the neurological-disability estimate has **reversed direction** between editions as more follow-up data accrued (pub3, PMID 33998668: RR 0.21 favouring gel; pub4, current: RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.59–1.68, no clear effect) | **High** (incidence) / **Low** (neurological disability) | Imprecision; the review authors' own explicit conclusion is that prophylactic gel "should not be incorporated into routine practice until additional information is available about the balance of risks and harms" |
| **Risk-factor-based screening (IDM/SGA/LGA/late preterm)** | Consensus/operational only — Cornblath 2000 (PMID 10790476) established the conceptual approach; O'Brien et al. 2023 (PMID 37782488, DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2023.3957) found 18 international guidelines it appraised showed "variability in the assessed quality of guidelines and a lack of consensus between screening criteria" | **Very low** (as a *specific risk-factor list*, not as the general screening concept) | Risk of bias (no guideline, BAPM included, has been validated against a randomised screened-vs-unscreened comparison — Alsweiler et al. 2022 confirms none exists); indirectness (O'Brien's appraisal is international, not BAPM-specific) |
| **Feeding-based first-line management (early/frequent feeding as the initial response)** | Physiologically and operationally embedded in Cornblath 2000 and every appraised guideline in O'Brien 2023; no dedicated RCT of feeding-frequency-as-intervention was located in this session's searches | **Very low** (consensus/physiological reasoning, not RCT-tested as a standalone intervention) | Risk of bias (no comparator arm exists in any of the sources located); this element sits closest to "unsupported by direct RCT evidence, extrapolated from physiological reasoning" of any element in the pathway |
| **Escalation to IV dextrose when gel/feeding fail** | Roberts et al. 2024 (PMID 39536722, DOI 10.1159/000541471): systematic review found only 6 studies (2 RCTs, 4 cohort, 711 infants total) addressing IV dextrose for neonatal hypoglycaemia treatment at all; one RCT comparison (IV dextrose vs oral sucrose bolus, 80 infants) suggested reduced repeated hypoglycaemia (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.20–2.18) but possibly increased hyperglycaemia risk (RR 2.33, 95% CI 0.65–8.39) | **Very low** | Imprecision (n=80 or n=60 per comparison); risk of bias (cohort designs predominate); **this is the single weakest-evidenced step in the entire pathway** — a treatment used routinely worldwide for decades has almost no dedicated trial evidence behind its specific use for confirmed hypoglycaemia |
| **Discharge criteria (24h minimum, 3 consecutive normal readings)** | Consensus/operational only; no dedicated outcome study of discharge-timing-as-intervention was located | **Very low** | Risk of bias (no comparator); extrapolated from general newborn observation practice, not glucose-specific trial evidence |
| **Framework's real-world effect on term admissions** | Nezafat Maldonado et al. 2026 (PMID 41338963, DOI 10.1136/archdischild-2025-328872), NNRD population study: term admissions for hypoglycaemia fell from 4.9 (95% CI 4.8–5.1) to 3.3 (95% CI 3.1–3.4) per 1000 term live births comparing 36 months before vs after the April 2017 Framework, with unchanged time-to-admission and length of stay | **Moderate** (as evidence of the Framework's practical effect on admission behaviour, explicitly *not* as evidence of the threshold's neurodevelopmental safety, which is CHYLD's separate contribution) | Risk of bias (before/after design, no contemporaneous control, cannot fully exclude secular trend as an alternative explanation) — and note 46.5% of admitted infants had ≥1 BAPM risk factor, meaning the majority (53.5%) did **not**, an important caveat on how cleanly the risk-factor list predicts who is actually admitted |

## (d) Where the evidence is weak, contested, or extrapolated — the permissive-hypoglycaemia controversy

**On locating the specific "cause for concern" commentary named in the brief:** a piece titled almost exactly "Thresholds for hypoglycaemic screening — a cause for concern?" was confirmed to exist via a CORE.ac.uk repository record, and its indexed content matches the brief's description precisely — but despite PubMed title searches, multiple author-guess web searches, and direct attempts to fetch the CORE.ac.uk, Infant journal, Europe PMC, CrossRef, and Semantic Scholar records (all blocked by this session's network egress proxy), **no PMID, DOI, author name, journal name, or year could be recovered**. Per the citation-integrity rule, it is **not cited here** and is logged in full in `05-references/could-not-verify-domain-C.md`. The controversy it represents is genuine and important, so it is presented below using verified primary sources that carry the same substantive arguments.

**The critique (the "permissive hypoglycaemia" position), with its verified evidentiary basis:**
The argument is that BAPM's threshold structure — an asymptomatic floor as low as 1.0–1.9 mmol/L (18–34 mg/dL) before gel is even offered, and treatment aimed at ≥2.0–2.6 mmol/L (36–47 mg/dL) rather than a more conservative historical target — undertreats risk relative to older UK practice, and that the Framework does not adequately distinguish a *screening* threshold (deliberately set to catch a margin of at-risk infants) from a *diagnostic* threshold (the level at which injury is believed to occur), by direct analogy with jaundice/phototherapy thresholds. Two verified historical papers supply the evidentiary weight behind this position: **Koh et al. 1988** (PMID 3202642) showed neurophysiological (evoked-potential) abnormality below 2.6 mmol/L even in asymptomatic children, arguing against relaxing the floor; **Lucas et al. 1988** (PMID 2462455, DOI 10.1136/bmj.297.6659.1304) — a multicentre UK cohort of 661 **preterm** infants — found that ≥5 days of "moderate" hypoglycaemia (<2.6 mmol/L) was associated with a 3.5-fold increase (95% CI 1.3–9.4) in neurodevelopmental impairment and 13–14 point reductions in Bayley mental/motor scores at 18 months, concluding "reappraisal of current management is urgently required." **Directness flag: Lucas 1988 is a preterm cohort**, not term, and is nonetheless the single most historically influential paper behind the conservative pre-BAPM UK practice the critique is defending — using a preterm-derived alarm to argue for more caution in term management is itself an extrapolation, one the critique's proponents do not typically make explicit.

**The reassurance (the position BAPM/CHYLD evidence supports):** the randomised, prospective CHYLD evidence (Domain E, McKinlay et al. 2015/2017) directly tests a treat-to-≥2.6-mmol/L policy in the population BAPM's Framework targets and finds **no excess combined neurosensory impairment** at 2 or 4.5 years — a materially stronger evidence base (a masked, prospective, treat-to-threshold cohort with continuous interstitial monitoring) than either 1988 paper had available. Alsweiler et al. 2022 (PMID 36568425) supplies the modern, more nuanced middle position: screening for neonatal hypoglycaemia cannot cleanly satisfy classical screening-test principles because neuroglycopenia — the condition that actually matters — has no bedside diagnostic test, so blood glucose is an imperfect proxy *by construction*, not because BAPM chose the wrong number. On this reading, the two positions are answering different questions (the Koh/Lucas evidence describes what happens near and below the threshold; CHYLD describes what happens when a treat-to-threshold *policy* is actually followed), and the disagreement is not fully resolved by either evidence base — the domain-specific executive-function and visual-motor dose-response signal in CHYLD's own 4.5-year data (Domain E) is, if anything, a point of partial convergence: hypoglycaemia is not neurologically inert even under BAPM's current threshold, which is exactly what the "cause for concern" critique would predict, even though the *global* impairment measures the critique implies should be markedly worse were not.

**This is presented as a genuine, unresolved two-sided disagreement, per the brief's explicit instruction — it is not adjudicated here.**

**A second, distinct tension — treatment vs prophylaxis dextrose gel, with a directly opposite Cochrane conclusion:** the current *treatment* Cochrane review (Edwards et al. 2022, PMID 35302645) concludes oral dextrose gel is "probably an effective and safe first-line treatment"; the current *prophylaxis* Cochrane review (Roberts et al. 2023, PMID 38014716) concludes prophylactic gel "should not be incorporated into routine practice" pending more safety data, after its own neurological-disability point estimate reversed direction between its pub3 and pub4 editions (RR 0.21 favouring gel → RR 1.00, no clear effect) as more follow-up data accrued. **These are not the same clinical question and must not be conflated**: BAPM's pathway uses gel to *treat* infants who are already hypoglycaemic (the higher-certainty review), not to *prevent* hypoglycaemia in at-risk-but-currently-normoglycaemic infants (the more cautious review, corresponding to the hPOD programme in Domain E, which itself showed an adverse motor/cognitive secondary signal at 2 years).

**A third gap, reported plainly rather than papered over:** the escalation step to IV dextrose — arguably the single highest-stakes step in the pathway, since it marks admission and separation from the mother — rests on only 6 studies totalling 711 infants (Roberts et al. 2024, PMID 39536722), with very-low-certainty evidence throughout. This is a genuinely thin evidence base for a universally-practised intervention, and it is the least-evidenced element of the entire BAPM pathway.

**Generalisability caveat:** every dextrose-gel RCT located in any Cochrane review here was conducted in a high-income country; a 2021 Campbell Systematic Review of neonatal nutrition interventions in low- and middle-income countries (PMID 37133295) explicitly found **zero** eligible dextrose-gel trials from LMIC settings — the entire evidence base for this now-standard UK first-line treatment is high-income-country-derived.

## (e) Practical implication for a UK Level 3 unit

The BAPM pathway's individual elements sit on markedly uneven evidence footing, and a governance document should say so plainly rather than treat the Framework as a uniformly evidence-based document: the **treatment threshold and dextrose-gel treatment step are the two elements with genuine RCT support** (CHYLD and Sugar Babies respectively, both direct evidence in the target population); **risk-factor screening, feeding-based first-line management, and discharge criteria are consensus/operational**, extrapolated from Cornblath-style pragmatic reasoning and physiological plausibility rather than tested against a randomised comparator; and **escalation to IV dextrose is the weakest-evidenced single element**, resting on a handful of small, mostly non-randomised studies despite being the step that separates mother and baby and triggers neonatal unit admission. The real-world NNRD evidence (Nezafat Maldonado et al. 2026) shows the Framework has reduced term admissions by roughly a third without detectably increasing time-to-admission or length of stay, which is reassuring at the population level but is evidence about admission *behaviour*, not about neurodevelopmental *safety* — that reassurance comes only from CHYLD. The permissive-hypoglycaemia critique should be taken seriously as a live, evidence-literate position rather than dismissed, precisely because CHYLD's own dose-response data (Domain E) shows hypoglycaemia under the current threshold is not neurologically inert in the executive-function and visual-motor domains — the disagreement is really about how much residual, domain-specific risk is acceptable at a threshold that reliably prevents *global* impairment, not about whether BAPM "got the number wrong" in any simple sense. Finally, units following both BAPM and NICE NG3 for infants of diabetic mothers should reconcile the two documents' differently-structured thresholds explicitly in local protocol, rather than assume they are identical.
