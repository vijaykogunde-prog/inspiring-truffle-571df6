# Domain B — Point-of-care glucometer vs laboratory/blood gas confirmation

**Directness:** Device-accuracy evidence spans four decades (1997–2026) and gestational ages from extremely preterm to term, which is unusual for this review — unlike Domains D/E/G, this is **not** a domain dominated by late-preterm/term-only trial evidence extrapolated downward. However, most individual device-validation studies are small, single- or two-centre, and heterogeneous in device generation, reference method (plasma hexokinase, blood gas analyser, or older glucose-oxidase/dehydrogenase lab methods), and population (healthy term vs NICU-admitted vs very/extremely preterm). Where a finding is specific to preterm or extremely preterm infants this is flagged; where it derives from term/mixed cohorts and is being read across to a Level 3 unit's preterm caseload, that is flagged too.

## (a) What the evidence shows

### Why neonatal point-of-care glucose measurement is a recognised problem, not a hypothetical one

Point-of-care (POC) glucometers and reagent strips were developed and validated for adult self-monitoring of diabetes, where the clinically important range is the middle-to-high end of the scale and hematocrit is a narrow, adult-typical band. Neonates invert both assumptions: the clinically important range is the **low** end (hypoglycaemia, not hyperglycaemia, is the acute threat), and haematocrit is both higher and far more variable than in adults (Innanen et al. 1997, *J Pediatr*, PMID 9003866, DOI 10.1016/s0022-3476(97)70326-3; Dietzen et al. 2013, *Diabetes Technol Ther*, PMID 23931714, DOI 10.1089/dia.2013.0160). Classic and modern studies converge on the same three interfering factors: **haematocrit** (glucose oxidase and some electrochemical methods read falsely low at high haematocrit, and vice versa), **bilirubin**, and **oxygen tension/therapy** — all of which are more prevalent and more extreme in sick and preterm neonates than in the adult populations these devices were designed for (Schlebusch et al. 1998, *Pediatr Pathol Lab Med*, PMID 9566281 [no DOI in PubMed record] — four portable analysers compared in neonates, with haematocrit effects of 5–12% per 10% haematocrit change and marked temperature/oxygen effects on two of the four devices; Demers & Smith 1999, *Clin Chem*, PMID 10471670 — noting most POC glucose analysers of that era "cannot accurately test glucose below 2.22 mmol/L [40 mg/dL]" and that "their accuracy at low glucose concentrations has not been a prime consideration in their design").

### Accuracy characteristics of bedside glucometers in neonates specifically

The single best modern answer to "how accurate are POC glucometers in neonates" is **St Clair et al. 2024** (*J Pediatr*, PMID 39675663, DOI 10.1016/j.jpeds.2024.114438) — a systematic review and meta-analysis (PROSPERO CRD42023488539) of 71 studies comparing POC glucose methods against a standard laboratory reference in neonates at risk of hypoglycaemia, explicitly excluding continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) and pre-1990 studies. Across five POC chemistries (glucose oxidase [GO] + photometry or electrochemistry, glucose dehydrogenase [GDH] + photometry or electrochemistry, and hexokinase [HK] + electrochemistry), **all methods showed high specificity (≥93%)**, but sensitivity varied materially by chemistry: GO+electrochemistry, GDH+electrochemistry, and HK+electrochemistry showed superior sensitivity to the photometric methods, and the summary ROC analysis identified **HK+electrochemistry as the most accurate methodology overall**, though the authors caution that only two studies used this method and call for confirmation across more settings. This is consistent, decades later, with individual device studies using electrochemical GDH or HK chemistry (StatStrip-class devices) performing well: Tendl et al. 2013 (*Clin Chem Lab Med*, PMID 23612555, DOI 10.1515/cclm-2012-0864) found the Nova StatStrip correlated well with a blood gas analyser across 13–389 mg/dL (0.7–21.6 mmol/L) in a preterm-heavy NICU population, unaffected by haematocrit, pH, or medication; Dietzen et al. 2013 found a modified maltose-insensitive glucose dehydrogenase chemistry (ACCU-CHEK Inform II/Performa/Aviva) gave 100% sensitivity for laboratory-confirmed hypoglycaemia (<2.2 mmol/L, 40 mg/dL) when the meter reading was below 2.8 mmol/L (50 mg/dL), with only weak haematocrit dependence outside the typical neonatal range (45–70%); and a 2025 UK-relevant multicentre Japanese study (Kuboi et al., *Ann Clin Biochem*, PMID 40022493, DOI 10.1177/00045632251326460) evaluating the Glutest Mint II against a blood gas analyser across **2943 measurements in 285 infants of all gestational ages from 22 weeks onward** found very high accuracy with no discernible effect of haematocrit or bilirubin — genuinely reassuring evidence that a modern, well-chosen device *can* perform adequately across the full gestational-age range that includes extremely preterm infants, which is a rarer finding in this literature than the more commonly cited caution.

Against this, the literature also repeatedly documents **specific device generations and specific glucose ranges where accuracy is poor**: Roth-Kleiner et al. 2010 (*Eur J Pediatr*, PMID 20574734, DOI 10.1007/s00431-010-1243-2) found that **none of three tested POCT systems (Elite XL, Ascensia Contour, and the ABL 735 blood gas analyser) met ISO 15197 accuracy criteria** in 1324 paired neonatal samples, with all three systems tending to *overestimate* glycaemia and the Contour system showing important inaccuracy with increasing haematocrit; the ABL 735 blood gas analyser was the most accurate of the three but required over 15 times more blood volume than the handheld glucometers. Ngerncham et al. 2011 (*Indian Pediatr*, PMID 22317986, DOI 10.1007/s13312-012-0133-2) and the related Kitsommart et al. 2013 study (*Eur J Pediatr*, PMID 23644649, DOI 10.1007/s00431-013-2019-2) tested the StatStrip and SureStep Flexx against plasma glucose in at-risk Thai neonates: sensitivity for detecting hypoglycaemia was only **62% (StatStrip) and 53.3% (SureStep)** at a 63 mg/dL (3.5 mmol/L) cutoff in Ngerncham's cohort, and 94.7%/100% at a 2.8 mmol/L (50 mg/dL) cutoff in Kitsommart's — the wide swing in reported sensitivity for the *same devices* between these two related studies, driven by the chosen confirmation cutoff, itself illustrates why the "what reading is actionable" question does not have a single clean answer (see (d) below). A recent Korean study of three glucometers in preterm/low-birth-weight infants specifically (Hwang et al. 2015, *Korean J Pediatr*, PMID 26388895, DOI 10.3345/kjp.2015.58.8.301) found correlation with laboratory values was **lower at glucose ≤60 mg/dL (3.3 mmol/L) than above it** for all three devices, and lower in preterm/LBW infants than the adult literature generally reports — a direct, preterm-specific demonstration of the low-end accuracy problem the brief asks about. Most strikingly for a Level 3 unit, Brooks et al. 2023 (*J Diabetes Sci Technol*, PMID 37864354, DOI 10.1177/19322968231207861) compared **capillary versus arterial** blood glucose monitor (BGM) readings against a reference method in 40 preterm and ill neonates: arterial BGM readings reliably met CLSI/FDA tolerance criteria, but **27.5% of capillary BGM results fell outside tolerance**, and the paper's own title states plainly that "capillary results warrant caution for hypoglycaemia" — a distinction that matters because capillary heel-stick sampling, not arterial line sampling, is the default cot-side method for the at-risk term/late-preterm population the BAPM pathway targets.

Two very recent studies (2025–2026) sharpen this picture further and pull in different directions. Sundar et al. 2026 (*Clin Ther*, PMID 42493372, DOI 10.1016/j.clinthera.2026.06.021) tested four newer-generation glucometers (Contour Plus, Accu-Chek Instant, OneTouch Select Plus, FreeStyle Optium Neo) against ISO 15197:2013, CLSI POCT12-A3, and the stricter UNICEF evaluation framework in 199 neonates: at glucose <100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) — the clinically relevant range — all four devices met ISO 15197 criteria, and two (Accu-Chek Instant, Contour Plus) additionally met CLSI standards, but **none met the UNICEF benchmark**, and haematocrit showed only weak association with discordance (Pearson's r −0.258 to +0.196). Bond et al. 2025 (*BMC Pediatr*, PMID 40818947, DOI 10.1186/s12887-025-05934-9) took a complementary simulation approach, altering haematocrit (18%, 40%, 55%) and glucose (40 and 150 mg/dL, i.e. 2.2 and 8.3 mmol/L) in adult blood and testing 11 commercial glucometers against a YSI 2300 reference analyser for a resource-constrained-settings audience: StatStrip and StatStrip Xpress 2 ranked best on combined accuracy/precision/cost, consistent with the electrochemical-HK/GDH pattern identified by St Clair's meta-analysis. Conversely, a 2025 US comparison of POC against central-laboratory testing in inborn NICU infants (Parikh et al., *J Perinatol*, PMID 41419656, DOI 10.1038/s41372-025-02543-3) found POC **underestimated** glucose relative to the central laboratory by a mean 2.3 mg/dL (0.13 mmol/L) — the opposite direction of bias to Roth-Kleiner's overestimation finding fifteen years earlier — with the discrepancy *increasing* with postnatal age; this inconsistency in the **direction** of POC-versus-laboratory bias across studies and device generations is itself a finding, not noise, and argues against assuming any single correction factor.

### Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) is a related but distinct technology, and is not a substitute for confirmatory testing

Because glycaemic variability and preterm CGM literature could otherwise be conflated with the POC-glucometer accuracy question, it is worth stating plainly: CGM measures **interstitial**, not blood, glucose, is used for trend/variability monitoring rather than single-reading diagnosis, and has a specific, well-documented weakness for exactly the diagnostic use-case this domain concerns. Nava et al. 2020 (*BMJ Open*, PMID 33361084, DOI 10.1136/bmjopen-2020-045335), a systematic review and meta-analysis of CGM accuracy in preterm infants (PROSPERO CRD42020152248, GRADE-assessed), found pooled **sensitivity for detecting hypoglycaemia of only 0.39** (specificity 0.99), against pooled sensitivity of 0.87 (specificity 0.99) for hyperglycaemia — i.e., CGM in preterm infants is good at ruling hypoglycaemia *in* when it fires, but misses the majority of true hypoglycaemic episodes, the opposite performance profile from what would be needed to replace point-of-care/laboratory confirmation for hypoglycaemia. This matches the earlier UK NIRTURE-trial CGM validation (Beardsall et al. 2012, *Arch Dis Child Fetal Neonatal Ed*, PMID 22791467, DOI 10.1136/archdischild-2012-301661, 188 very low birth weight infants, 5207 paired readings): CGM correlated well with point-of-care glucose overall (r=0.94) and was stable over 7 days, but "accuracy of single readings to detect set thresholds of hypoglycaemia, or hyperglycaemia was poor" — the device's own authors' conclusion is that CGM is useful for **trend** information and guiding the *need* for a blood glucose check, not as a diagnostic replacement for one. A more recent very-preterm CGM accuracy study (Bonet et al. 2024, *J Pediatr*, PMID 39579867, DOI 10.1016/j.jpeds.2024.114416, 501 paired readings in 51 infants ≤32 weeks) reports an acceptable mean absolute relative difference of 7.1% against point-of-care blood glucose, but this is a comparison of CGM against POC, not against a laboratory reference, and does not bear on the POC-versus-lab confirmation question directly.

### The specific point-of-care reading below which UK guidance recommends laboratory/blood gas confirmation

The **BAPM Framework for Practice** ("Identification and Management of Neonatal Hypoglycaemia in the Full Term Infant," originally October 2017, with a 2024 revision identified during this search — see (d)) states the position directly: "Near patient testing devices tend to be less accurate in the lower range, especially <2.0 mmol/L [36 mg/dL], and therefore **all low values (≤2.6 mmol/L, 47 mg/dL) require confirmation using blood gas analysis** as this is considered the gold standard for measuring blood glucose." Handheld glucometers used for cot-side screening are additionally required to meet ISO 15197:2013 accuracy criteria and carry CE marking. This ≤2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) figure is **the single clearest, source-stated "confirm below X" number** in UK guidance, and it is not coincidentally identical to the CHYLD/Sugar Babies treatment threshold (Domain C) — the same numeric boundary does double duty as both the treatment-escalation trigger and the laboratory-confirmation trigger. Screening itself is specified as capillary POC testing performed 3-hourly, immediately before feeds. Above this figure, a point-of-care reading is treated as actionable on its own for feeding/first-line management without waiting for laboratory confirmation — this is important operationally, since delaying feeding pending a laboratory result would itself be harmful in a genuinely hypoglycaemic infant.

The UK **Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA)** device-safety guidance, "Point of care testing – Blood glucose meters," takes a more conservative position, stating that glucose meters are "unreliable in the detection of hypoglycaemia in neonates, but are accurate for values above 3 mmol/L [54 mg/dL]," and recommending that "laboratory blood glucose estimation should be used for screening of neonatal hypoglycaemia until a more reliable technique becomes available." This creates a genuine, source-documented internal tension within UK official guidance: **BAPM's clinical pathway treats ≤2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) as the confirmation trigger and accepts POC readings above that as actionable**, while **MHRA's device-safety framing treats the entire sub-3 mmol/L (54 mg/dL) range as unreliable** and would, read literally, support laboratory screening as the default rather than the exception. In practice, BAPM's clinical pathway is what governs UK NICU/postnatal-ward practice (it is the framework actually implemented and audited via the National Neonatal Research Database — see Domain A), but the discrepancy is worth stating plainly rather than silently resolving, because it reflects two different UK bodies drawing the confirmation line at different points using overlapping but not identical evidence.

The device-accuracy literature itself does not converge on a single confirmation cutoff either: Ngerncham et al. 2011 derived cutoffs of 63 mg/dL (StatStrip) and 62 mg/dL (SureStep) — both close to 3.5 mmol/L — "with 100% negative predictive value" below which confirmatory plasma glucose was recommended, materially higher than BAPM's 2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL); Dietzen et al. 2013 found 2.8 mmol/L (50 mg/dL) gave 100% sensitivity for true hypoglycaemia with their device; and CLSI POCT12-A3 itself (below) does not specify a single neonatal confirmation threshold at all, leaving this a matter of local device validation and institutional policy rather than a single evidence-derived number that transfers across devices.

### CLSI POCT12-A3 and other device-performance standards

**CLSI POCT12-A3** ("Point-of-Care Blood Glucose Testing in Acute and Chronic Care Facilities; Approved Guideline") is currently in its **Third Edition**, originally published in 2013 and **reaffirmed in September 2018**; no newer edition was identified in this session's search (conducted 2026), meaning the standard's substantive text is now thirteen years old and its most recent reaffirmation eight years old. The standard addresses programme administration, operator authorisation, meter selection and verification, and quality-assurance procedures for POC glucose testing generally, but — notably — **it is a general acute/chronic-care facility standard, not a neonatal-specific one**, and several of the accuracy studies above (Sundar 2026; Sheen et al. 2023, *Clin Ther*, PMID 37248091, DOI 10.1016/j.clinthera.2023.05.005, comparing Contour Plus/Contour Plus One accuracy in 114 neonates and 106 critically ill adults side by side) explicitly test neonatal devices *against* CLSI POCT12-A3 as one of several benchmarks (alongside ISO 15197:2013 and, in Sundar's case, a UNICEF framework), rather than the standard itself containing neonate-specific accuracy tiers. A contemporaneous methodological critique of the standard (Krouwer 2013, *J Diabetes Sci Technol*, PMID 24124969, DOI 10.1177/193229681300700532) argues that POCT12-A3's tiered accuracy structure leaves approximately 2% of results entirely unspecified for performance limits — "hard to fathom," in the author's words, given that this is exactly the tail of results (including very low values) most likely to drive clinical decisions — and recommends an error-grid-based specification instead. This is a structural, standards-level reason to treat "meets CLSI POCT12-A3" as necessary but not sufficient evidence of adequate neonatal low-range performance, independent of any individual device's study data.

No UK-specific point-of-care glucose testing standard (distinct from BAPM's clinical guidance and MHRA's device-safety guidance, both above) was identified in this search.

### Diagnosis for governance/audit purposes: point-of-care or laboratory-confirmed values?

This question does **not** have a single clean answer in the located literature, and that absence is itself the honest finding rather than a search failure. The BAPM Framework's clinical pathway is built around **point-of-care values as the operational currency of the pathway** — screening, treatment-threshold decisions, and (above the ≤2.6 mmol/L confirmation trigger) escalation are all driven by cot-side readings, with laboratory/blood gas confirmation reserved for the low-value subset. This means that, in ordinary practice, the number that determines whether a baby is treated, and the number that would be extracted into a governance or audit dataset (e.g., the National Neonatal Research Database analyses discussed in Domain A), is very often a point-of-care reading, not a laboratory-confirmed one — unless the specific episode fell into the low-value confirmation-required band, in which case both values would exist but the guidance is silent on which one should be the "of-record" figure for audit purposes. No source located in this search — BAPM, MHRA, CLSI, or the peer-reviewed accuracy literature — makes an explicit, freestanding statement of the form "governance/audit diagnosis of neonatal hypoglycaemia must be made on laboratory-confirmed values." The nearest thing to a contrary, minority position comes from a very recent (2026) Canadian retrospective study: Hamama et al. (*J Pediatr Endocrinol Metab*, PMID 42137950, DOI 10.1515/jpem-2025-0736; erratum at PMID 42387304, DOI 10.1515/jpem-2026-0438), comparing 108 NICU infants' hypoglycaemic Nova StatStrip readings against confirmatory central-laboratory serum glucose, found strong correlation (r=0.825) with the point-of-care meter reading systematically *higher* than the laboratory value (Deming regression slope 1.223), and concludes — provocatively, and against the mainstream of this literature — that this "questions the need for confirmatory serum samples in the presence of bedside hypoglycemic results," since a systematic positive bias in the meter means a low POC reading is, if anything, more likely to be a true or worse hypoglycaemic value than a falsely low one. This is a single small retrospective single-centre study using one specific device (StatStrip, already established above as one of the better-performing chemistries) and should not be read as displacing BAPM's confirmation requirement; but it is a genuine, recent, peer-reviewed voice arguing the opposite direction to the conventional "always confirm low values" position, and per the brief's rule on giving contested findings fair prominence it is reported here rather than omitted.

## (b) Why — physiological and methodological rationale

The physiological basis for reduced device accuracy in neonates rests on three converging factors, all well established in the biochemistry of these assays rather than being a mere empirical curiosity. First, **haematocrit interference**: most POC chemistries (glucose oxidase and several glucose dehydrogenase formulations) partition glucose between plasma and red cells during the brief reaction time of a strip-based assay, so at neonatal-typical haematocrit (commonly 45–70%, well above the healthy adult range) the true whole-blood-to-plasma glucose ratio is altered in ways the device's calibration — built for adult haematocrit — does not correctly compensate for (Dietzen et al. 2013; Schlebusch et al. 1998). Second, **the low end of the measurement range is inherently the hardest part of any colorimetric or electrochemical assay to resolve precisely**, because the signal-to-noise ratio of the enzymatic reaction degrades as substrate concentration falls, which is why device manufacturers — whose primary market and validation population is adult diabetic hyperglycaemia management — have historically had little commercial incentive to optimise performance in the 1.5–3.0 mmol/L (27–54 mg/dL) range that is central to neonatal practice but peripheral to adult diabetes care (Demers & Smith 1999; Lang & Hussain 2014, *Adv Clin Chem*, PMID 24783355, DOI 10.1016/b978-0-12-800094-6.00006-6). Third, **capillary heel-stick sampling itself introduces additional variance beyond the device chemistry** — peripheral vasoconstriction, sample dilution with interstitial fluid, and clotting/haemolysis artefacts from a slow, squeezed capillary draw are all more prevalent in sick, cold, or hypoperfused neonates, which is the mechanistic reason Brooks et al. 2023 found capillary readings materially less reliable than arterial readings using the *same* meter and the *same* infants — the discrepancy is in the sample, not only the device. Electrochemical hexokinase- and glucose-dehydrogenase-based methods (StatStrip-class devices) outperform photometric glucose-oxidase methods in the St Clair 2024 meta-analysis at least in part because hexokinase is intrinsically less sensitive to the maltose/galactose cross-reactivity and oxygen-tension dependence that affect older photometric and some electrochemical GO chemistries (Warner et al. 2011, *Diabetes Technol Ther*, PMID 21175272, DOI 10.1089/dia.2010.0129, explicitly flagging Accu-Chek's older maltose-sensitive strips as unsafe for neonatal use for this reason).

## (d) Where the evidence is weak, contested, or extrapolated

Four genuine tensions and gaps stand out, none resolved by the current literature:

1. **No single, universally evidence-derived "confirm below X" number exists, despite BAPM stating one clearly.** BAPM's ≤2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) is the clearest and most operationally important figure in this domain — it is stated plainly, it is the figure a UK unit should use, and it usefully coincides with the CHYLD/Sugar Babies treatment threshold (Domain C) — but it is a consensus/operational choice, not a value derived from a device-accuracy study testing that specific cutoff. The peer-reviewed device literature independently derives higher cutoffs (2.8–3.5 mmol/L / 50–63 mg/dL, depending on device and study) using formal sensitivity/NPV analysis against a laboratory reference, and the UK's own MHRA guidance implies an even more conservative >3 mmol/L (54 mg/dL) reliability floor. This should be stated plainly to a UK Level 3 unit rather than papered over: BAPM's number is the one to follow operationally, but it is not the same number the device-accuracy literature would derive if asked "at what point-of-care reading is the negative predictive value for true hypoglycaemia adequate."

2. **The direction of point-of-care bias relative to the laboratory reference is inconsistent across studies and eras**, which undermines any simple "POC reads X higher/lower than lab, so correct by Y" heuristic. Older studies (Roth-Kleiner 2010) found systematic overestimation; a recent study (Parikh 2025) found systematic underestimation with a different device in a different population; Hamama 2026 found overestimation again with StatStrip specifically. This is most plausibly explained by genuine device-generation and population heterogeneity rather than by a single underlying truth the literature has been converging on — it has not converged, and that should be stated rather than resolved by picking the most recent study as if it were definitive.

3. **The Hamama 2026 contrarian finding sits in real tension with the BAPM confirmation requirement and with the great majority of the accuracy literature**, and is not resolved here. It is a single, small (n=108), retrospective, single-device, single-centre study; the great weight of evidence (St Clair 2024's 71-study meta-analysis; the low-end-accuracy-degradation literature; Brooks 2023's capillary-specific caution) supports continuing to require confirmation of low point-of-care readings before treatment escalation, and BAPM's guidance should not be read as under active peer-reviewed challenge on the strength of this one paper. But per the brief's instruction to give contested and minority findings fair prominence, it is flagged rather than silently dismissed: it is a genuine, recent, peer-reviewed voice, from a specialist neonatal endocrinology/laboratory-medicine collaboration, arguing that routine confirmatory serum sampling may be unnecessary overhead given a well-performing modern device — a position that, if replicated at scale, would have material workflow and infant-comfort implications (fewer repeat blood draws) for UK practice.

4. **The governance/audit-diagnosis question is a genuine evidence gap, not merely an underexplored one.** No guideline or accuracy study located in this search directly addresses whether a governance dataset (e.g., NNRD-derived audit of admissions for hypoglycaemia, as used in the Domain A real-world evaluation of the BAPM Framework) should, or in practice does, distinguish point-of-care from laboratory-confirmed values when recording an episode of hypoglycaemia. This matters because the practical answer — that most recorded episodes are point-of-care values by default, with laboratory confirmation only for the subset that fell in the low-value confirmation band — has real implications for how audit incidence figures should be interpreted (a device with a positive bias, as several studies above found, would tend to *under*-record hypoglycaemia in audit data; a device with a negative bias would over-record it), but this implication does not appear to have been tested empirically anywhere in this literature.

**Directness caveat specific to this domain:** unlike most other domains in this review, the underlying device-accuracy evidence base here is *not* concentrated in a single late-preterm/term-at-risk trial programme — it spans healthy term infants, at-risk NICU populations, and (via Kuboi 2025, Tendl 2013, Hwang 2015, Beardsall 2012, Bonet 2024, Nava 2020) preterm and very preterm infants directly. This is a genuine strength of this domain relative to Domains D and E. The caveat that remains is that CLSI POCT12-A3 and ISO 15197:2013 are themselves general (adult/paediatric, not neonate-specific) standards being applied, sometimes awkwardly, to a neonatal population — the standards were not derived from neonatal data, even where individual studies test neonatal devices against them.

## (e) Practical implication for a UK Level 3 unit

Three points should govern local practice and any local point-of-care policy document. First, **the BAPM ≤2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) confirmation threshold is the number to operationalise** — a point-of-care reading above this level is actionable on its own for feeding/first-line management without waiting for a laboratory or blood gas result (avoiding a treatment delay that would itself be harmful), while a reading at or below it should trigger blood gas or laboratory confirmation before further escalation, consistent with both BAPM guidance and the weight of the device-accuracy literature (even though that literature would, on its own diagnostic-accuracy terms, sometimes derive a slightly higher cutoff). Second, **device selection matters materially and should not be assumed generic**: the evidence favours electrochemical hexokinase/glucose-dehydrogenase chemistry (StatStrip-class devices) over older photometric glucose-oxidase devices, particularly for the extremes of haematocrit and the low glucose range relevant to a Level 3 unit's preterm caseload, and a unit should confirm its own in-use device has been validated against a neonatal (not only adult) reference population — the 2025 Shikoku multicentre study (Kuboi et al.) is the most reassuring single piece of evidence that a well-chosen modern device can perform adequately even in extremely preterm infants, but this has not yet been independently replicated outside Japan. Third, **capillary sampling technique is a source of error independent of the device itself**: given Brooks et al.'s finding that over a quarter of capillary readings fell outside tolerance criteria in preterm/ill neonates even when the same device performed reliably on arterial samples, technique (adequate perfusion, avoiding excessive squeezing, prompt analysis) deserves as much governance attention as device choice. Finally, **CGM should not be adopted, and should not be assumed by staff, to substitute for point-of-care or laboratory confirmation of a hypoglycaemic episode** — its evidence base supports a trend-monitoring and screening-adjunct role (flagging when a confirmatory blood glucose check is needed) but not a diagnostic one, given its documented poor sensitivity for discrete hypoglycaemic events.
