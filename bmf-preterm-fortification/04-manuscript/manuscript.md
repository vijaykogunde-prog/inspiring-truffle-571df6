# Fortifying human milk for preterm infants: what the evidence actually supports, and where it runs out

## Summary

Fortification of human milk for preterm infants is near-universal practice in high-income neonatal units, but the trial evidence supporting *when*, *how much*, and *with what product* to fortify is thinner, more inconsistent, and more contested than routine practice implies. Multi-nutrient fortification reliably increases short-term growth velocity by a modest margin, with no clear effect on necrotising enterocolitis (NEC) risk in either direction. Randomised evidence on timing (feed-volume threshold versus postnatal day) shows no consistent growth or safety advantage to starting earlier, and one meta-analysis links early fortification to a longer hospital stay. No trial has ever tested fortification timing specifically in infants with antenatal absent or reversed end-diastolic flow (AEDF/REDF), and no trial or current guideline addresses when to reintroduce fortifier after surgical NEC or spontaneous intestinal perforation (SIP) — both areas of practice rest on extrapolation, not evidence. Whether fortifier *source* (bovine versus human-milk-derived) matters for NEC or mortality is genuinely unresolved: three meta-analyses suggest a benefit for human-milk-derived fortifier, but the largest and most rigorous single trial to test the same question directly found no difference. Targeted and individualised fortification improves growth in pooled analysis, but one trial attempting a modular approach was stopped early for feeding intolerance. No randomised trial of any fortification variable — whether to fortify, when, with what source, or how individualised — has yet shown a clear neurodevelopmental benefit.

## Key points

- Fortification improves short-term growth velocity by a modest, consistent margin (roughly 1.8–2.7 g/kg/day extra weight gain) but has not been shown to improve neurodevelopmental outcome in any adequately designed trial.
- The evidence on *when* to start fortification (feed-volume threshold or postnatal day) does not support a strong practice preference either way; one meta-analysis links earlier fortification to longer hospital stay.
- Current practice for infants with antenatal AEDF/REDF, and for reintroducing fortifier after surgical NEC/SIP, rests on extrapolation from adjacent literatures (feed-initiation trials, epidemiological association studies, general enteral-nutrition guidelines) — no fortification-specific trial exists for either scenario.
- Whether human-milk-derived fortifier reduces NEC or mortality compared with bovine-derived fortifier is unresolved: three meta-analyses suggest benefit, but the largest single RCT (N-forte) found none.
- An acidified liquid fortifier carries a clear metabolic-acidosis signal compared with a non-acidified alternative in one adequately sized trial — a product-chemistry finding independent of dose or source.

## Introduction

Preterm infants fed exclusively on mother's own or donor human milk accumulate a nutrient deficit relative to intrauterine accretion rates, because mature human milk's protein, energy and mineral density falls short of a very preterm infant's requirements. Multi-nutrient fortification — adding protein, energy, calcium, phosphate and other micronutrients to expressed milk — has become standard practice in most level-3 neonatal units, including across the UK, on the plausible premise that closing this nutrient gap will improve growth and, ultimately, later development.

What is currently done, however, varies more than the confidence with which it is described. Units differ on the feed volume or postnatal day at which fortification starts, on which product is used (bovine-derived powder or liquid, human-milk-derived, or increasingly individualised/targeted preparations), on how cautiously to proceed in infants with antenatal Doppler abnormality or a prior episode of NEC, and on whether to continue fortification after discharge. Much of this variation is defended by appeal to "the evidence," yet the underlying trial base for several of these decisions is either genuinely thin or has simply never been tested in the population where the decision is hardest to make.

This review asks, domain by domain, what the trial and guideline evidence actually supports for the timing, dose, product choice and safety of human milk fortification, and — as importantly — where current practice is extrapolation rather than evidence. It deliberately keeps two questions separate that are frequently conflated in the secondary literature: *whether and when to fortify at all*, and, if fortifying, *bovine-derived versus human-milk-derived product*. Throughout, **HMF** is used as the general term for human/breast milk fortifier; **BMF** (or BMBF, bovine milk-based fortifier) is reserved specifically for the bovine-derived product, exactly as several of the trials discussed below (notably the Nordic N-forte trial) themselves define it, to avoid collapsing these two distinct clinical questions into one. What is deliberately out of scope: general trophic/minimal enteral feeding and feed-advancement-rate literature not specific to fortification, and the nutrient-enriched-*formula* literature for formula-fed infants, which is a different intervention from continuing a human-milk fortifier and is noted only where needed to prevent confusion with it.

## Search methods

PubMed and Consensus (a claim-search engine spanning PubMed, Semantic Scholar and Scopus) were searched without date restriction, in English, using concept blocks for population (preterm/VLBW/ELBW), intervention (human/breast milk fortification, including bovine- and human-milk-derived product terms), and eight secondary-question domains (timing/dose; AEDF/REDF and IUGR/SGA; post-surgical NEC/SIP; short-term growth; neurodevelopment; GI tolerance and osmolality; post-discharge fortification; concentrated/targeted/product-comparison strategies). Cochrane reviews and included RCTs were citation-chained forward and backward. Forty-nine studies were retained for extraction across the eight domains, each independently confirmed by PMID this session; none of the seed literature named in the original scoping pass failed verification. ## Does fortification help, and by how much?

The core question — does fortifying human milk improve growth compared with leaving it unfortified — has the field's most consistent answer. The current Cochrane review (18 RCTs, 1,456 infants) found low-to-moderate certainty evidence that multi-nutrient fortification increases in-hospital weight gain (mean difference [MD] 1.76 g/kg/day, 95% CI 1.30 to 2.22), linear growth (MD 0.11 cm/week, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.15) and head-circumference growth (MD 0.06 cm/week, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.08), with low-certainty evidence of no clear effect on NEC risk in either direction (risk ratio [RR] 1.37, 95% CI 0.72 to 2.63) [Brown 2020, PMID 35658821]. A 2026 update using a newer trial-trustworthiness screening tool (INSPECT-SR) alongside standard risk-of-bias assessment pooled 15 RCTs (1,079 infants) and found somewhat larger point estimates for the same growth parameters, but its own authors graded the evidence low certainty because of substantial unexplained heterogeneity and because the trustworthiness screen flagged concerns in 13 of the 15 included trials [Rallis 2026, PMID 42451102] — a genuinely useful caution that the apparently growing effect size across successive updates may partly reflect evolving trial-quality issues rather than a strengthening true effect. A 2025 network meta-analysis of 59 studies ranked a mother's-own-milk-plus-donor-milk-plus-human-milk-based-fortifier regimen top for mortality, NEC and time to full feeds, while its own authors concluded there remains insufficient high-quality evidence to support or refute any single regimen definitively [Campbell-Yeo 2025, PMID 40431391].

The honest summary: fortification's growth-velocity benefit is real, modest, and consistently replicated across three independent pooled analyses spanning three decades of trials; its effect on NEC risk is genuinely uncertain rather than reassuring, with confidence intervals that comfortably straddle unity; and — critically, addressed fully below — the growth benefit has not been shown to translate into a neurodevelopmental one.

## When to start, and does the volume threshold matter?

Two variables are manipulated across the timing trials and should not be conflated: the *feed-volume threshold* at which fortifier is introduced (as low as 20 mL/kg/day versus the conventional ~100 mL/kg/day), and the *postnatal day* of introduction, independent of feed volume. The Cochrane review of early-versus-late fortification pools only two RCTs (237 infants) and finds no effect on growth, time to regain birth weight, or NEC, graded low certainty for risk of bias and imprecision [Thanigainathan 2020, PMID 32726863]; a predecessor systematic review reaches the same narrative conclusion from the same two trials [Alyahya 2019, PMID 31326969]. A separate meta-analysis of three RCTs (309 infants) found no growth difference between early and late fortification but a **significantly longer hospital stay with early fortification** (MD 4.29 days, 95% CI 0.84 to 7.75), alongside a non-significant trend toward more feed intolerance and NEC — a null-on-growth, positive-on-harm finding that deserves at least as much weight as the more numerous smaller positive reports elsewhere in this literature [Basu 2020, PMID 32458060]. The originally cited RCT for the 20-versus-100 mL/kg/day comparison found no difference in days to full feeding or feeding-intolerance episodes, with higher early protein intake as its only positive finding [Shah 2016, PMID 27112041].

More recent trials shift toward postnatal-day manipulation and body-composition endpoints. In extremely preterm infants (≤28 weeks), fortifying with a human-milk-based product from feeding day 2 did not improve the primary fat-free-mass outcome at 36 weeks postmenstrual age, though secondary length-gain and head-circumference findings favoured earlier fortification [Salas 2023, PMID 37551512]. A second trial comparing postnatal day 4–7 against day 10–14 (feed volume held similar between arms) was likewise null on its primary fat-free-mass outcome, with secondary weight, fat-free-mass and length gains favouring earlier fortification [Salas 2025, PMID 39986385]. Neither trial supports a strong timing recommendation on its primary endpoint; both leave open, via secondary findings, the possibility of a modest benefit that larger trials might confirm or refute.

## Special populations: antenatal Doppler abnormality and growth restriction

This is where the evidence-to-practice gap is widest. No randomised trial has ever tested fortification timing, dose or safety specifically in infants with antenatal absent or reversed end-diastolic umbilical artery flow (AEDF/REDF) or in growth-restricted infants more broadly. What exists instead is a feed-*initiation* trial tradition and an epidemiological literature establishing why caution is biologically plausible, neither of which answers the fortification question directly.

The ADEPT trial (404 infants, UK and Ireland) randomised growth-restricted infants with abnormal antenatal Doppler to start *enteral feeds* — not fortifier — on day 2 versus day 6, finding earlier feeding reached full sustained feeds sooner with no increase in NEC and several secondary benefits [Leaf 2012, PMID 22492770]. A pre-specified subgroup analysis of the same cohort found infants below 29 weeks tolerated the identical protocol far worse than those at or above 29 weeks (39% versus 10% NEC on the same regimen) [Kempley 2013, PMID 23973795] — a direct demonstration that even a feed-*initiation* rule does not transfer uniformly across gestational ages within this exact high-risk population, let alone a fortification rule extrapolated from it. A retrospective Doppler-stratified cohort found a clear severity gradient of feeding intolerance, NEC and SIP by Doppler pattern (worst in combined umbilical-artery-plus-ductus-venosus AREDF), confirming the underlying risk gradient but reporting nothing about fortification exposure [Martini 2022, PMID 36501150]. The mechanistic case for caution traces to two small, decades-old UK studies showing markedly higher NEC rates with AREDF independent of prematurity or growth restriction (53% versus 6% in one; 22% versus 6% in a smaller replication) [Malcolm 1991, PMID 1863128; Wilson 1991, PMID 1776906] — foundational and mechanistically important, but containing no fortification data whatsoever and pre-dating modern fortification practice entirely.

The practice of waiting for full feeds and clinical stability before fortifying an AEDF/REDF or growth-restricted infant is, on this evidence, a reasonable and precaution-driven extrapolation — but an extrapolation nonetheless, not a directly evidenced fortification-specific practice. This should be stated to trainees and to families as plainly as it is stated here.

## After necrotising enterocolitis and surgical NEC/SIP

The picture here is, if anything, starker. Restarting *any* enteral feeding after medical NEC has at least some direct evidence: a single-centre cohort restarting feeds once portal venous gas had cleared on ultrasound for three consecutive days (median 4 days post-onset) found shorter time to full feeds, shorter central-line duration, less catheter sepsis and shorter hospital stay than a historical, later-restart comparison group, with no clear increase in NEC recurrence — though the authors themselves state their study was underpowered to exclude a higher recurrence risk [Bohnhorst 2003, PMID 14571225]. A systematic review of nutrition and SIP, drawn overwhelmingly (16 of 19 included studies) from historical-control designs, found that earlier enteral nutrition (within the first three days of life) was associated with reduced SIP incidence in three studies and reduced mortality/neurodevelopmental impairment in two, addressing only feed *initiation* [Olaloye 2020, PMID 32397283].

*When to add fortifier* once feeds have restarted after medical or surgical NEC, and *whether fortifier source affects recurrence risk*, are essentially unaddressed. Full-text review of the two most current guideline/position-paper documents in this exact area — the 2024 European (ERNICA) surgical NEC guideline [Hulscher/Irvine 2024, PMID 39925108] and the 2026 Italian multi-society position paper on post-operative neonatal GI-surgery nutrition [De Rose 2026, PMID 41519795] — confirms that neither contains a fortifier-specific timing or dosing recommendation distinct from general enteral-feeding advice, despite both addressing post-NEC/post-surgical nutrition in detail. This corroborated silence, from two independent and current multidisciplinary expert efforts, is itself the finding for this domain: whatever a given unit's local practice is for reintroducing fortifier after surgical NEC or SIP, it is unit-level clinical judgement, not an evidence-based or even a consensus-guideline-based protocol.

## Does fortifier source matter — bovine or human-milk-derived?

Three meta-analyses converge on a possible advantage for human-milk-derived fortifier over bovine-derived (BMF) product on necrotising enterocolitis and mortality, but the largest and most rigorously conducted single trial testing the same comparison directly found no difference — a discrepancy that should be reported prominently rather than resolved by preferring the more numerous positive syntheses.

A meta-analysis of six RCTs (334 infants) found lower NEC (RR 0.38, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.95) and surgical NEC (RR 0.13, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.67) with human-milk-derived fortifier, but a lower weight-gain rate than with bovine fortifier (MD −1.08 g/kg/day) — a genuine trade-off, and one whose NEC benefit **lost statistical significance** in a sensitivity analysis excluding higher-risk-of-bias studies [Ananthan 2020, PMID 32277813]. A closely related two-trial pooled analysis found a consistent NEC-reduction direction (RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.98) [Grace 2020, PMID 32943531]. A four-trial meta-analysis restricted to extremely preterm/ELBW infants on an exclusive human-milk diet found a significant **mortality** reduction with human-milk-derived fortifier (RR 0.50, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.94) [Galiș 2024, PMID 38542821].

Set against all three is the Nordic N-forte trial — 228 extremely preterm infants, the largest and most rigorously blinded/adjudicated individual RCT of this exact comparison — which found **no difference** in the composite outcome of NEC, sepsis or mortality between human-milk-based and bovine-based fortifier (odds ratio 1.05, 95% CI 0.61 to 1.81) [Jensen 2024, PMID 38545091]. The parent RCT underlying an 18-month neurodevelopmental follow-up likewise found no difference between fortifier sources on feeding tolerance or a composite morbidity/mortality index [O'Connor 2018, PMID 29878061], and that same trial's follow-up found no difference in Bayley-III cognitive, language or motor scores at 18 months between fortifier sources [Hopperton/O'Connor 2019, PMID 32154499]. Whether human-milk-derived fortifier meaningfully reduces NEC or mortality compared with bovine fortifier is, on the totality of this evidence, genuinely unresolved — not a settled positive finding awaiting wider adoption.

## Getting the dose right: concentrated, targeted, and product-chemistry considerations

Concentrated liquid preterm formula used as a fortifier has reasonable RCT-level support as an alternative to standard powder: a blinded multicentre trial (150 infants) found significantly higher achieved weight and linear growth with an ultraconcentrated liquid fortifier compared with standard powder, with no difference in feeding tolerance, sepsis or NEC [Moya 2012, PMID 22987877].

Individualised or "targeted" fortification — adjusting the fortifier dose to measured or inferred milk composition rather than a fixed recipe — shows a genuine growth benefit in the most current pooled synthesis (12 RCTs, 575 infants): significantly greater weight, length, head-circumference and fat-free-mass gain with individualised fortification, though effects on mortality, NEC and 18-month neurodevelopment remain "very uncertain" by the reviewers' own explicit statement [Bell 2026, PMID 42391159]. Set directly against this pooled growth benefit is a cautionary single-trial finding that must not be reduced to a footnote: a trial of modular, ingredient-by-ingredient targeted fortification was **stopped early after only 39 of a planned 220 infants were enrolled, because of serious feeding intolerance in five infants** [Seliga-Siwecka 2023, PMID 36771325]. Read together, these findings tell a coherent story — individualised fortification can improve growth when it is deliverable, but a specific modular approach to delivering it failed on feasibility and safety grounds in routine practice, and the pooled evidence's own authors do not claim a demonstrated long-term clinical benefit.

A separate, easily overlooked product-chemistry finding concerns liquid fortifier acidification: a 164-infant multicentre RCT found that an acidified liquid fortifier was associated with substantially more metabolic acidosis than a non-acidified alternative (27% versus 5% of infants), alongside more vomiting, gastric residuals and abdominal distension, and lower early weight gain [Schanler 2018, PMID 30195561]. This is a moderate-certainty, clinically actionable single-trial finding independent of both the dose and source-type questions above, and is easily missed if a literature search is framed only around "how much" rather than "what chemical form."

## Tolerance, constipation, and the osmolality question

Constipation is poorly captured as a named outcome in most fortification trials, typically subsumed under "feeding intolerance" or "abdominal distension." One trial did measure it directly and prospectively: comparing bovine colostrum with a conventional bovine fortifier, stools hardened with increasing fortifier *dose* in both study arms — confirming that fortification-associated stool hardening is a real, dose-dependent, measurable phenomenon regardless of product, though significantly less pronounced with colostrum [Kappel 2022, PMID 36432444]. A retrospective cohort found no overall association between HMF use and feeding intolerance, but identified early initiation, full-strength initiation, and rapid supplementation rate as independent risk factors, with dose-response thresholds for fortifier concentration and rate of increase [Zhang 2022, PMID 36364872].

The mechanistic literature — measuring how fortification raises feed osmolality, and how commonly co-administered medications add further osmotic load on top of it [Herranz Barbero 2020, PMID 32479524; Choi 2016, PMID 26863130; Chandran 2016, PMID 28030867] — must be kept conceptually separate from this clinical-outcome literature. The American Academy of Pediatrics' commonly cited 450 mOsm/kg ceiling for enteral feeds is a **consensus safety threshold arrived at by expert judgement, not a value validated against hard clinical outcomes in any randomised comparison**. No trial has randomised infants to different osmolality bands and measured clinical GI outcomes; this distinction between a sensible precautionary ceiling and an evidence-validated one should not be blurred.

## Does any of this affect the brain?

This is the domain most exposed to conflating a growth surrogate with the outcome that actually matters, and it deserves the plainest possible statement: **no randomised trial of any fortification variable has yet demonstrated a clear neurodevelopmental benefit.**

Following 214 very preterm infants to 6 years corrected age, post-discharge fortification of mother's own milk did not improve full-scale IQ compared with unfortified milk (median 104 versus 105.5, P=0.29), nor any subdomain of a validated developmental questionnaire — a clean, long-follow-up, clinically important null [Klamer 2022, PMID 35807888]. The 18-month follow-up of the fortifier-*source* RCT above found no difference in any Bayley-III domain between human-milk-based and bovine-based fortifier [Hopperton/O'Connor 2019, PMID 32154499]. A trial of individualised versus standard fortification found real growth benefits but, by its own authors' account, mostly non-significant Bayley-III differences at 18 months, with one exception in a protein-stratified subgroup [Rochow 2025, PMID 40507033]. The single trial best positioned to give a definitive, adequately powered answer on fortifier source and neurodevelopment — the N-forte trial's planned 2- and 5.5-year follow-up — has not yet been published as of this search [Jensen 2021 protocol, PMID 34815288].

None of this is an argument against fortification, which retains its modest, well-replicated growth benefit and, per the domain above, an unresolved-but-plausible NEC/mortality signal for at least one product comparison. It is an argument for describing fortification's neurodevelopmental benefit to families and trainees exactly as the evidence supports it: unproven, not merely "not yet fully quantified."

## What happens after discharge?

The post-discharge literature must be read carefully because two different interventions are frequently conflated: nutrient-enriched *formula* for formula-fed infants (a Cochrane review of 16 RCTs, 1,251 infants, found inconsistent benefit from "postdischarge formula" but clearer growth benefit from full "preterm formula" against standard term formula, with few neurodevelopmental assessments and no differences detected in those performed [Young/Walsh 2016, PMID 27958643]) is a different question from continuing a human-milk fortifier on breastfed infants at home.

For the latter, genuine question, the evidence is consistently null on growth and neurodevelopment, with one signal favouring a different kind of benefit. A randomised comparison of fortified versus unfortified mother's milk from shortly before discharge to 4 months corrected age found no difference in growth at 12 months [Zachariassen 2011, PMID 21402642] — a finding that sits alongside Klamer's 6-year null IQ result above, from what may be a related trial tradition. A UK quality-improvement project found improved growth trajectories after introducing home fortifier use, but as an uncontrolled before-after audit this cannot be confidently attributed to the intervention rather than secular practice change [Marino 2018, PMID 30552093]. A US retrospective comparison found that continuing commercial fortifier at home, rather than switching to formula-based enrichment, was associated with significantly higher rates of continued human-milk feeding — with no difference in growth [Lamport 2023, PMID 38072151]. Taken together, the case for continuing HMF at home may rest more convincingly on preserving breastfeeding continuation than on any demonstrated growth or developmental advantage.

## Limitations

This review is narrative rather than systematic: searches, while broad and citation-chained through Cochrane reviews and included RCTs, were not dual-screened, and study selection involved judgement rather than a pre-registered protocol. The evidence base itself carries a specific, recurring design weakness worth naming rather than gesturing at generically: several of the domain's most-cited single-centre positive findings (the UK quality-improvement project on home fortification, the Taiwanese cohort on concentrated liquid fortifier as a rescue strategy) are before-after or historical-control designs vulnerable to secular-trend confounding, while the field's few adequately blinded, adequately powered RCTs (N-forte, the Cochrane reviews' constituent trials) are disproportionately null or trade-off findings — a pattern consistent with, rather than contradicting, the well-recognised distortion of underpowered single-centre positive reporting in neonatal nutrition research generally. English-language restriction was not independently confirmed as a search constraint and should be treated as a real, unquantified limitation. Applicability to a UK level-3 setting is generally reasonable for the general-VLBW evidence (the dominant trial populations), but the resource-limited-setting framing of some product-substitution trials (e.g., preterm formula powder as a cost-driven fortifier alternative) limits their direct relevance where cost is not the binding constraint.

## Conclusions and implications for practice

Fortification of preterm human milk retains a solid basis for its core, modest growth-velocity benefit, and this review does not argue against continuing it as routine practice. What the evidence does not support is confidence about several of the decisions clinicians make around that core practice. The timing threshold within the 20–100 mL/kg/day range makes little demonstrated difference and may carry a length-of-stay cost if started very early; whether bovine or human-milk-derived fortifier better protects against NEC or death is unresolved by the single largest trial designed to answer exactly that question; targeted fortification improves growth but has failed, in at least one careful attempt, on feasibility grounds; and no fortification variable of any kind has yet been shown to improve neurodevelopmental outcome. Two areas of everyday clinical practice — fortification in infants with antenatal Doppler abnormality, and fortifier reintroduction after surgical NEC or SIP — are conducted entirely on extrapolation and clinical judgement, a fact that should be stated to trainees and, where relevant, to families, rather than left to be assumed as evidence-based. None of this is a call to abandon current practice; it is a call to describe its evidence base with the precision the practice itself does not currently receive.

## Research gaps

1. An adequately powered randomised trial of fortification timing (not merely feed initiation) specifically in infants with antenatal AEDF/REDF or Doppler-abnormal growth restriction — the single largest identified extrapolation gap in this literature.
2. Publication of the N-forte trial's planned 2- and 5.5-year neurodevelopmental follow-up (human-milk-based versus bovine-based fortifier, extremely preterm infants), which would be the first adequately powered, long-term neurodevelopmental readout for fortifier source.
3. Completion and reporting of the ongoing three-arm MaxiMoM InForM trial (standard versus target versus blood-urea-nitrogen-adjustable fortification, planned n=615, Bayley-IV cognitive score at 18–24 months as primary outcome) [Beggs 2025, PMID 41047263] — currently the only adequately powered trial in progress with a hard long-term endpoint for individualised fortification.
4. Independent replication of the acidified-versus-non-acidified liquid fortifier metabolic-acidosis signal, currently resting on a single trial.
5. Any prospective (rather than historical-control) study — even an observational multicentre registry study via national neonatal audit infrastructure — characterising current practice and outcomes for fortifier reintroduction timing after surgical NEC or SIP, an area with essentially no data of any design to build on.
