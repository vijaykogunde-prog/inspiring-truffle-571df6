# Domain 1 — Timing and volume threshold for initiation of human milk fortification

## (a) What the trials show, and how consistent it is

The direct trial evidence is thin and consistently null. The Cochrane review (Thanigainathan & Abiramalatha, 2020; PMID 32726863) pools only two RCTs (237 VLBW infants total) comparing fortification started at low feed volumes (20 mL/kg/d in one trial, 40 mL/kg/d in the other) against fortification started at 100 mL/kg/d in both. Meta-analysis shows no effect on time to regain birth weight (MD −0.06 d, 95% CI −1.32 to 1.20), linear growth velocity (MD 0.10 cm/wk, 95% CI −0.03 to 0.22) or head-growth velocity (MD −0.01 cm/wk, 95% CI −0.07 to 0.06), and no effect on NEC — all graded low certainty by the review authors for risk of bias (unblinded) and imprecision. The predecessor systematic review (Alyahya et al. 2019; PMID 31326969), drawing on the same underlying two-trial evidence base (171 infants), reaches the same conclusion by narrative synthesis: no significant impact of early versus delayed fortification on any outcome, with inconsistent EF/DF definitions between the two trials.

The one meta-analysis to find a *directional* signal points the wrong way for early fortification: Basu et al. (2020; PMID 32458060), pooling three RCTs (309 infants), found no growth benefit from early fortification but a **significantly longer hospital stay** with early fortification (MD 4.29 days, 95% CI 0.84–7.75), alongside a non-significant trend toward more feed intolerance and NEC. The authors themselves grade this very-low certainty, but it is the single most quotable null-or-negative finding in this domain and must not be diluted by the more numerous small positive reports elsewhere in the literature.

The foundational RCT most commonly cited as the "20 vs 100 mL/kg/d" trial (Shah et al. 2016; PMID 27112041, n=100) found no difference in days to full feeding volume (20 vs 20 days, P=.45) or feeding-intolerance episode count (58 vs 57) — its only significant finding was higher early protein intake with early fortification, a nutrient-delivery surrogate, not a growth or safety outcome.

More recent trials shift the question toward *postnatal day* rather than feed volume, and toward body-composition endpoints rather than simple weight. Salas et al. (2023; PMID 37551512, n=150, extremely preterm ≤28 weeks) randomised human-milk-based fortification from feeding day 2 against standard (later, bovine) fortification, with fat-free-mass (FFM) z-score at 36 weeks PMA as the primary outcome — null on the primary outcome, with secondary signals toward higher length-gain velocity and smaller head-circumference z-score decline in the early group. Salas et al. (2025; PMID 39986385, n=80, GA 29–34 weeks) randomised fortification at postnatal day 4–7 versus day 10–14 (both arms already at 60–80 mL/kg/d feeds, so this trial manipulates postnatal day with feed volume held constant) — again null on the primary FFM z-score outcome, with nominally significant secondary gains in weight, FFM grams and length. A secondary analysis of the Salas 2023 cohort (Tucker et al. 2025; PMID 40714049) reports a difference in BPD severity distribution favouring the early group (P=.039) — hypothesis-generating only, as BPD was not the parent trial's pre-specified outcome.

## (b) Mechanistic rationale

The rationale for delaying fortification rests on plausible but unproven concerns: fortifiers increase feed osmolality and protein/mineral load at a time when gut motility and mucosal barrier function are still immature, and clinicians have historically wanted to see tolerance of unfortified milk before adding an osmotic and metabolic load. The rationale for early fortification is a cumulative-deficit argument — every day of unfortified feeding at low volume represents lost protein and energy accretion relative to intrauterine reference growth, and this deficit compounds over the first two postnatal weeks, which is precisely the period several of the newer trials (Salas 2023, Salas 2025) target with body-composition rather than simple-weight endpoints, on the premise that fat-free mass may be more biologically meaningful than weight alone for later neurodevelopment.

## (c) What has been tried

Trial designs split cleanly into two manipulated variables that the brief specifically asks not to be conflated: **feed-volume threshold** (Thanigainathan's constituent trials, Shah 2016 — comparing 20 or 40 mL/kg/d against 100 mL/kg/d, feed volume as the trigger) versus **postnatal day** (Salas 2023, Salas 2025 — comparing day 2–7 against day 10–14, with feed volume already similar between arms). Basu's pooled meta-analysis mixes both trial types without separating them, which likely contributes to its inconsistency with the narrower two-trial Cochrane pool. Newer trials also increasingly use human-milk-based fortifier specifically for the "early" arm (Salas 2023) as a deliberate design choice to avoid confounding early timing with early bovine-protein exposure — a methodological refinement worth noting explicitly, since it means "early fortification" trials are not always testing the same product across arms.

## (d) Where the evidence fails to settle the question

The headline finding across every systematic review in this domain — Cochrane, Alyahya, and Basu alike — is that the RCT evidence base is too small (2–3 trials, under 350 infants total) to support or refute early fortification with any confidence. No trial has been adequately powered for NEC as a primary outcome, and none has followed to a neurodevelopmental endpoint (see Domain 5). The Basu longer-LOS signal and the Thanigainathan/Alyahya nulls sit in tension with the more recent body-composition trials' secondary positive findings (Salas 2023, Salas 2025), but the latter are single, moderately-sized trials with null primary outcomes — the positive secondary findings should not be read as contradicting the earlier nulls on growth and tolerance; they simply ask a different, narrower question (body composition) that the older trials did not measure. No trial in this domain enrols or stratifies by AEDF/REDF or SGA status specifically — that evidence gap is addressed in Domain 2.

## (e) UK level-3 NICU relevance

Current UK practice (broadly following BAPM/ESPGHAN convention of fortifying at or near 100 mL/kg/d) is not contradicted by this evidence base, but nor is it strongly supported over an earlier-start alternative — the honest reading is that the trial evidence is too underpowered to mandate a change in either direction, and the one comparatively larger meta-analysis (Basu) leans toward caution about earlier fortification given the longer-LOS signal. A UK unit considering earlier fortification as part of a nutrition-optimisation quality initiative should do so as a practice change without expecting a "will improve growth" evidence base to justify it, and should track LOS and feed-intolerance episodes prospectively given Basu's signal.
