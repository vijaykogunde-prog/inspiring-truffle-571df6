# Domain 8 — Concentrated/high-dose, targeted/adjustable, and fortifier-source (bovine vs human-milk-derived) strategies

This domain deliberately keeps the brief's Section 4(b) question — bovine-derived versus human-milk-derived fortifier (the reserved "BMF" sense) — separate from the concentrated/targeted-dosing question, while covering both as instructed.

## (a) What the evidence shows, and how consistent it is

**(i) Fixed high-dose vs standard-dose powdered HMF.** No dedicated high-dose-vs-standard-dose powder trial distinct from the targeted-fortification literature below was identified; this sub-question is effectively absorbed into the targeted/individualised fortification literature, since modern "high-dose" strategies are almost universally individualised (BUN- or milk-analysis-guided) rather than a simple fixed higher dose.

**(ii) Concentrated liquid preterm formula as a rescue/alternative fortification strategy.** Moya et al. (2012; PMID 22987877, n=150, multicentre, blinded RCT) compared a new ultraconcentrated liquid human milk fortifier (~20% more protein than standard) against control powdered HMF in infants ≤1250g, finding significantly higher achieved weight and linear growth with the liquid fortifier (P=.04 and P=.03, intention-to-treat), no difference in feeding tolerance or days to full feeds, and no difference in sepsis or NEC — a genuinely reassuring, moderate-certainty positive result from an adequately designed trial. Lin et al. (2020; PMID 32722642, Taiwan, before-after QI cohort, n=73) used concentrated preterm formula as a liquid fortifier specifically as an initiation-stage rescue strategy for feed-intolerant ELBW infants, reporting higher enteral intake, better weight z-score at term, and better 24-month language and motor scores compared with a historical cohort — a positive signal, but from the weakest study design in this domain (non-randomised, historical control, small numbers), and should not be given equal evidentiary weight to Moya's RCT despite reporting a superficially similar "concentrated liquid fortifier works" conclusion.

**(iii) Targeted/adjustable/individualised fortification.** This is the sub-question with the most striking null/harm signal in the entire brief, and the brief's own instruction is to give it full prominence rather than a footnote. Seliga-Siwecka et al. (2023; PMID 36771325, Poland) randomised preterm infants ≤32 weeks to tailored modular fortification (protein/carbohydrate/lipid components) versus standard fixed-dose HMF — the trial was **stopped early after only 39 of a planned 220 infants were randomised, because of serious feeding intolerance in 5 cases**. The truncated efficacy comparison (weight-gain velocity 27.01 vs 25.84 g/d, P=0.0776) is essentially uninterpretable given the severe underpowering that resulted from stopping for safety, but the safety signal itself — that modular, ingredient-by-ingredient targeted fortification was not feasible in routine NICU practice in this trial, in the authors' own words — is the headline finding and must not be diluted by more numerous smaller positive reports of targeted fortification elsewhere in this domain.

Those more positive reports exist and should be reported alongside, not instead of, the Seliga-Siwecka signal. Parat et al. (2020; PMID 32326177, n=36) found higher protein intake and larger flank skinfold thickness with mid-infrared-spectroscopy-guided targeted fortification, with a post-hoc subgroup (≥30 intervention days) showing 3% higher fat-free mass — small, exploratory, post-hoc. Rochow et al. (2025; PMID 40507033, detailed fully in Domain 5) found significantly better growth and growth velocity with double-blind targeted fortification versus standard, with Bayley-III scores numerically but mostly not significantly higher. The most current pooled synthesis, Bell et al. (2026; PMID 42391159, 12 RCTs, 575 infants, Cochrane RoB-1 and GRADE), concludes that individualised fortification *likely increases* growth parameters (head circumference MD 1.76mm/wk, weight MD 2.87g/kg/d, length MD 3.20mm/wk, fat-free mass MD 121.4g — all moderate certainty) but that effects on **mortality (RR 0.20), NEC (RR 0.29) and 18-month neurodevelopment remain very uncertain**, with the review's own authors stating plainly that whether the growth benefit translates into meaningful long-term functional benefit is unproven. Read together, the Seliga-Siwecka trial and the Bell meta-analysis tell a coherent, non-contradictory story: individualised fortification can improve growth when it is tolerated and deliverable, but its practical feasibility and its downstream clinical benefit are both genuinely uncertain, and one well-conducted trial attempting a modular approach specifically failed on feasibility/safety grounds.

**(iv) Acidified vs non-acidified liquid fortifier.** Schanler et al. (2018; PMID 30195561, n=164, multicentre RCT) found no significant difference in overall 29-day weight gain between acidified and non-acidified liquid HMF, but the non-acidified product showed significantly greater early (day 1–15) weight gain (17.9 vs 15.2 g/kg/d, P=.001) and a stark safety difference: metabolic acidosis in 27% (acidified) versus 5% (non-acidified) of infants (P<.001), more vomiting, gastric residuals and abdominal distension with the acidified product. This is a clear, single-trial, moderate-certainty safety signal that is easily missed if a search or synthesis is framed only around "dose" rather than product chemistry, exactly as the brief warns — it is a product-chemistry finding, not a dosing finding, and should be reported with equal prominence to the growth-related findings elsewhere in this domain.

**(v) Bovine vs human-milk-derived fortifier (the brief's reserved "BMF" sense).** This is the most internally inconsistent sub-question in the domain, and that inconsistency is itself the finding worth reporting prominently. Three meta-analyses converge on a possible NEC/mortality advantage for human-milk-derived fortifier: Ananthan et al. (2020; PMID 32277813, 6 RCTs, 334 infants) found lower NEC (RR 0.38, 95% CI 0.15–0.95) and surgical NEC (RR 0.13, 95% CI 0.02–0.67) with human-milk-derived fortifier, but explicitly noted this NEC benefit **lost significance in sensitivity analysis excluding high-risk-of-bias studies**, alongside a lower weight-gain penalty with the human-milk-derived product (MD −1.08 g/kg/d) — a genuine trade-off, not a one-directional "human is better" story. Grace et al. (2020; PMID 32943531, 2 RCTs, largely overlapping with Ananthan's trial pool, 334 infants) found a consistent NEC reduction (RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.22–0.98). Galiș et al. (2024; PMID 38542821, 4 RCTs, 681 infants) found a **significant mortality reduction** with human-milk-derived fortifier (RR 0.50, 95% CI 0.26–0.94, P=0.03) with no significant NEC difference in this particular pool (RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.20–1.17) and a borderline-non-significant BPD signal favouring the human-milk-derived group. Set against all three positive meta-analyses is the single largest and most rigorously conducted individual RCT in this exact comparison, N-forte (Jensen et al. 2024; PMID 38545091, 228 infants), which found **no difference** in the composite NEC/sepsis/mortality outcome between HMBF and BMBF (OR 1.05, 95% CI 0.61–1.81, P=0.86) — a clean null from the trial with the largest single sample size and most rigorous blinding/adjudication procedures in this literature. The parent RCT underlying the Hopperton/O'Connor ND follow-up (O'Connor et al. 2018; PMID 29878061, n=127) likewise found no difference in feeding interruptions or the composite mortality/morbidity index between HMBF and BMBF. This is precisely the kind of small-positive-meta-analyses-versus-large-null-single-trial discrepancy the brief's null-finding rule requires be surfaced prominently rather than smoothed into an unqualified "human milk-derived fortifier reduces NEC and mortality" headline.

Two further fortifier-source variants deserve separate mention as emerging categories rather than simple bovine-vs-human comparisons. Bovine colostrum, tested against conventional bovine fortifier in the FortiColos trial (Ahnfeldt et al. 2023; PMID 37004355, n=232), showed similar growth with more protein delivered and a non-significant trend toward more late-onset sepsis with colostrum (23/113 vs 14/116, P=0.08) — a trend, not a null, that warrants explicit mention rather than dismissal. Freeze-dried donor-milk fortifier (Rochow et al. 2025 observational; PMID 41097134, n=64) showed excellent feeding tolerance and no safety signal in a small matched cohort restricted to infants ≥30 weeks, with the authors themselves cautioning that its protein-to-energy ratio may be suboptimal for infants under 1500g — explicitly not yet evidence applicable to the smallest, highest-risk infants. Chinnappan et al. (2021; PMID 33970187, n=123, India) found preterm formula *powder* (a cost-driven substitute in a resource-limited setting, distinct from Lin's concentrated *liquid* formula rescue strategy) noninferior to standard HMF for weight gain with lower feed-intolerance rates — most relevant to resource-limited settings rather than a well-resourced UK unit where cost is less often the binding constraint.

## (b) Mechanistic rationale

Human-milk-derived fortifiers are hypothesised to reduce NEC/inflammatory risk by avoiding bovine milk protein entirely at a time when the preterm gut may be more susceptible to cow's-milk-protein-mediated inflammatory injury, at the cost of typically lower protein/energy density than bovine-based products (explaining the weight-gain trade-off Ananthan identified). Acidification of liquid fortifiers is a preservation/stability strategy that appears to carry an unintended metabolic cost (acid load contributing to metabolic acidosis) not shared by non-acidified liquid formulations. Targeted/individualised fortification is premised on the well-established fact that human milk macronutrient content varies substantially between mothers and over lactation, such that a fixed-dose fortifier systematically under- or over-fortifies a substantial proportion of feeds — the Seliga-Siwecka trial's failure suggests that correcting for this variability via manual modular titration may impose a practical/tolerance burden that offsets the theoretical nutritional precision gained.

## (c) What has been tried

The full range specified in the brief has trial-level evidence: fixed high-dose (absorbed into targeted literature), concentrated liquid preterm formula (Moya, Lin), targeted/adjustable (Seliga-Siwecka, Parat, Rochow, Bell's pooled synthesis, and the ongoing MaxiMoM InForM trial below), acidified vs non-acidified (Schanler), and bovine vs human-milk-derived source (Ananthan, Grace, Galiș, N-forte, O'Connor 2018, plus the colostrum and freeze-dried-donor-milk variants).

## (d) Where the evidence fails to settle the question

Three genuine, well-evidenced tensions run through this domain and should each be preserved rather than resolved by editorial fiat in the eventual manuscript: (1) targeted/individualised fortification improves growth (Bell, moderate certainty) but one well-conducted RCT attempting it (Seliga-Siwecka) failed on feasibility/safety grounds, and mortality/NEC/neurodevelopment effects remain very uncertain; (2) human-milk-derived fortifier shows a NEC/mortality advantage across three meta-analyses that is not replicated by the single largest, most rigorous individual RCT (N-forte) testing the same comparison, and even within the positive meta-analyses the NEC signal is explicitly fragile to risk-of-bias exclusion (Ananthan); (3) product chemistry (acidified vs not) carries a clear, easily-overlooked safety signal (Schanler) independent of, and potentially more actionable than, either the dosing or source-type questions. A registered, adequately powered trial is currently underway that could resolve much of tension (1): the MaxiMoM InForM trial (Beggs et al. 2025; PMID 41047263, planned n=615, Canada), a three-arm double-blind RCT of standard versus target versus BUN-adjustable fortification with Bayley-IV cognitive score at 18–24 months as its primary outcome — this is named explicitly in Section 6 as the single trial most likely to change practice in this domain, and none of the currently completed trials (including Rochow and Bell's pooled synthesis) has this combination of adequate power and a hard long-term primary endpoint.

## (e) UK level-3 NICU relevance

A UK unit choosing between fortifier products should weigh the N-forte null (its largest, most rigorous single-trial evidence point) against the smaller positive meta-analyses when considering a routine switch to human-milk-derived fortifier purely on NEC/mortality grounds, while noting the genuine growth trade-off Ananthan identified. The Schanler acidification finding is directly actionable and lower-controversy: where a non-acidified liquid alternative exists, it appears to carry materially less metabolic-acidosis risk on current single-trial evidence. Targeted/individualised fortification, while showing a real growth benefit in pooled analysis, should not be adopted via ad hoc modular titration without structured tolerance monitoring, given the Seliga-Siwecka trial's direct demonstration that this approach can fail on safety grounds in routine practice; units interested in individualisation are better served waiting for the MaxiMoM InForM trial's results than improvising a modular protocol locally.
