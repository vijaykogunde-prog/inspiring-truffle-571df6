# Domain 4 — Short-term growth outcomes

## (a) What the trials show, and how consistent it is

This is the densest and most internally consistent part of the literature: fortification of human milk reliably increases in-hospital growth-velocity parameters by a modest margin, and this finding replicates across three independent pooled syntheses spanning three decades' worth of trials.

The current Cochrane review (Brown et al. 2020, CD000343.pub4; PMID 35658821 — an update of the review the brief cites as "Brown 2016," PMID 27155888, now superseded) pools 18 RCTs (1456 infants) comparing multi-nutrient-fortified against unfortified human milk, finding low-to-moderate certainty evidence of increased in-hospital weight gain (MD 1.76 g/kg/d, 95% CI 1.30–2.22), linear growth (MD 0.11 cm/wk, 95% CI 0.08–0.15) and head-circumference growth (MD 0.06 cm/wk, 95% CI 0.03–0.08), with low-certainty evidence of **no clear effect on NEC risk** (RR 1.37, 95% CI 0.72–2.63; 13 studies, 1110 infants) — a confidence interval comfortably straddling unity in both directions, which should be reported as genuine uncertainty rather than either reassurance or an unresolved safety signal. Data on growth or development beyond infancy were sparse and showed no clear effect either way.

A 2026 update (Rallis et al.; PMID 42451102), pooling 15 RCTs (1079 infants) using the newer INSPECT-SR trial-trustworthiness tool alongside RoB2, found larger point estimates for the same growth parameters (weight MD 2.74 g/kg/d, 95% CI 1.27–4.22; length MD 0.08 cm/wk, 95% CI 0.02–0.14; head circumference MD 0.06 cm/wk, 95% CI 0.03–0.10) with no significant differences in feeding intolerance, NEC, late-onset sepsis, chronic lung disease, time to full feeds, length of stay or mortality. Critically, the review's own authors graded this evidence *low certainty* because of substantial unexplained heterogeneity (I² 87–95% across growth outcomes) and because their INSPECT-SR screening flagged one included trial with serious trustworthiness concerns and twelve with some concerns, out of fifteen total. This larger effect estimate should not be read as superseding Cochrane's more conservative figure without carrying forward this trustworthiness caveat explicitly.

A 2025 network meta-analysis (Campbell-Yeo et al.; PMID 40431391, 59 studies) ranked mother's-own-milk-plus-donor-milk-plus-human-milk-based-fortifier as the top-ranked regimen for mortality, NEC and time to full enteral feeds, while a mother's-own-milk-plus-phosphorus-fortifier combination ranked top for ROP and BPD — but the authors' own conclusion is that there remains insufficient high-quality evidence to support or refute any single fortification regimen definitively; the ranking indicates direction of preference across a sparse, indirect comparison network, not a confident practice recommendation.

## (b) Mechanistic rationale

Exclusively breast-milk-fed preterm infants accumulate cumulative protein, energy and mineral deficits because unfortified mature human milk does not meet the nutrient density required to match intrauterine accretion rates for a rapidly growing preterm infant — the physiological premise underlying fortification generally. Multi-nutrient fortification adds protein, energy, calcium, phosphate and other micronutrients intended to close this gap, and the growth-velocity outcomes measured in these trials (weight, length, head circumference gain per week) are direct surrogates for whether that gap is in fact being closed, at least over the timescale of a single NICU admission.

## (c) What has been tried

The 18–59 trials pooled across these reviews vary considerably in fortifier product (bovine powder was the near-universal product across the majority of trials underlying the Cochrane and Rallis reviews, reflecting when most of this evidence was generated), fortification threshold, and population (birthweight and gestational-age ranges). None of the three reviews summarised here specifically isolates AEDF/REDF, post-NEC, or extremely-preterm (<28 week) subgroups as a planned analysis — that stratification, where it exists at all, sits in the network meta-analysis (Campbell-Yeo) and in the product-specific meta-analyses covered under Domain 8.

## (d) Where the evidence fails to settle the question

The consistent theme across all three syntheses, stated by their own authors, is that short-term growth-velocity gains are real but modest, and that long-term growth and neurodevelopmental data are too sparse to know whether these gains matter for the outcomes that actually concern families and clinicians (see Domain 5). The Rallis review's own trustworthiness screening — finding concerns in 13 of 15 included trials — is a genuinely useful methodological advance that should be carried into any manuscript discussing why growth-velocity effect sizes have appeared to grow larger in more recent pooled estimates: this may reflect evolving trial quality issues as much as a true change in the underlying biological effect.

## (e) UK level-3 NICU relevance

The modest, consistent growth-velocity benefit from fortification (roughly 1.75–2.75 g/kg/d extra weight gain, a fraction of a millimetre extra head/length growth per week) is unlikely to be controversial as a rationale for routine fortification of preterm human milk in a UK level-3 setting, and is broadly consistent with current NICE/BAPM practice. What UK clinicians should take from this domain, precisely and without over-claiming, is that the growth-velocity evidence base does not by itself establish a NEC safety signal in either direction, and does not establish that closing the short-term growth gap improves long-term outcomes — both of those questions belong to Domains 5 and 6 respectively, and neither is answered by the growth-velocity literature alone.
