# Domain 5 — Long-term neurodevelopmental outcome

The brief requires every claim in this domain to be explicitly tiered by directness. This synthesis follows that structure throughout: Tier (i) trials randomised fortification (or fortifier timing/type) and followed to a formal neurodevelopmental endpoint; Tier (ii) trials/cohorts report neurodevelopment as a secondary or associated outcome without fortification being the randomised variable; Tier (iii) studies use a surrogate/mechanistic outcome (chiefly head-circumference growth velocity) as a proxy for neurodevelopment.

## (a) What the trials show, and how consistent it is

**Tier (i).** Four trials meet the strict definition of randomising a fortification variable with a formal neurodevelopmental endpoint, and their findings are consistently null-to-modest, never dramatic.

Klamer et al. (2022; PMID 35807888), following 214 very preterm infants to 6 years corrected age, found that post-discharge fortification of mother's own milk did **not** improve full-scale IQ (median 104 fortified vs 105.5 unfortified, P=0.29) or any Five-to-Fifteen questionnaire domain, compared with unfortified mother's milk — a clean, clinically important null finding at a genuinely long follow-up interval using a validated instrument (WISC-IV). Both breastfed groups (fortified and unfortified alike) outperformed a preterm-formula comparison group on verbal comprehension and motor scores, an important secondary finding suggesting the relevant contrast for later cognition may be breast milk versus formula rather than fortified versus unfortified breast milk.

Hopperton/O'Connor et al. (2019; PMID 32154499), the 18-month follow-up of a blinded RCT randomising fortifier *source* (human milk-based, HMBF, versus bovine milk-based, BMBF) in infants <1250g, found no significant difference between HMBF and BMBF on Bayley-III cognitive (94.7 vs 95.9), language (92.4 vs 93.1) or motor (95.6 vs 97.7) composite scores, nor in the combined death-or-neurodevelopmental-impairment outcome.

Rochow et al. (2025; PMID 40507033), a double-blind RCT of individualised/targeted fortification (macronutrient-content-guided) versus standard fixed-dose fortification in infants <30 weeks, found significantly better growth and growth velocity with targeted fortification, and numerically higher Bayley-III scores across cognitive, language and motor domains at 18 months — but the authors themselves state plainly that most of these Bayley-III differences did **not** reach statistical significance, with one exception (expressive language in a protein-stratified subgroup) and one binary threshold finding (zero versus three infants scoring ≤70 on the motor domain). This trial demonstrates a clear growth benefit with, at most, a suggestive but not confirmed neurodevelopmental signal.

The N-forte trial (Jensen et al., protocol 2021 PMID 34815288, short-term results 2024 PMID 38545091) is the largest and most rigorously designed trial in this space (228 extremely preterm infants, HMBF vs BMBF), with neurodevelopmental follow-up explicitly planned at 2 and 5.5 years — but as of this search (2026-09-12), only the short-term composite NEC/sepsis/mortality outcome has been published (null: OR 1.05, 95% CI 0.61–1.81), and the neurodevelopmental follow-up has **not yet been reported**. This is flagged in Section 6 as the single trial most likely to move this domain's evidence forward once it reports.

**Tier (ii).** O'Connor et al. (2016 JAMA; PMID 27825008) is presented here specifically as a worked example of correct Tier-ii labelling per the brief's instruction: this trial randomises donor human milk versus preterm formula as a supplement to mother's milk (not fortification) in 363 VLBW infants, finding no significant difference in Bayley-III cognitive (92.9 vs 94.5), language or motor composite scores at 18 months. Any background HMF use in either arm was not the randomised variable and is not reported as a stratifying factor — this trial answers "does donor milk vs formula affect neurodevelopment," not a fortification question, and citing it as fortification evidence would be a category error this synthesis explicitly avoids.

Ericson et al. (2023; PMID 37242201), a Swedish longitudinal cohort of 142 *moderately* preterm infants (32–36 weeks — a materially different, less severe population than the VLBW/extremely-preterm focus of the Tier-i trials above) followed to 6 years, found no significant differences in neurological, growth or health outcomes between infants who received exclusive breast milk versus fortified breast milk and/or formula during their NICU stay. This is reassuring in direction but the fortification exposure was clinically indicated rather than randomised, so confounding by indication (sicker or more slowly growing infants more likely to be fortified) cannot be excluded, and the paper's own authors call for larger studies.

**Tier (iii).** No dedicated head-circumference-velocity-as-ND-proxy study was extracted as a standalone Domain 5 entry; this tier's evidence lives in the growth-velocity data already reported in Domain 4 (head-circumference gain of roughly 0.06 cm/week with fortification, per Brown 2020 and Rallis 2026). The correlation between early head-circumference growth and later cognitive outcome is well established in the broader neonatal literature, but it is a population-level correlation, not a validated individual-level surrogate endpoint — a roughly 0.06 cm/week difference in head growth should not be verbally translated into an implied IQ-point benefit anywhere in the eventual manuscript.

## (b) Mechanistic rationale

The biological argument connecting fortification to neurodevelopment runs through nutrient-dependent brain growth during a period of intense synaptogenesis and myelination in the third trimester equivalent — protein, energy, iron, zinc and long-chain fatty acid intake are each independently linked to brain structural development in the wider preterm-nutrition literature. The difficulty, borne out empirically in this domain's Tier-i trials, is that a nutrient intervention producing a real but modest short-term growth-velocity effect does not reliably translate into a measurable long-term cognitive effect at the sample sizes these trials have achieved.

## (c) What has been tried

Tier-i trial designs split into fortify-vs-not (Klamer, at the post-discharge stage only), fortifier-source (HMBF vs BMBF: Hopperton/O'Connor, and the not-yet-ND-reported N-forte trial), and fortification-strategy (targeted vs standard: Rochow). No identified Tier-i trial has tested in-hospital fortification timing (Domain 1's question) against a neurodevelopmental endpoint — the timing trials (Salas, Shah, Thanigainathan's constituent trials) have not been followed to any ND assessment identified in this search.

## (d) Where the evidence fails to settle the question

This is the domain most exposed to surrogate-outcome conflation, and the honest summary is that no Tier-i trial has yet demonstrated a clear neurodevelopmental benefit from any fortification variable (whether/not, source, or individualisation strategy) at the sample sizes achieved so far — Klamer's IQ finding is a clean null; Hopperton/O'Connor's fortifier-source comparison is a clean null; Rochow's targeted-fortification trial shows a real growth effect with, at most, a hinted-at but statistically unconfirmed ND signal. The field's best hope for a definitive answer — N-forte's planned 2- and 5.5-year follow-up — has not yet reported. Any manuscript drawing on this domain should resist the temptation to infer neurodevelopmental benefit from the Domain 4 growth-velocity literature; that inference is exactly the conflation the brief warns against, and the Tier-i trial evidence that has actually tested the ND question directly does not support it.

## (e) UK level-3 NICU relevance

Families and clinicians in a UK NICU should be given an honest, unhedged message: fortification improves short-term growth velocity by a modest amount, but there is no good direct trial evidence — as opposed to plausible extrapolation from growth data — that this translates into better long-term neurodevelopmental outcome, for the fortify-vs-not question, the fortifier-source question, or (with the single caveat that a trend was seen) the individualised-fortification question. This should not be used as an argument against fortification, which remains supported for its growth and (per Domain 8) some mortality/NEC signals — but it should shape how the neurodevelopmental benefits of fortification are described to parents in discharge counselling, particularly for post-discharge (home) fortification decisions, which is exactly the scenario Klamer's null IQ finding addresses most directly.
