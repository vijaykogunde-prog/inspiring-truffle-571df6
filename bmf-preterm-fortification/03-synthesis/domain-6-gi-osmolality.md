# Domain 6 — GI side effects: constipation, feeding intolerance, osmolality

## (a) What the evidence shows, and how consistent it is

Constipation is, as the brief anticipated, poorly captured as a *named* outcome in most fortification trials — the large RCTs and Cochrane reviews covered in Domains 1 and 4 report "feeding intolerance," "gastric residuals" or "abdominal distension" as composite or umbrella categories, rarely disaggregating a specific stool-consistency or stool-frequency measure. The one RCT identified that does prospectively and specifically measure bowel habit is the FortiColos bowel-habits sub-study (Kappel et al. 2022; PMID 36432444, n=242, very preterm infants randomised to bovine colostrum versus conventional bovine fortifier): stools hardened significantly with increasing fortifier *dose* in **both** arms (P<0.01), though significantly less with bovine colostrum than with conventional fortifier (P<0.05), and laxative treatment was prescribed later in the colostrum group. This is a genuinely useful finding structurally: it demonstrates a dose-response relationship between fortification and stool hardening that exists regardless of product, with the choice of product modulating but not eliminating the effect — direct evidence, at the level of a named, prospectively measured outcome, that fortification-associated constipation is a real, measurable phenomenon rather than an assumption carried over from osmolality theory alone.

The retrospective Chinese cohort (Zhang et al. 2022; PMID 36364872, n=298, Southwest China) found no overall association between HMF use per se and feeding intolerance, but identified specific practice-level risk factors within HMF-fed infants: early initiation (P=0.02), full-strength initiation (P=0.04) and fast supplementation rate (P=0.004) each independently increased feeding-intolerance risk, with threshold effects at fortifier concentration >24 mg/mL and supplementation rate >12.5 mg/mL/day identified via dose-response modelling. This is retrospective, single-centre, and drawn from a fortification-practice context that differs from Western/UK convention (the authors' own recommended protocol — initiate at 100 mL/kg/day feeds, 1:50 concentration advancing to 1:25 over 38+ hours — is itself more conservative than some Western guidelines), so direct generalisability to a UK unit is uncertain, but the underlying dose-response signal (faster/earlier/stronger fortification carries more intolerance risk) is mechanistically coherent with the osmolality literature below.

The osmolality-mechanism literature is deliberately kept separate here, per the brief's explicit instruction, from the clinical-outcome literature above. Herranz Barbero et al. (2020; PMID 32479524) and Choi et al. (2016; PMID 26863130) are laboratory/modelling studies measuring and predicting how fortifier addition raises human milk osmolality — they measure osmolality, not clinical outcomes, and neither should be cited as establishing a validated clinical harm threshold. Chandran et al. (2016; PMID 28030867) extends this mechanistic work usefully by showing that commonly co-administered NICU medications add further osmotic load on top of fortification itself — a practically important, frequently overlooked point for bedside practice, but again a laboratory measurement, not a clinical-outcome finding.

## (b) Mechanistic rationale

Fortifier addition increases feed osmolality by adding solute (protein, carbohydrate, minerals) to a fixed volume of milk; higher osmolality is hypothesised to slow gastric emptying, alter intestinal motility, and in the most severe hypothesised pathway, contribute to mucosal injury that could predispose to NEC. This is a plausible, dose-dependent mechanistic chain, and the Zhang and Kappel clinical findings above are broadly consistent with a dose-response relationship between fortifier load and GI tolerance. What has never been established, and what the brief specifically instructs this synthesis to state plainly, is a validated causal link from a specific measured osmolality value to a specific clinical outcome (constipation severity, feeding intolerance incidence, or NEC risk) in a randomised comparison.

## (c) What has been tried

The American Academy of Pediatrics' commonly cited 450 mOsm/kg ceiling for enteral feeds is a **consensus safety threshold**, arrived at by expert judgement from the mechanistic/measurement literature (of the type represented by Herranz Barbero, Choi and Chandran here) — it has not been validated against hard clinical outcomes in a randomised comparison, and no trial identified in this search tests infants fed above versus below this threshold as a randomised clinical question. This distinction — consensus ceiling versus RCT-validated threshold — is one of the clearest "say plainly where evidence is weak" opportunities in the entire brief and should not be softened in the eventual manuscript.

## (d) Where the evidence fails to settle the question

No RCT has been identified that randomises infants to different feed-osmolality bands and measures clinical GI outcomes prospectively — every osmolality data point in this literature comes from bench measurement or modelling, while every clinical-outcome data point (Zhang, Kappel) comes from either an observational cohort or a product-comparison RCT that does not report osmolality values alongside its clinical outcomes. The two literatures — osmolality measurement and clinical GI outcome — have not yet been formally joined in a single study design, which is precisely the gap the brief instructs this synthesis to name rather than paper over by implication.

## (e) UK level-3 NICU relevance

UK units following the consensus ~450 mOsm/kg ceiling for enteral feeds (accounting for both fortifier and any co-administered medications, per Chandran's finding) are following a reasonable precautionary practice, not a directly RCT-validated one. The Zhang and Kappel findings both support a practical, low-cost bedside principle independent of that specific numeric threshold: fortification introduced gradually (rather than at full strength immediately) and at a measured rate is associated with better tolerance than rapid, early, full-strength introduction — this is a more directly actionable, if still imperfectly evidenced, message than the osmolality ceiling itself.
