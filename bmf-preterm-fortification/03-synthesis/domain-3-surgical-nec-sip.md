# Domain 3 — Post-surgical NEC and spontaneous intestinal perforation (SIP)

## (a) What the evidence shows, and how consistent it is — organised by the brief's three sub-questions

**(i) When to restart any enteral feeding after medical/surgical NEC or SIP.** This is the only sub-question with any direct trial-adjacent evidence. Olaloye et al.'s systematic review (2020; PMID 32397283) of 19 studies (predominantly historical-control designs — 16 of 19 — plus 2 case series and 1 cohort) found that 3 studies reported reduced SIP incidence when enteral nutrition was initiated within the first 3 days of life, and 2 studies reported reduced mortality and neurodevelopmental impairment with early feeding, concluding narratively that early enteral nutrition in ELBW infants appears to reduce SIP incidence without increasing mortality. Bohnhorst et al.'s single-centre cohort (2003; PMID 14571225, n=26 early-feeding vs n=18 historical comparison) found that restarting feeds once portal venous gas had been absent for three consecutive days on ultrasound — a median of 4 days after NEC onset, versus 10 days in the historical comparison group — was associated with a shorter time to full feeds, shorter central-line duration, less catheter sepsis and shorter hospital stay, with no clear increase in NEC recurrence or death (2 vs 1 recurrence, 1 vs 2 NEC-related deaths). The authors themselves explicitly state their study was underpowered to exclude a higher recurrence risk — a limitation this synthesis preserves rather than smooths over.

**(ii) When it is then safe to add fortifier once feeds have restarted.** This sub-question is essentially unaddressed. Neither the ERNICA 2024 European surgical NEC guideline (Hulscher/Irvine et al.; PMID 39925108) nor the 2026 Italian SIN/SICP/SINUPE joint position paper on post-operative GI-surgery nutrition (De Rose et al.; PMID 41519795) contains a fortifier-specific timing or dosing recommendation distinct from general enteral-feeding advice, despite both documents explicitly addressing enteral nutrition after NEC/GI surgery. This absence was confirmed by full-text review of both documents specifically for this brief, not assumed. The ERNICA guideline grades most of its own recommendations (very) low certainty, reflecting how sparse the underlying surgical-NEC nutrition trial evidence is generally, let alone for fortification specifically. The N-forte trial (Jensen et al. 2024; PMID 38545091), the largest recent RCT of fortifier type (human-milk-based vs bovine-based) in exclusively human-milk-fed extremely preterm infants, reports only a composite NEC-stage-II-III/sepsis/mortality outcome (41/115 HMBF vs 39/113 BMBF, OR 1.05, 95% CI 0.61–1.81, P=0.86) with no SIP-specific or post-surgical-refeeding stratification in the published short-term report — checked specifically and confirmed absent, per the brief's instruction to report rather than infer.

**(iii) Whether fortifier choice (bovine vs human-milk-derived) affects recurrence or subsequent NEC/SIP risk.** No dedicated trial or subgroup analysis addressing recurrence specifically after a prior NEC/SIP episode was located. The nearest available evidence is the general (not post-NEC-specific) HMBF-vs-BMBF meta-analyses covered in Domain 8, which address *first-episode* NEC risk in an unselected preterm population, not recurrence risk in infants who have already had NEC or SIP.

## (b) Mechanistic rationale

NEC and SIP are distinct pathologies with different proposed mechanisms — NEC a diffuse ischaemic-inflammatory process, SIP a more focal lesion often associated with early indomethacin or corticosteroid exposure — but both leave the bowel wall structurally and functionally compromised, creating a rationale for cautious, incremental feed reintroduction (the portal-venous-gas-clearance criterion used by Bohnhorst is one operationalisation of "structural recovery"). The rationale for delaying fortifier specifically, once feeds have restarted, mirrors Domain 2's logic — added osmotic and protein load on a recently injured or still-recovering gut — but as established above, this rationale has not been tested in a dedicated trial.

## (c) What has been tried

Trial and cohort designs cluster almost entirely around feed-*restart* timing (portal-gas clearance criteria, fixed-day protocols, historical-control comparisons of earlier versus later feed reintroduction). No identified study varies fortifier introduction timing as an independent variable after NEC/SIP, and no identified study randomises fortifier type specifically as a recurrence-prevention strategy.

## (d) Where the evidence fails to settle the question

This domain has the widest gap between what guidelines are expected to say and what the evidence supports. Two independent, current, multidisciplinary guideline/position-paper efforts (ERNICA 2024 and the Italian SIN/SICP/SINUPE 2026 paper) both address post-NEC/post-surgical nutrition in detail and both are silent on fortifier-specific timing — this corroborated silence is itself the Domain 3 finding, not a search failure. The feed-restart evidence base (sub-question i) is dominated by historical-control designs (Olaloye: 16 of 19 included studies), which are inherently confounded by secular changes in NICU care over the study period, and even the most cited single-centre cohort (Bohnhorst) is explicitly self-described as underpowered for its most safety-critical outcome (NEC recurrence). Sub-questions (ii) and (iii) are not merely under-evidenced; they are, on the evidence retrieved, essentially unaddressed in the peer-reviewed literature as of this search.

## (e) UK level-3 NICU relevance

UK practice for restarting feeds after medical or surgical NEC typically follows a cautious, incremental, ultrasound- or clinically-guided approach broadly consistent with the Bohnhorst protocol's logic, though not derived from UK-specific trial data. The point at which fortifier is reintroduced once feeds have restarted is, on the evidence reviewed here, a matter of local unit convention and clinical judgement rather than evidence-based protocol — units should recognise this explicitly rather than assume their local practice (however sensible) rests on a trial base it does not have. This is one of the clearest candidates in the whole brief for a unit-level audit or a multicentre UK observational study (e.g., via the National Neonatal Research Database) to at least characterise current practice variation, pending any prospective trial.
