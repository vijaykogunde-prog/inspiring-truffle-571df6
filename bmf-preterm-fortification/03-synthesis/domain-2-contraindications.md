# Domain 2 — Contraindications: antenatal AEDF/REDF and IUGR/SGA

## (a) What the trials/practice show, and how consistent it is

There is no fortification-specific RCT in the AEDF/REDF or growth-restricted subgroup. What exists is a feeding-*initiation* trial tradition (ADEPT) and an observational Doppler-stratified GI-outcome literature, neither of which directly tests when or whether to add fortifier.

The ADEPT trial (Leaf et al. 2012; PMID 22492770, n=404, UK/Ireland, 54 units) randomised growth-restricted infants with abnormal antenatal umbilical artery Doppler to start *enteral feeds* (not fortifier) on day 2 ("early") versus day 6 ("late"), with feed-volume advancement identical between arms. Full sustained enteral feeding was reached sooner with early feeding (median 18 vs 21 days, HR 1.36, 95% CI 1.11–1.67) with no difference in NEC (18% vs 15%, RR 1.2, 95% CI 0.77–1.87) and secondary benefits (shorter PN duration, less high-dependency care, better discharge weight SDS) favouring early feeding. A pre-specified subgroup analysis (Kempley et al. 2013; PMID 23973795) stratified the same cohort by gestation: infants <29 weeks tolerated the identical protocol far worse than those ≥29 weeks — full feeds at a median of 28 days versus 19 days (HR 0.35), and NEC in 39% versus 10% (RR 3.7, 95% CI 2.4–5.7). This is a critical internal finding: even within a single trial's growth-restricted, Doppler-abnormal population, a uniform feeding protocol behaves very differently by gestational age, which argues against extrapolating any single timing rule — for feeding, let alone for fortification — uniformly across this subgroup.

The observational literature on Doppler-stratified GI outcomes (Martini et al. 2022; PMID 36501150, n=570, single Italian tertiary NICU) shows a gradient of risk: infants with combined umbilical-artery-plus-ductus-venosus AREDF have significantly higher feeding intolerance, NEC and SIP rates and later time to full enteral feeds than Doppler-normal controls; umbilical-artery AREDF alone and brain-sparing redistribution also show increased feeding intolerance and delayed full feeds, with brain-sparing additionally raising NEC rates. This confirms a severity gradient by Doppler pattern but, again, does not report fortification exposure or timing as a study variable.

The historical epidemiological foundation for treating AREDF as a NEC risk factor independent of prematurity and growth restriction comes from two small, old UK studies: Malcolm et al. (1991; PMID 1863128, Arch Dis Child) found NEC in 53% of AREDF-exposed morphologically normal fetuses versus 6% of Doppler-normal matched controls, and Wilson et al. (1991; PMID 1776906, Arch Dis Child), published as a corroborating letter, found a consistent though smaller-magnitude association (22% vs 6%). Both are small (fewer than 25 exposed infants each), retrospective or correspondence-format, and predate modern fortification practice entirely — they establish the *mechanistic and epidemiological* case for caution, not a fortification-specific evidence base.

## (b) Mechanistic rationale

Antenatal Doppler abnormality reflects placental insufficiency and chronic fetal hypoxia, which can trigger a brain-sparing circulatory redistribution away from the gut (and other viscera) toward the brain. This splanchnic hypoperfusion is the proposed mechanism linking AEDF/REDF to later feeding intolerance, NEC and SIP — an intestine that has been chronically under-perfused in utero is hypothesised to tolerate the metabolic and osmotic demands of enteral feeding, and by extension fortification, less well than a normally perfused gut. This is biologically distinct from the IUGR/SGA category defined by birthweight centile alone, which captures a much more heterogeneous population including infants whose growth restriction has no antenatal Doppler correlate at all.

## (c) What has been tried

Only feed-*initiation* timing has been tested in a randomised design in this population (ADEPT and its subgroup/companion analyses). No RCT has randomised fortification timing, dose, or product specifically within an AEDF/REDF or Doppler-stratified population. The Martini cohort is the closest available dataset linking Doppler status to GI outcomes with any granularity, and it is observational and silent on fortification.

## (d) Where the evidence fails to settle the question — the central extrapolation flag

This is, as the brief anticipated, the single largest evidence gap identified across the entire brief. Current clinical practice — waiting for full feeds and clinical stability before fortifying an AEDF/REDF or growth-restricted infant — is an **extrapolation** from the ADEPT feed-initiation trial tradition and the AREDF–NEC epidemiological literature, not a conclusion drawn from any trial that actually randomised fortification in this subgroup. This must be stated plainly rather than allowed to read as an evidence-based recommendation: no adequately powered RCT of fortification timing has ever been conducted specifically in the AEDF/REDF or SGA-without-AEDF subgroup. The Kempley subgroup analysis additionally warns that a single timing rule derived from a mixed-gestation trial population does not necessarily transfer even to feed *initiation*, let alone fortification, in the most preterm growth-restricted infants (<29 weeks). Domain 2 and Domain 3 share this same structural gap — both rest on extrapolation from adjacent literatures rather than direct trial evidence.

## (e) UK level-3 NICU relevance

BAPM and unit-level guidance in the UK generally recommends caution — delayed feed initiation and a more gradual approach to fortification — in infants with antenatal AEDF/REDF, consistent with the ADEPT trial's own design (which itself tested only a 2-versus-6-day feed-initiation window, not a "wait for full feeds" strategy as commonly practised). Units should recognise that their local "wait and stabilise before fortifying" protocols for this subgroup are a reasonable, precaution-driven extrapolation from feed-initiation and epidemiological evidence, not a directly evidenced fortification-specific practice, and that an adequately powered RCT of fortification timing in this exact subgroup — identified explicitly in Section 6 as the single trial most likely to change practice here — does not yet exist.
